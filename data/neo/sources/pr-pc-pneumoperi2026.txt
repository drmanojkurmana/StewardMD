Neonatal pneumopericardium is a rare form of air leak syndrome associated with high morbidity and mortality. With advances in neonatal care, the incidence of pneumopericardium has decreased, as with other air leak syndromes; however, most cases occur in premature infants with a history of respiratory distress and mechanical ventilation. Pneumopericardium should be considered in the differential diagnosis of newborns experiencing sudden respiratory distress. Early diagnosis and appropriate treatment can significantly reduce mortality. This case report presents the management of isolated pneumopericardium using ultrasound-guided pericardiocentesis, which was detected after sudden clinical deterioration in a premature female infant born at 28 gestational weeks and followed up on mechanical ventilation. Recognition and prompt intervention by neonatal intensive care unit physicians significantly impact the patient’s prognosis.

Thoracic air leaks are defined as air accumulation in a space where it is not normally present after leakage from the tracheobronchial tree.
[
1
]
Different types include pneumothorax, pneumomediastinum, pneumopericardium, pulmonary interstitial emphysema, pneumoperitoneum, and subcutaneous emphysema. Air leaks occur more frequently in the neonatal period than at other times in life. Pneumopericardium occurs when air accumulates in the pericardial sac surrounding the heart and is one of the rarest types of air leaks in newborns.
[
2
]
It can occur in isolation or secondary to other air leak syndromes, such as pneumomediastinum, pneumothorax, pneumoperitoneum, subcutaneous emphysema, and interstitial emphysema.
[
3
]
If air rapidly accumulates in the limited pericardial space, reducing venous return and cardiac output, it can progress to cardiac tamponade and, without prompt intervention, cause significant morbidity and mortality.
In this case report, we aimed to present the successful treatment of a premature infant with isolated pneumopericardium who developed sudden hemodynamic deterioration while receiving mechanical ventilation support and was treated with ultrasound-guided pericardiocentesis. We also aimed to discuss current data and management strategies for neonatal pneumopericardium in light of the literature.
Case Report
At 28 1/7 gestational weeks, a female infant weighing 1115 g was delivered by emergency cesarean section due to maternal vaginal bleeding and premature contractions. The antenatal steroid course had been completed. The infant was born active and did not require resuscitation at birth, with Apgar scores of 7 and 8 at 1 and 5 minutes, respectively. During postnatal follow-up, the patient was intubated and admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit after failing to reach target saturations under nasal continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) support. Surfactant was administered due to respiratory distress syndrome (RDS), and an umbilical venous catheter was inserted. Due to prematurity and RDS, the patient was followed up under mechanical ventilation with synchronized intermittent positive pressure ventilation (SIPPV).
On the 7
th
day of life, intermittent desaturations and increased oxygen demand were detected, and blood gas analysis revealed respiratory acidosis. Auscultation revealed bilaterally equal lung sounds. A chest radiograph showed equal lung aeration with a minimal reticulogranular pattern. Anemia was not detected, and sepsis markers were negative in laboratory evaluations. Cranial ultrasonography revealed bilateral stage 1 intraventricular hemorrhage. Due to endotracheal tube secretions, the decision was made to reintubate the patient to rule out partial obstruction. Following reintubation and positive pressure ventilation, the patient suddenly deteriorated, and desaturation and bradycardia occurred; the heart rate was <60/min, and cardiopulmonary resuscitation was initiated. Peripheral circulation deteriorated, and on auscultation, S1 and S2 were muffled, while both lung fields were equally ventilated. A chest radiograph obtained immediately showed a radiolucent air band consistent with pneumopericardium, conforming to the cardiac silhouette and not extending beyond the great vessels. Furthermore, the distal tip of the endotracheal tube had advanced into the right main bronchus (
Fig. 1
).
Figure 1
Chest X-ray showing a halo sign – continous air around the cardiac silhouette extending beyond the level of great vessels. The distal end of the endotracheal tube was lying deep in carina near the right main bronchus.
Subxiphoid pericardiocentesis was planned under ultrasound guidance. Because this was an emergency, the family was informed by phone, and verbal consent was obtained. As routine clinical practice, informed consent forms are obtained from all patients’ legal guardians upon admission for such emergency interventions. After aseptic skin preparation, a 21-gauge needle attached to a 10-mL syringe was carefully inserted into the pericardial space. Air bubbles were observed, and when 4 mL of air was aspirated, the patient’s heart rate increased to >100 bpm and SpO
2
reached 100%. The patient was placed on a ventilator at lower pressures and stabilized with supportive care. A follow-up chest radiograph showed regression of the pneumopericardium (
Fig. 2
). Follow-up blood gas analysis showed resolution of respiratory acidosis. Postprocedural echocardiography revealed normal ventricular function and minimal pericardial air. The patient recovered completely and was extubated on postpartum day 11. After 55 days of intensive care follow-up, the patient was discharged with full clinical recovery. She continues to be in good general condition with regular outpatient follow-up. Permission was obtained from the patient’s legal guardian to share these data as a case report.
Figure 2
Chest X-ray taken immediately after pericardiocentesis shows a significant decrease in the air around the cardiac silhouette.
Discussion
Pneumopericardium, one of the severe air leak syndromes, was first described by Bricheteau in 1844.
[
4
]
The incidence of air leak syndromes increased significantly between 1960 and 1990, largely due to the aggressive ventilation strategies used in the treatment of RDS in the era before surfactant use.
[
5
]
Over time, various improvements in neonatal care, such as the introduction of gentler ventilation methods, commercial surfactants, and antenatal steroid administration, have led to a decrease in cases of pneumopericardium.
[
6
]
Nevertheless, preterm infants who are mechanically ventilated and have RDS are still particularly affected. Studies show that the incidence of pneumopericardium is inversely related to birth weight and gestational age, and the condition is still associated with considerable mortality and morbidity.
[
1
,
7
]
In addition to prematurity and low birth weight, other risk factors include RDS, the use of advanced ventilation techniques, CPAP, meconium aspiration syndrome, congenital heart surgery, intensive neonatal resuscitation, and inappropriate placement of an endotracheal tube.
[
8
,
9
]
In a comprehensive case series by Burt and Lester, pneumopericardium was found in 50 of 3,841 newborns observed.
[
8
]
Of these cases, 66% also had pneumomediastinum, while only three cases were reported as isolated pneumopericardium, similar to the case described here. In a separate retrospective study, the incidence of pneumopericardium was found to be 2% in 2389 very low birth weight infants and 3.5% in 1349 infants who were mechanically ventilated. Other associated leaks were pneumothorax (49%), pulmonary interstitial emphysema (46%), pneumomediastinum (39%), subcutaneous emphysema (17%), pneumoperitoneum (10%), and systemic vascular air embolism (5%).
[
10
]
Pneumopericardium may develop in infants receiving both conventional and high-frequency ventilation and has been reported as a complication associated with increased peak inspiratory and end-expiratory pressures.
[
11
,
12
]
In air leak syndromes, the underlying pathophysiology is the rupture of overinflated alveoli, allowing air to escape into the interstitial tissue, from where it tracks along the connective tissue sheaths toward the hilum. This escaped air can rupture the pleura, leading to pneumothorax; accumulate in the mediastinum, forming pneumomediastinum; or enter the pericardial space, causing pneumopericardium. Although the exact route by which air enters the pericardial cavity remains controversial, histologic studies have revealed areas without collagenous tissue in the pericardial reflections near the openings of the pulmonary veins. Air reaching the perivascular sheaths or mediastinum may enter the pericardium through these structurally weaker regions where the pericardium folds over the pulmonary veins.
[
11
,
13
]
In some cases, a neonate’s first few breaths result in an abrupt increase in intrathoracic pressure, leading to small air leaks that may subsequently be exacerbated by positive pressure ventilation.
[
14
]
The pericardium consists of two layers: an inner visceral layer that tightly surrounds the surface of the heart and an outer parietal layer that is fibrous and less elastic. Gas or fluid can accumulate in the potential space between these layers, leading to compression of the heart and the development of cardiac tamponade. Such compression increases diastolic ventricular pressure, restricts cardiac filling, and leads to a rapid decrease in stroke volume.
[
15
]
In neonates, even small amounts of air filling the pericardial space can lead to rapid onset of hemodynamic instability due to decreased cardiac contractility caused by tamponade. Literature reports indicate that the volume of air aspirated during pericardiocentesis in neonatal tamponade ranges from 2.9 to 14 mL/kg.
[
6
,
16
]
In our patient, stability was restored after the release of approximately 4 mL of air, which is consistent with previously reported data.
The clinical spectrum of pneumopericardium ranges from the absence of symptoms to life-threatening cardiac tamponade, depending on the amount and rate at which free air accumulates in the pericardial cavity.
[
17
]
The sudden onset of hemodynamic instability, which may include bradycardia, dyspnea, cyanosis, acidosis, and hypotension, is typical.
[
18
]
Physical signs can include muffled or diminished heart sounds, prolonged capillary refill, abrupt development of cutis marmorata, poor peripheral perfusion, and worsening respiratory distress. In their analysis of 50 neonatal cases, Burt and Lester identified bradycardia and cyanosis as the most common symptoms.
[
8
]
On electrocardiography, low-voltage waveforms with reduced QRS amplitudes can be observed.
[
1
]
In many cases, cardiopulmonary deterioration does not improve with supplemental oxygen, increased ventilation, inotropic therapy, or even cardiopulmonary resuscitation.
[
19
]
Our patient also did not respond to resuscitation measures, but rapid identification of pneumopericardium on radiography and immediate intervention led to stabilization. In this case, the probable cause was deep advancement of the endotracheal tube into the right bronchus, resulting in volutrauma from positive pressure ventilation. A similar case, also due to deep placement of the endotracheal tube, has been documented in the literature in a term infant.
[
9
]
Definitive diagnosis is usually confirmed by chest radiography. Air surrounding the heart, including the inferior surface of the heart, can be seen.
[
18
]
Burt and Lester referred to this as the “halo sign” and found that it was present in 96% of 50 cases of pneumopericardium; however, it was not always observed when pneumopericardium was first diagnosed.
[
8
]
The main findings that differentiate pneumopericardium from other air leaks, such as pneumomediastinum and pneumothorax, are that the air is seen in the pericardium below the heart and does not protrude above the great vessels, as in pneumomediastinum. Echocardiography, together with the detection of air bubbles in the pericardial space, can be helpful in the diagnosis of pneumopericardium.
In hemodynamically stable patients, the preferred treatment for pneumopericardium is conservative and consists of careful monitoring and lowering ventilatory pressure. However, monitoring should be performed in a facility where pericardiocentesis can be performed if necessary.
[
20
,
21
]
This ensures that emergency pericardiocentesis can be performed without delay in the event of acute cardiac tamponade.
Symptomatic pneumopericardium can quickly become life-threatening and require urgent drainage by direct pericardiocentesis via the subxiphoid approach.
[
7
,
18
]
In this technique, a 22G peripheral vascular cannula is inserted below the xiphoid process of the neonate and guided toward the left shoulder, with aspiration occurring during catheter advancement. With the increasing use of point-of-care ultrasound, ultrasound guidance is now recommended for the procedure.
[
22
,
23
]
In cases where pneumopericardium persists after the procedure, the catheter can be left in place to allow continuous pericardial drainage.
[
22
]
Previous reports have described supplemental oxygen therapy as “nitrogen washout” for the treatment of pneumopericardium and other neonatal air leak syndromes.
[
24
,
25
]
However, this strategy is not appropriate for infants born before 32 weeks of gestation due to the increased risk of complications from oxidative stress.
[
26
]
Furthermore, one study found no evidence that supplemental oxygen or nitrogen washout accelerated the resolution of spontaneous pneumothorax.
[
27
]
Before the introduction of surfactant therapy, pneumopericardium had a very poor prognosis in premature infants.
[
28
]
The mortality rate was particularly high when cardiac tamponade developed as a complication.
[
18
]
This was partly due to the severity of the underlying conditions that predisposed preterm infants to pneumopericardium.
[
29
]
The review by Burt and Lester reported an overall mortality rate of 72%.
[
8
]
However, with the advent and widespread adoption of surfactant therapy, outcomes have improved considerably, and timely diagnosis combined with prompt intervention can help reduce both morbidity and mortality.
[
2
,
29
]
Conclusion
In conclusion, pneumopericardium in neonates remains a rare but potentially life-threatening condition. Early detection and appropriate treatment can significantly reduce the risk of death. It should be included in the differential diagnosis of neonates presenting with sudden respiratory distress. This case demonstrates that full recovery is possible with prompt diagnosis and treatment and underscores the critical role of neonatal intensive care physicians in ensuring a favorable outcome.
