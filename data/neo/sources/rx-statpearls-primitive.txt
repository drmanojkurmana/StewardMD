Continuing Education Activity

Primitive reflexes are essential brainstem-mediated motor responses that support neonatal survival, feeding, protection, and early sensorimotor development. Although these reflexes are expected to integrate during infancy as cortical control matures, their persistence in children or reemergence in adults may indicate significant neurological dysfunction, including cerebral palsy, autism spectrum disorder, dementia, and other neurodegenerative conditions. This course reviews the assessment of primitive reflexes and the valuable insights they provide into neurodevelopmental status, neurological integrity, and disease progression across a patient's lifespan. Participants will gain an understanding of the neuroanatomy, developmental timelines, clinical assessment techniques, and diagnostic significance of primitive reflexes, as well as normal and abnormal reflex patterns, standardized assessment tools, high-risk pediatric and adult populations, and early intervention strategies. This activity for healthcare professionals is designed to enhance learners' competence in identifying primitive reflexes, applying neurological assessment across the lifespan, and implementing an appropriate interprofessional approach to the early identification of neurologic disease.

Objectives:

Identify normal developmental timelines of primitive reflexes associated with neuroanatomical pathways across the lifespan.

Differentiate between normal developmental patterns and pathological features of primitive reflexes to identify patients at risk for neurological dysfunction.

Implement evidence-based management strategies that reduce complications associated with abnormal primitive reflexes.

Collaborate with interprofessional team members to improve early identification of patients demonstrating abnormal primitive reflex patterns across a lifespan.

Introduction

Primitive reflexes are involuntary, brainstem-mediated motor responses present at birth that facilitate neonatal survival and early development. These reflexes emerge during gestation and are fully established in term newborns, supporting critical functions in feeding, protection, and sensorimotor integration. The central nervous system gradually inhibits these reflexes between 4 and 6 months of age as the cortex matures and voluntary motor control develops.
[1]
However, primitive reflexes may persist beyond expected developmental milestones or reappear in adulthood, signaling neurological dysfunction.

The following primitive reflexes can be categorized by function:

Oral-feeding reflexes (rooting, sucking): facilitate neonatal nutrition

Protective reflexes (Moro, asymmetric tonic neck reflex): provide defensive responses

Grasp reflexes (palmar, plantar): promote attachment behaviors

Lower extremity reflexes (Babinski, stepping, Galant): support early motor development
[1]
[2]

In adults, the reemergence of primitive reflexes, termed frontal release signs, indicates frontal lobe pathology or diffuse cerebral dysfunction associated with dementia and neurodegenerative diseases.
[3]
[4]
[5]
Understanding the neuroanatomical pathways, developmental timelines, and clinical significance of each primitive reflex enables clinicians to utilize reflex assessment as a sensitive screening tool for neurological integrity across the lifespan.
[6]
[1]

Clinical Overview

Standardized Assessment Tools

Primitive reflex assessment has historically lacked standardization, resulting in inconsistent documentation and interpretation. No single universally accepted instrument exists exclusively for primitive reflex assessment. Instead, several validated neurologic and developmental tools incorporate evaluation of primitive reflexes as part of a broader neurodevelopmental assessment. Selection of an instrument depends on patient age and clinical purpose, including screening for cerebral palsy, assessment of neuromotor maturation, or comprehensive neurologic evaluation.

Among infants, commonly used instruments include the Capute Primitive Reflex Profile, which grades 7 primitive reflexes and helps distinguish functional severity in children with cerebral palsy.
[7]
The Hammersmith Infant Neurological Examination is a standardized neurologic assessment for infants aged 2 to 24 months and is recognized by the American Academy of Pediatrics for early detection of cerebral palsy; a Brief-HINE screening version has also demonstrated high sensitivity and specificity.
[8]
Additional validated infant assessments include the Infant Neurological International Battery, the Standardized Infant NeuroDevelopmental Assessment, and the Zafeiriou Primitive Reflex Profile.
[9]
[10]

For preschool- and school-age children, the Sally Goddard-Blythe battery is commonly used to assess retained primitive reflexes and their association with motor, sensory, and academic difficulties.
[11]
[12]
[13]
Combined assessment of primitive reflexes and postural reactions may improve early identification of infants at risk for cerebral palsy and developmental delay. Prognostic significance varies by age; absent reflexes in early infancy and persistent reflexes later in development are associated with adverse neurologic outcomes.

The Children's Primitive Reflex Integration Assessment Scale, developed from a large study of school-age children in China and published in 2025, has been proposed as a structured tool for identifying retained primitive reflexes. This tool, designed for children aged 6 to 9, measures 7 reflex dimensions (Moro reflex, asymmetric tonic neck reflex [ATNR], symmetrical tonic neck reflex [STNR], tonic labyrinthine reflex [TLR], spinal Galant reflex [SGR], spinal Perez reflex [SPR], and Landau reflex) to evaluate motor dysfunction.
[6]
Although some institutions have begun incorporating the scale into clinical and research settings, the Children's Primitive Reflex Integration Assessment Scale has not yet achieved broad validation or formal endorsement by major pediatric or neurologic organizations as a standardized screening instrument.

Assessment technique significantly impacts reliability. Each reflex requires specific patient positioning, stimulus application, and observation of the response.
[1]
For example, the Moro reflex requires sudden head extension while supporting the infant's body, whereas the Babinski reflex requires lateral plantar stimulation from the heel to the toes.
[14]
[15]
Improper technique can yield false-negative or false-positive results, underscoring the need for standardized training across disciplines.
[6]
Combined assessment of primitive reflexes and postural reactions may improve early identification of infants at risk for cerebral palsy and developmental delay. Prognostic significance varies by age; absent reflexes in early infancy and persistent reflexes later in development are associated with adverse neurologic outcomes.

Key Clinical Considerations

High-Risk Populations

High-risk newborns often exhibit abnormal primitive reflexes. Studies of premature infants and those with low birth weight, perinatal asphyxia, or maternal substance exposure demonstrate reduced rates of normal primitive reflex responses, with normal Moro, Babinski, and sucking reflexes observed in only 42.9%, 58.7%, and 63.5% of affected infants, respectively.
[16]
These high-risk populations require systematic assessment of primitive reflexes as part of comprehensive neurodevelopmental surveillance.
[17]
For example, infants with prenatal opioid exposure frequently demonstrate hyperactive primitive reflexes associated with neonatal opioid withdrawal syndrome, including exaggerated Moro responses, hyperactive rooting, and tremulous movements.
[16]
[2]
Recognition of these patterns facilitates early diagnosis and appropriate neurologic and developmental management.

Neurological Implications Across the Lifespan

Persistence of primitive reflexes beyond 4 to 6 months strongly correlates with neurodevelopmental conditions, particularly cerebral palsy and autism spectrum disorders.
[18]
[19]
[20]
[10]
The presence of 5 or more abnormal primitive reflexes has high predictive value for the diagnosis of cerebral palsy, with specific reflex patterns helping to differentiate spastic, dyskinetic, and ataxic subtypes.
[19]
[10]

In adults, the reemergence of primitive reflexes indicates frontal lobe pathology or diffuse cerebral dysfunction. Dementia patients have a 13.94- to 16.38-fold higher risk of exhibiting primitive reflexes than age-matched controls.
[3]
Individuals with frontotemporal dementia exhibit primitive oral and grasp reflexes more often than those with Alzheimer disease, and reflex patterns correlate with neuroimaging findings of frontal lobe atrophy.
[4]

Clinical Significance

Oral-Feeding Reflexes

Oral-feeding reflexes are critical primitive reflexes that support neonatal survival by enabling infants to locate, initiate, and safely coordinate feeding. The rooting, sucking, and swallowing reflexes emerge during fetal development and rely on complex brainstem and cranial nerve pathways to facilitate effective nutrition while protecting the airway. Assessment of these reflexes provides important information about neurological maturity and feeding readiness, whereas abnormal, persistent, or reemergent reflex patterns may signal underlying neurodevelopmental or neurological disorders requiring further evaluation.

Rooting reflex

The rooting reflex is one of the most essential primitive reflexes for neonatal survival, directly facilitating the infant's ability to locate and initiate feeding. This reflex is elicited when the corner of an infant's mouth is stroked or touched, triggering a predictable sequence: the newborn turns the head toward the side of stimulation, opens the mouth, and extends the tongue in a thrusting motion (see
Image
. Rooting Reflex). Present from approximately 28 weeks of gestation, the rooting reflex begins in utero and reaches full maturity by term delivery, ensuring the newborn can immediately seek the breast or bottle after birth.
[21]

The rooting reflex operates through the trigeminal cranial nerve (V), which mediates facial sensation. When perioral cutaneous receptors detect tactile stimulation at the corner of the mouth or on the cheek, sensory signals travel via the ophthalmic, maxillary, and mandibular divisions of cranial nerve V to the brainstem, where integration occurs in the pons and medulla.
[21]
[22]
The motor response, head turning toward the stimulus and mouth opening, involves coordination of multiple cranial nerves: cranial nerve V for jaw opening via the trigeminal motor nucleus, cranial nerve VII for facial muscle movement, and cranial nerve XII for tongue protrusion.

This reflex functions as a searching mechanism, allowing the infant to orient toward the food source even before visual acuity is sufficient to guide feeding behaviors. Testing should occur when the infant is in a quiet alert state, as the reflex may be difficult to elicit during deep sleep or active crying. The response is graded as: strong (immediate, vigorous head turning with wide mouth opening and prominent tongue protrusion), moderate (head turning present but less vigorous, with adequate mouth opening), weak (minimal or delayed head turning, incomplete mouth opening), or absent (no response to repeated stimulation).
[16]
[21]
Asymmetry should be assessed and documented by testing each side independently.

Differentiation from the sucking reflex

Differentiating the rooting reflex from the sucking reflex is critical, though both work synergistically during feeding.
[21]
While the rooting reflex initiates when the corner of the mouth is stimulated and produces head turning and mouth opening, the sucking reflex activates when the hard palate or posterior tongue is stimulated and generates rhythmic sucking movements. The sucking reflex appears at 32 to 35 weeks of gestation and coordinates breathing with swallowing, a complex integration that matures around 37 weeks of gestation.
[16]
During swallowing, epiglottic closure temporarily interrupts breathing to prevent aspiration, demonstrating sophisticated neural coordination underlying oral feeding.

Developmental timeline of the rooting reflex

The rooting reflex typically disappears by 4 to 6 months of age as the frontal lobe matures and voluntary motor control supersedes primitive reflexes.
[1]
Individual variation exists: some infants show weaker responses as early as 3 months, while others maintain moderate rooting until 5 to 6 months without pathology. The reflex often becomes less prominent during sleep after 3 months and may be more easily elicited when the infant is hungry. Gradual suppression of rooting facilitates the development of voluntary oral-motor skills necessary for solid food intake.

Abnormal rooting reflex presentations

Persistence of rooting beyond 4 to 6 months, particularly when accompanied by other retained reflexes, strongly suggests congenital cerebral palsy or a significant neurodevelopmental disorder.
[18]
[19]
Clinical presentation varies. Mild persistence may manifest as subtle head turning toward perioral touch during bath time or face washing. Moderate persistence involves obvious head turning during attempted spoon feeding, with the infant repeatedly turning away from the midline.

Severe persistence presents with vigorous, obligatory head turning responses that disrupt feeding attempts, often accompanied by a strong tongue thrust that forcefully expels nonliquid food. When rooting fails to integrate appropriately, affected infants exhibit characteristic oral-motor dysfunction. Persistent drooling represents a hallmark finding, as reflexive tongue thrust prevents the infant from developing the posterior tongue retraction needed to collect and swallow saliva. The tongue's anterior position, often protruding between the lips even at rest, prevents the lip seal necessary to contain saliva.
[21]

Parents report constant wetness requiring multiple daily bib changes, perioral skin irritation, and occasional secondary
Candida
infections. A hyperactive rooting reflex present from birth may indicate prenatal substance exposure, particularly to opiates.
[2]
Affected neonates exhibit exaggerated responses to minimal perioral stimulation, with vigorous, sometimes frantic, head turning and mouth opening that interfere with successful breastfeeding latching, typically occurring alongside tremors, irritability, and other withdrawal signs.
[16]

Weak or inconsistent rooting in newborns raises concerns about neurological maturity or brainstem dysfunction. Preterm infants often demonstrate weak rooting proportional to their degree of prematurity, with responses strengthening as they approach term-equivalent age. However, weak rooting in full-term infants suggests possible hypoxic-ischemic injury, intraventricular hemorrhage, or congenital structural brain abnormalities.
[23]

The complete absence of rooting in neonates requires immediate investigation.
[22]
Because cranial nerve V mediates the sensory component of this reflex, congenital trigeminal anesthesia results in the complete absence of rooting and loss of facial sensation. The most serious complication involves the corneal reflex, which shares cranial nerve V as its afferent pathway (with cranial nerve VII providing efferent innervation for the blink). Infants with trigeminal anesthesia cannot blink protectively in response to corneal irritation, leading to recurrent corneal injuries, exposure keratitis, ulceration, and potential permanent vision loss. The triad of absent rooting, absent corneal reflex, and reduced tearing mandates immediate ophthalmologic intervention with aggressive corneal lubrication and protective measures.
[21]

Adult reappearance of the rooting reflex

In adults, reemergence of the rooting reflex indicates frontal lobe pathology or diffuse cerebral dysfunction.
[3]
[4]
Dementia patients, particularly those with frontotemporal dementia, demonstrate primitive oral reflexes, including rooting more frequently than age-matched controls. Light touch or stroking of the perioral region triggers reflexive head turning toward the stimulus, mouth opening, and sometimes tongue protrusion, responses that disrupt voluntary feeding behaviors.

Reappearing rooting and other primitive oral reflexes place older adult patients at substantially increased risk for malnutrition and aspiration pneumonia.
[24]
Nursing home residents demonstrating rooting and other oral primitive reflexes are at significantly increased risk of malnutrition, as reflexive responses interfere with voluntary eating behaviors and efficient oral intake.
[24]

Sucking and swallowing reflexes

The sucking reflex is activated when the hard palate or the posterior tongue is stimulated, generating rhythmic sucking movements essential for nutritive feeding (see
Image
. Sucking Reflex). This reflex appears at 32 to 35 weeks of gestation and matures through 37 weeks, when coordination of sucking, swallowing, and breathing becomes established.
[16]
Assessment involves placing a gloved finger or pacifier in the infant's mouth and observing the quality, rhythm, and strength of sucking movements.
[16]

Figure 2

(CC BY 2.0)

The swallow reflex coordinates with sucking to transport milk from the oral cavity through the pharynx and esophagus while protecting the airway.
[21]
During swallowing, the soft palate elevates to close the nasopharynx, the larynx elevates, the epiglottis tilts posteriorly to cover the tracheal opening, and the upper esophageal sphincter relaxes to allow bolus passage. This complex sequence requires integration of multiple cranial nerves (V, VII, IX, X, XII) and brainstem nuclei.
[21]

Weak or absent sucking in term newborns suggests neurological immaturity, brainstem dysfunction, or cranial nerve pathology.
[23]
Discoordinated sucking and swallowing increase the risk of aspiration and necessitate modified feeding strategies.
[18]
Persistence of primitive, uncoordinated sucking patterns beyond 6 months interferes with the development of mature oral-motor skills required to manage solid foods.
[21]

Protective Reflexes

Protective reflexes are primitive motor responses that help infants react to environmental threats, maintain postural awareness, and support early sensorimotor development. The Moro reflex and ATNR emerge during late gestation and contribute to protective behaviors, sensory integration, body awareness, and the development of coordinated movement patterns. Careful assessment of these reflexes provides valuable information about neurological integrity, while absent, asymmetric, persistent, or exaggerated responses may indicate underlying neuromuscular, developmental, or central nervous system disorders requiring further evaluation and intervention.

Moro reflex

The Moro reflex, also known as the startle reflex, is a protective response to sudden disruption of balance or to the perception of falling (see
Image
. Moro Reflex).
[14]
[2]
The Moro reflex appears by 28 weeks of gestation and persists until approximately 4 to 6 months of age.
[1]
This reflex serves a dual purpose: alerting caregivers to potential danger through visible distress and preparing the infant for protective responses. The Moro reflex is elicited by suddenly extending the infant's head backward (approximately 30 degrees) while supporting the body, creating a sensation of falling. The examiner can also produce the response by making a loud noise near the infant or sharply striking the examination table surface near the infant's head. The complete Moro response occurs in the following 2 distinct phases:

. Accessed June 11, 2026
(CC BY 2.0)

Phase 1
(extension/abduction): The infant suddenly extends and abducts both arms symmetrically at the shoulders, opens the hands with finger extension, and often extends the legs. The mouth typically opens, and the infant may cry or appear startled.

Phase 2
(flexion/adduction): Within 1 to 2 seconds, the arms flex and adduct across the chest in an embracing motion, hands close into fists, and legs flex. This second phase represents the "embrace" component of the reflex.
[14]

Clinicians should document symmetry (bilateral equal response), completeness (both phases present), and vigor (strong, moderate, weak, or absent).
[16]
An asymmetric Moro reflex suggests unilateral pathology, eg, a brachial plexus injury (Erb palsy, affecting the C5-C6 roots, prevents arm abduction on the affected side), a clavicle fracture (pain inhibits movement on the affected side), or hemiparesis from a cerebral injury.
[14]

Abnormal Moro reflex presentations

Persistence of the Moro reflex beyond 6 months is abnormal and associated with neurodevelopmental disorders.
[18]
[19]
Children with a retained Moro reflex often demonstrate hypervigilance, anxiety, sensory processing difficulties, and poor impulse control.
[20]
The persistent startle response interferes with normal daily activities, as unexpected sounds, movements, or visual stimuli trigger the reflex, disrupting attention and emotional regulation.

An absent or weak Moro reflex in neonates raises serious concerns. Complete absence may indicate severe central nervous system depression from birth asphyxia, intracranial hemorrhage, or congenital neurological abnormalities. Generalized hypotonia associated with an absent Moro reflex suggests conditions, eg, spinal muscular atrophy, congenital myopathies, or chromosomal disorders.
[2]
An exaggerated or hyperactive Moro reflex occurs in infants with neonatal opioid withdrawal syndrome, hypoglycemia, hypocalcemia, or intracranial pathology causing increased intracranial pressure. These infants show a low threshold for reflex elicitation, with prolonged or repetitive responses to minimal stimulation.
[16]

Asymmetric tonic neck reflex

The ATNR, also termed the "fencing position," appears around 35 weeks of gestation and typically integrates by 3 to 4 months of age.
[1]
This reflex is elicited by gently rotating the infant's head to 1 side while the infant is supine and calm, and holding the position for 10 to 15 seconds. The reflex should be tested with head rotation to both sides. A positive ATNR demonstrates extension of the arm and leg on the side toward which the face turns (the "face" side) and flexion of the contralateral arm and leg, mimicking a fencer's stance.
[1]
[2]

The ATNR facilitates the development of hand-eye coordination by bringing the hand into the infant's visual field as the head turns. It also promotes sensory awareness of body laterality and may support the development of cross-lateral movement patterns necessary for crawling. The response may be obligatory (limbs assume the position and remain fixed) or facultative (limbs show the tendency but can be overcome).

Abnormal asymmetric tonic neck reflex presentations

An obligatory ATNR persisting beyond 4 to 6 months prevents the infant from bringing both hands to the midline (a critical milestone for bimanual play), reaching across the midline, and eventually rolling, sitting, and crawling.
[19]
[2]
Infants with a persistent obligatory ATNR cannot maintain a symmetric posture and experience difficulty with the following:

Bringing hands together at the midline to explore objects

Reaching across the body to grasp objects on the opposite side

Rolling from supine to prone (the extended arm prevents rolling)

Developing symmetric sitting balance

Coordinating reciprocal crawling movements

A persistent ATNR strongly associates with cerebral palsy, particularly spastic quadriplegia and hemiplegia.
[10]
In spastic cerebral palsy, the ATNR reflex often remains obligatory well beyond infancy, contributing to characteristic asymmetric posturing and movement patterns. Children with autism spectrum disorders also demonstrate higher rates of persistent ATNR, which may contribute to the motor coordination difficulties and sensory processing challenges observed in this population.
[20]

An absent ATNR in neonates raises concerns about neurological integrity, though its absence is less alarming than the absence of other protective reflexes, eg, the Moro. However, a complete absence of the ATNR, especially when combined with other absent or abnormal reflexes, suggests diffuse neurological dysfunction.
[2]

Grasp Reflexes

Grasp reflexes are primitive motor responses that support early interaction with the environment and contribute to the development of voluntary hand and foot function. The palmar and plantar grasp reflexes emerge during fetal development and reflect the integrity of sensory, motor, and cortical pathways involved in neuromotor maturation. Assessment of these reflexes provides valuable information about neurological development, while persistent, asymmetric, absent, or reemergent grasp responses may indicate cerebral palsy, peripheral nerve injury, frontal lobe dysfunction, dementia, or other neurological disorders requiring further evaluation.

Palmar grasp reflex

The palmar grasp reflex appears by 28 weeks of gestation and typically disappears by 4 to 6 months, coinciding with the development of voluntary reaching and grasping. This reflex is elicited by applying gentle pressure to the infant's palm with the examiner's finger, moving from the ulnar side of the hand toward the radial side (see
Image
. Palmar Reflex).
[5]
[2]
The response consists of flexion of all fingers around the stimulus, creating a grip strong enough for the infant to briefly support their own weight when both hands grasp simultaneously (though this should not be tested due to obvious safety concerns). The neural pathway involves cutaneous mechanoreceptors in the palm transmitting signals via the median and ulnar nerves to spinal cord segments C8-T1.
[5]

The motor response involves the flexor muscles of the fingers, which are innervated by the same nerves. Cortical maturation, particularly of the frontal lobes, gradually inhibits this subcortical reflex pathway. Assessment should document the strength of the response (strong, moderate, weak, absent), symmetry between hands, and release capability. Normal infants demonstrate a symmetric, strong grasp that releases when the dorsum of the hand is stroked or when the infant's attention shifts. Testing should occur when the infant is calm and alert, as crying or sleeping states may diminish the response.
[16]

Abnormal Palmar grasp reflex presentations

Persistence of the palmar grasp beyond 6 months prevents the development of voluntary hand release, which is a prerequisite for manipulative play, feeding skills, and eventually writing.
[5]
Infants with a retained palmar grasp cannot voluntarily open their hands to drop objects, transfer toys between hands, or use a pincer grasp for small-object manipulation. This retention is characteristic of cerebral palsy, particularly the spastic hemiplegic and quadriplegic types.
[18]
[19]

In adults, the grasp reflex can reappear as a frontal release sign associated with frontal lobe lesions, diffuse cerebral atrophy, and dementia.
[3]
The adult grasp reflex is elicited by stroking the patient's palm, triggering involuntary finger flexion and grasping. Unlike the infant reflex, which is a normal developmental phenomenon, the adult grasp reflex indicates pathological loss of cortical inhibition.
[5]
The reflex may be further classified as follows:

Simple grasp
: Fingers flex around the stimulus

Groping reflex
: Fingers pursue the stimulus when it moves away

Traction response
: The grasp tightens when traction is applied

The groping and traction components suggest more severe frontal lobe dysfunction. Studies correlate the presence of the grasp reflex in dementia with hypometabolism in the bilateral superior frontal gyri, putamen, and thalamus, reflecting dysfunction in the corticostriatal motor circuit.
[4]
An asymmetric palmar grasp in newborns suggests unilateral pathology, including brachial plexus injury (particularly Klumpke palsy affecting C8-T1), peripheral nerve injury, or a contralateral cerebral lesion causing hemiparesis.
[14]

Plantar grasp reflex

The plantar grasp reflex is elicited by applying pressure to the sole of the foot just proximal to the toes, causing the toes to flex around the stimulus. This reflex appears by 28 weeks of gestation and typically disappears between 9 and 12 months, later than the palmar grasp reflex.
[2]
Persistence beyond 12 months may interfere with the development of standing and walking because the toes reflexively flex rather than maintain the extension needed for a stable foot position during weight-bearing.
[1]

Lower Extremity Reflexes

Lower extremity reflexes are primitive motor responses that reflect the maturation and integrity of spinal, brainstem, and corticospinal pathways involved in posture, locomotion, and sensorimotor development. Assessment of reflexes, eg, the Babinski, stepping, and Galant reflexes, provides valuable information about neurological development and motor pathway function, while abnormal persistence, absence, asymmetry, or reemergence may indicate underlying neuromuscular or central nervous system pathology.

Babinski reflex (plantar reflex)

The Babinski reflex, formally termed the plantar reflex in infants, is one of the most clinically significant primitive reflexes because of its different implications in pediatric and adult populations.
[1]
This reflex is elicited by firmly stroking the plantar surface along the lateral aspect, starting at the heel and moving toward the toes in a smooth, continuous motion. In infants, the normal response is dorsiflexion of the great toe with fanning (abduction) of the other toes (see
Image
. Babinski Reflex). This response reflects the physiological immaturity of the corticospinal tract, which has not yet completed myelination. The reflex is present at birth and typically disappears between 12 and 24 months of age as corticospinal tract myelination progresses and cortical inhibition develops.
[15]

In infants, the normal response is dorsiflexion of the great toe with fanning (abduction) of the other toes. Ben Pansky, Public Domain, via Wikimedia Commons

Assessment technique significantly affects results. The stimulus should be firm enough to elicit a response but not painful. The examiner uses a blunt object (a reflex hammer handle, key, or thumbnail) to stroke along the lateral border of the foot. The movement should be smooth and deliberate, taking approximately 2 to 3 seconds to complete the heel-to-toe stroke. Testing should occur with the infant or child relaxed, as voluntary flexion or withdrawal responses can obscure the true reflex.
[15]
Documentation should include: the direction of great toe movement (dorsiflexion vs. plantarflexion), the presence and pattern of toe fanning, symmetry between feet, and the patient's age for developmental context.
[15]

Clinical significance of the Babinski reflex across the lifespan

In infants and young children up to 24 months of age, the Babinski reflex is normal, reflecting ongoing CNS maturation.
[15]
Asymmetry or the absence of the expected response may indicate neurological pathology. The critical transition occurs when myelination of the corticospinal tract is complete. After 24 months, the normal response to plantar stimulation becomes plantar flexion (downward curling) of all toes, termed the plantar response. Persistence of the Babinski sign beyond 24 months indicates upper motor neuron pathology affecting the corticospinal tract.
[15]

In adults, the presence of the Babinski sign is always pathological, most often indicating 1 of the following conditions:

Stroke, affecting the motor cortex or the internal capsule

Spinal cord lesions interrupting corticospinal tract pathways

Multiple sclerosis with demyelinating plaques

Brain tumors compressing motor pathways

Progressive neurodegenerative diseases

Metabolic encephalopathies

Severe meningitis or encephalitis
[15]

The Babinski sign localizes pathology to the corticospinal tract but does not identify the exact lesion level. Associated examination findings may aid localization; for example, leg weakness may suggest a cerebral lesion, whereas a distinct sensory level is more consistent with spinal cord pathology.
[15]

Abnormal Babinski reflex presentations in infants

The absence of Babinski reflexes in newborns, particularly bilaterally, raises concerns about severe neurological dysfunction affecting sensory or motor pathways or the spinal cord.
[2]
[23]
Premature infants may show weak or inconsistent Babinski responses due to CNS immaturity, which should strengthen as gestational age advances. A persistent, strong Babinski reflex beyond 24 months, especially when accompanied by increased lower-extremity tone, ankle clonus, and delayed walking, strongly suggests spastic cerebral palsy. The combination of a retained Babinski reflex with other persistent primitive reflexes has high predictive value for a diagnosis of cerebral palsy.
[10]

Stepping reflex

The stepping reflex, also known as the walking or dance reflex, appears at birth and typically disappears by 2 to 3 months of age. This reflex is elicited by holding the infant upright under the arms while the feet touch a flat surface. When the examiner tilts the infant forward slightly, the infant makes alternating stepping movements as if walking.
[2]
[1]
The reflex demonstrates coordination of hip flexion, knee flexion, and ankle dorsiflexion on 1 side, with simultaneous extension on the opposite side, the basic pattern of reciprocal gait.

The stepping reflex does not represent true ambulation but rather a subcortical motor pattern that disappears as cortical control develops. Interestingly, the reflex reappears months later, as intentional walking begins at 10 to 15 months, reflecting cortical maturation and voluntary motor control. Assessment requires the infant to be in a quiet, alert state. The examiner supports the infant in an upright position, ensures the infant's feet are in contact with the examination surface, and observes for stepping movements. Documentation should include presence/absence, quality of the stepping pattern, symmetry between legs, and any associated movements (arm swinging, trunk rotation).
[6]

Abnormal stepping reflex presentations

An absent stepping reflex in newborns may indicate lower motor neuron pathology, severe hypotonia, or spinal cord injury. However, the stepping reflex shows high variability even in healthy neonates and is considered less reliable than other primitive reflexes for neurological assessment. Asymmetric stepping, in which 1 leg demonstrates normal movement while the other remains flaccid or spastic, suggests unilateral pathology, eg, hip dislocation, femoral fracture, brachial plexus injury affecting the lower roots (though this primarily affects the upper extremity), or early hemiparetic cerebral palsy.
[18]
[2]

Galant reflex (truncal incurvation)

The Galant reflex appears by 32 weeks of gestation and typically disappears by 4 to 6 months. This reflex is elicited by stroking the infant's back approximately 1 cm lateral to the spine from the shoulder to the buttocks while the infant is held in ventral suspension. The normal response consists of lateral flexion of the trunk toward the stimulated side, with the pelvis swinging upward on that side.
[2]

The Galant reflex may facilitate fetal positioning during delivery by causing lateral flexion in response to uterine wall pressure. Persistence beyond infancy is associated with enuresis, fidgeting while seated (clothing touching the back triggers the reflex), poor concentration, and difficulty with fine motor tasks.
[6]
[13]
An absent Galant reflex may indicate spinal cord pathology or peripheral nerve dysfunction.
[2]

Frontal Release Signs in Adults

In adults, the reemergence of primitive reflexes indicates frontal lobe pathology or diffuse cerebral dysfunction. These reflexes, termed frontal release signs, occur when cortical inhibition fails due to neurodegeneration, vascular disease, or structural lesions. The most clinically significant frontal release signs include the snout reflex, glabellar tap, palmomental reflex, and grasp reflex.
[3]

Snout reflex

The snout reflex is elicited by gently tapping the upper lip with a reflex hammer or finger.
[3]
A positive response consists of puckering or protruding the lips without turning the head. The snout reflex specifically localizes to frontal lobe lesions and demonstrates higher specificity for frontal pathology than other frontal release signs. The snout reflex must be differentiated from the rooting reflex, which involves turning the head toward the stimulus in addition to lip puckering. A snout reflex without head turning suggests frontal lobe dysfunction, while a snout reflex with head turning indicates more diffuse cerebral pathology affecting both frontal lobes and deeper structures.
[4]

Glabellar tap (Myerson sign)

The glabellar tap is performed by repeatedly tapping the patient's forehead in the midline between the eyebrows (the glabella) with a finger or reflex hammer. In normal individuals, the first few taps elicit blinking that quickly habituates, and the patient stops blinking after 4 to 5 taps as the stimulus becomes familiar. An abnormal response, termed the Myerson sign, consists of persistent blinking without habituation throughout 10 or more taps.

The glabellar tap reflex is associated with Parkinson disease, frontotemporal dementia, and progressive supranuclear palsy.
[3]
Failure to habituate to blinking reflects dysfunction in frontal cortical areas responsible for habituating to repetitive sensory stimuli.
[4]
The test must be performed correctly, as tapping from above or behind the patient can prevent visual anticipation, which could suppress the reflex.

Palmomental reflex

The palmomental reflex is elicited by firmly stroking the thenar eminence with a blunt object (eg, a key). An abnormal response involves contraction of the mentalis muscle on the ipsilateral side of the chin, causing visible dimpling or puckering of the chin skin.
[3]
This reflex occurs in both normal older adult individuals and patients with frontal lobe disease, making its clinical significance somewhat controversial. However, bilateral brisk palmomental reflexes, combined with other frontal release signs, strengthen the evidence for frontal pathology.
[4]
The reflex is more prevalent in patients with frontotemporal dementia and may appear early in the disease.
[3]

Clinical Significance of Frontal Release Signs

Studies demonstrate that dementia patients have a 13.94- to 16.38-fold higher risk of exhibiting primitive reflexes compared to cognitively normal age-matched controls.
[3]
Frontotemporal dementia (FTD) patients demonstrate frontal release signs more frequently than Alzheimer disease patients, with 63.6% of FTD patients exhibiting primitive reflexes compared to 48.5% in Alzheimer disease. Neuroimaging correlates of primitive reflexes show hypometabolism in bilateral superior frontal gyri, putamen, and thalamus, reflecting dysfunction in the corticostriatal motor circuit.

The presence of multiple frontal release signs helps differentiate dementia subtypes: predominantly frontal signs suggest FTD. In contrast, a mix of cortical and subcortical signs suggests Alzheimer disease or Lewy body dementia.
[4]
The clinical utility of frontal release signs extends beyond diagnosis to prognosis and safety assessment.
[3]
Patients with multiple frontal release signs demonstrate higher rates of falls, aspiration, and functional decline. The reemergence of oral primitive reflexes (rooting, snout, sucking) specifically increases the risk of dysphagia, aspiration pneumonia, and malnutrition, necessitating modified diets and supervised feeding.
[24]

Additional Considerations

Conditions Associated with Abnormal Primitive Reflexes in Pediatric Populations

Cerebral palsy

Persistence of multiple primitive reflexes beyond 4 to 6 months is strongly associated with cerebral palsy and developmental delay, and the presence of 5 or more abnormal reflexes correlates closely with these diagnoses. Specific patterns of retained reflexes may also help differentiate the following cerebral palsy subtypes.
[18]
[10]

Spastic cerebral palsy
: Characterized by retained ATNR, persistent palmar grasp, obligatory Moro reflex, and persistent Babinski sign beyond 24 months. Increased muscle tone, hyperreflexia, and ankle clonus accompany these reflex abnormalities. The retained ATNR contributes to asymmetric posturing and prevents midline hand activities.

Dyskinetic cerebral palsy
: May show fluctuating primitive reflex responses that vary with the infant's state and movement attempts. Reflexes may be absent during hypotonic phases and exaggerated during hypertonic or dystonic episodes.

Ataxic cerebral palsy
: Often demonstrates weaker or inconsistent primitive reflexes rather than strong persistence, reflecting cerebellar dysfunction affecting reflex integration and motor coordination.
[18]
[10]

Early identification through systematic assessment of primitive reflexes enables timely referral to developmental specialists, initiation of physical and occupational therapy, and implementation of early intervention strategies to optimize developmental outcomes.
[25]
The window for neuroplasticity is greatest in the first 2 to 3 years of life, making early detection critical.
[26]

Autism spectrum disorders

Persistent primitive reflexes serve as early indicators of autism, with retained oral reflexes (rooting, sucking) and the ATNR occurring more often in children later diagnosed with autism spectrum disorder (ASD). Retained oral reflexes may contribute to the feeding selectivity and texture aversions characteristic of autism. A reflexive tongue thrust limits oral exploration of varied textures, potentially contributing to restricted dietary preferences and sensory processing difficulties. Retained ATNR in autism interferes with bilateral coordination, making activities requiring both hands (eg, catching a ball, using scissors, or typing) particularly challenging.

Because reflex integration depends on frontal lobe and cerebellar maturation, retention indicates delayed neural development in pathways critical for motor planning and sensory integration. Children with ASD who demonstrate multiple retained primitive reflexes benefit from occupational therapy interventions focused on sensory and reflex integration, which complement behavioral and educational interventions.
[20]

Feeding and aspiration risk

Persistent oral primitive reflexes beyond 6 months severely interfere with feeding development.
[21]
[24]
A tongue positioned anteriorly cannot perform the sophisticated movements required for bolus formation and propulsion during chewing and swallowing. Clinical manifestations include:

Excessive drooling persisting beyond infancy due to the inability to collect and swallow saliva

Difficulty managing textures beyond purees, with gagging on any food with texture

Prolonged mealtimes with inadequate intake leading to failure to thrive

Increased aspiration risk from discoordinated swallowing mechanisms

Social limitations preventing participation in family meals or age-appropriate eating contexts

These feeding difficulties necessitate a comprehensive evaluation by a speech-language pathologist to assess swallowing, select a modified diet, and implement therapeutic feeding interventions. Some children require supplemental tube feeding to ensure adequate nutrition while oral motor skills develop.

Primitive Reflex Assessment in Geriatric Populations

Dementia diagnosis and differentiation

Primitive reflex assessment contributes to dementia diagnosis and subtype differentiation in older adult patients. The pattern and severity of frontal release signs help distinguish between the following dementia types.
[3]
[4]

Frontotemporal dementia
: High prevalence of grasp reflex, snout reflex, and palmomental reflex correlating with frontal lobe atrophy on neuroimaging. Behavioral variant FTD demonstrates frontal release signs earlier and more prominently than language variants.

Alzheimer disease
: May show frontal release signs in the moderate-to-severe stages, but typically presents with memory deficits and posterior cortical signs (parietal dysfunction) earlier than frontal signs. Primitive reflexes appear later in the disease course than in FTD.

Vascular dementia
: Frontal release signs depend on lesion location. Subcortical vascular dementia affecting frontal-subcortical circuits demonstrates primitive reflexes, while cortical vascular dementia shows more variable patterns.

Lewy body dementia
: May demonstrate Parkinsonian features, including non-habituating glabellar tap, along with fluctuating cognition and visual hallucinations.

Functional decline and safety concerns

The presence of primitive reflexes in older adult patients correlates with functional decline and increased safety risks. Frontal release signs include:

Greater impairment in activities of daily living (ADLs) and instrumental ADLs

Higher fall risk due to postural instability and impaired protective responses

Increased aspiration risk when oral primitive reflexes interfere with coordinated swallowing

Greater need for supervised or assisted care

Shorter time to nursing home placement
[3]
[24]

These associations make primitive reflex assessment valuable for prognosis, care planning, and family counseling regarding expected disease trajectory and care needs.

Summary and Interprofessional Team Considerations

Primitive reflexes are involuntary, brainstem-mediated motor responses that emerge during fetal development and support neonatal feeding, protection, attachment, and early motor function. These reflexes are normally inhibited during the first months of life as cortical maturation and voluntary motor control develop. Absent, asymmetric, exaggerated, persistent, or reemergent primitive reflexes may indicate neurological dysfunction and serve as important clinical markers across the lifespan. In infants and children, abnormal reflex patterns are associated with conditions such as cerebral palsy, developmental delay, autism spectrum disorder, perinatal brain injury, and neonatal withdrawal syndromes.
[6]
In adults, the reappearance of primitive reflexes as frontal release signs may indicate frontal lobe pathology, dementia, or other neurodegenerative disorders. Accurate assessment requires standardized examination techniques, age-appropriate interpretation, and familiarity with validated neurodevelopmental assessment tools to support early diagnosis, prognostication, and intervention.
[26]

Interprofessional collaboration is essential for optimizing outcomes in patients with abnormal primitive reflex findings. Physicians, primary care clinicians, and advanced practitioners perform comprehensive neurologic evaluations, coordinate diagnostic testing, identify high-risk populations, and facilitate timely referral to developmental specialists, neurologists, geriatricians, or rehabilitation services. Nurses play a key role in developmental surveillance, patient and caregiver education, monitoring functional status, and reinforcing follow-up recommendations. Physical therapists, occupational therapists, and speech-language pathologists evaluate motor, sensory, feeding, and swallowing impairments and implement individualized intervention plans that support developmental progress and reduce complications such as aspiration, malnutrition, and functional decline. Pharmacists contribute by reviewing medications that may affect neurologic function and supporting safe medication management in patients with complex neurologic disorders. Effective communication, shared decision-making with patients and caregivers, coordinated care transitions, and ongoing reassessment promote early intervention, reduce long-term disability, improve safety, and enhance quality of life through a systems-based, patient-centered approach.
[26]

Nursing, Allied Health, and Interprofessional Team Interventions

Neonatal and Pediatric Care

Nursing responsibilities

Nurses serve as frontline observers of primitive reflexes during feeding, diaper changes, and routine caregiving activities.
[16]
Ongoing observation enables early recognition of concerning findings, including feeding difficulties, asymmetric movements, exaggerated startle responses, and other abnormal reflex patterns. Careful documentation of serial observations helps clinicians identify changes in reflex expression over time and supports accurate developmental monitoring.

Within the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU), nurses perform systematic reflex screening for high-risk infants, including those born prematurely, with low birth weight, following perinatal asphyxia, or with prenatal substance exposure.
[17]
Nursing responsibilities also include educating parents regarding expected reflex integration timelines and red-flag warning signs that warrant further evaluation, eg, persistent rooting, inability to bring the hands to midline, strong tongue thrust, and excessive startle responses.

Lactation and feeding support specialists

Lactation consultants and feeding support specialists require a thorough understanding of rooting and sucking reflexes to effectively address breastfeeding and feeding challenges.
[21]
Clinical management includes identifying weak rooting responses that impair latch initiation and recognizing persistent rooting behaviors that interfere with the transition to spoon feeding. These specialists also provide caregivers with strategies to minimize inadvertent triggering of primitive reflexes during feeding and support the development of age-appropriate oral-motor skills.

Therapeutic Interventions

Neurodevelopmental physical therapy

Neurodevelopmental physical therapy supports assessment of gross motor development, evaluates the functional impact of retained primitive reflexes, and promotes integration of persistent reflex patterns into functional movement.
[25]
Therapeutic interventions include positioning strategies, facilitation of voluntary movement, core strengthening exercises, and developmental sequencing activities that progress from tummy time to sitting and subsequently to crawling. Early intervention helps optimize motor development and reduces the long-term impact of retained reflexes on functional mobility.

Occupational therapy

Occupational therapy focuses on fine motor development, sensory processing, and performance of age-appropriate daily activities.
[26]
Management of retained palmar grasp reflexes includes tactile desensitization techniques, facilitation of hand opening, task-oriented fine-motor activities, graded hand-strengthening exercises to promote mature grasp development, and sensory integration interventions designed to improve functional participation.

Speech-language pathology

Speech-language pathologists assess oral-motor function and swallowing safety in children with retained oral primitive reflexes.
[24]
[21]
Therapeutic interventions include perioral and intraoral desensitization, tongue mobility training, graded advancement of food textures, compensatory feeding strategies, and dietary modifications, eg, pureed foods, mechanically soft diets, or thickened liquids. Alternative feeding methods, including gastrostomy tube placement, may become necessary when oral intake fails to provide adequate nutrition or safe swallowing cannot be achieved.

Interprofessional Coordination

Successful management of retained primitive reflexes depends on coordinated interdisciplinary care. Regular team meetings support ongoing review of patient progress and facilitate timely modification of treatment plans. Shared documentation promotes unified treatment planning and enhances continuity of care across disciplines.
[25]
Consistent terminology and communication among healthcare professionals improve clinical decision-making and reduce fragmentation of care. Active family participation remains central to successful intervention, and all team members should support caregiver understanding of treatment goals and expected outcomes.

Geriatric Care

Nursing care for dementia

Nurses caring for older adults with dementia must recognize reemergent primitive reflexes as potential indicators of neurological decline.
[3]
Clinical management includes implementing safety measures to reduce the risk of falls and aspiration. Effective strategies include maintaining clear walking pathways, ensuring adequate lighting, promoting appropriate footwear use, providing assistive devices when indicated, and supervising ambulation based on functional status.

Feeding-related interventions frequently require collaboration with speech-language pathology services.
[24]
Management may include modifying food textures, reducing environmental distractions during meals, implementing safe swallowing techniques and positioning strategies, and adhering to aspiration precautions, eg, maintaining an upright position after meals and providing consistent oral hygiene.

Family education and support

The interprofessional team plays a critical role in educating families regarding the clinical significance of frontal release signs and their association with progressive neurological disease.
[24]
Family discussions should include realistic expectations regarding cognitive and functional decline, recognition of aspiration symptoms (eg, coughing, wet vocal quality, and recurrent pneumonia), and practical home-based fall-prevention strategies. Team members should connect families with caregiver support programs, respite care resources, and community services while facilitating discussions regarding advance care planning and future care needs.

Interprofessional dementia care

Comprehensive dementia management requires coordination among neurology, geriatrics, nursing, physical therapy, occupational therapy, speech-language pathology, nutrition, and social work professionals.
[3]
[24]
Regular interdisciplinary care conferences support ongoing assessment of patient safety, functional status, nutritional needs, and family concerns. Consistent clinical approaches across home, outpatient, hospital, and long-term care environments promote continuity of care and improve patient-centered outcomes.

Documentation and Monitoring

High-quality assessment and longitudinal monitoring require the use of validated evaluation tools. Clinical documentation should include the presence and quality of primitive reflexes, symmetry of responses, associated movements, serial changes over time, and relationships between reflex findings and developmental progress or functional decline. Structured electronic health record templates support systematic screening, standardized documentation, and long-term tracking of neurological findings across the continuum of care.
[6]
