# Mixed phenotype acute leukaemia (MPAL) - Management

## Grounding note
DeVita's leukaemia chapter (Section 13, "Management of Acute Leukemias") names this entity only
once, in passing, while explaining that acute leukaemias are classically split into AML and ALL
and that "a subset of patients with acute leukemia are considered as having ambiguous lineage
(commonly referred to as biphenotypic or mixed lineage acute leukemia) with clinical and
therapeutic implications." The chapter does not carry a dedicated management section for this
entity; its treatment discussion proceeds separately down the AML and ALL pathways. Because of
this, almost every specific management claim below is marked as general oncology standard rather
than DeVita-sourced. Where a claim is DeVita-grounded it is stated plainly without that tag.

## What DeVita's section establishes
- Acute leukaemia is diagnosed and classified using morphology, immunophenotyping by flow
  cytometry, and cytogenetic/molecular testing, and this diagnostic framework is the entry point
  for every acute leukaemia including ambiguous-lineage disease.
- Ambiguous-lineage (biphenotypic/mixed lineage) acute leukaemia is explicitly recognised as
  having "clinical and therapeutic implications" distinct from AML or ALL alone, meaning it is
  not simply treated as a variant of either parent disease by default.

## Diagnostic confirmation before treatment (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease)
- Treatment cannot be planned until the diagnosis is secured by comprehensive immunophenotyping
  (flow cytometry across a full lineage-marker panel) plus cytogenetics and molecular studies,
  specifically to identify or exclude BCR-ABL1 and KMT2A (MLL) rearrangements, since these define
  recognised genetic subtypes with distinct treatment implications.
- Because MPAL sits at the interface of myeloid and lymphoid disease, misclassification changes
  the entire treatment strategy, so pathology review should be by a centre experienced in
  ambiguous-lineage leukaemia before induction begins.

## Treatment approach by disease biology (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease)
There is no single stage-based framework for MPAL analogous to solid tumour staging; treatment
is stratified mainly by genetic subtype, lineage predominance, and fitness for intensive therapy.

- **BCR-ABL1-positive MPAL**: managed with an ALL-type induction backbone combined with a
  BCR-ABL1-targeted tyrosine kinase inhibitor, mirroring the approach used in Philadelphia
  chromosome-positive ALL, given the shared driver lesion.
- **KMT2A (MLL)-rearranged MPAL**: recognised as a distinct, generally higher-risk genetic
  subtype; induction choice and post-remission strategy are individualised, with a lower
  threshold for proceeding to transplant given the adverse biology associated with KMT2A
  rearrangement in acute leukaemia more broadly.
- **Other (non-BCR-ABL1, non-KMT2A) MPAL**: most commonly treated with an ALL-based induction
  regimen rather than an AML-based one, reflecting reported outcome data favouring
  lymphoid-directed induction in this group, though myeloid-directed induction may be considered
  when myeloid antigen expression clearly predominates or when a centre's own experience
  supports it.
- Across subtypes, treatment is delivered by clinicians experienced in acute leukaemia given the
  absence of a single standardised regimen; enrolment in a clinical trial should be actively
  sought where available given how uncommon and heterogeneous this diagnosis is.

## Role of allogeneic transplantation (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease)
- Allogeneic haematopoietic stem cell transplantation in first remission is considered for many
  patients with MPAL, reflecting the disease's generally aggressive behaviour and the relatively
  limited durability of chemotherapy alone, particularly for KMT2A-rearranged and other
  higher-risk subtypes.
- Transplant timing and eligibility follow the same fitness, donor-availability, and comorbidity
  assessment used for other acute leukaemias.

## Role of surgery and radiotherapy
Surgery has no role in MPAL, which is a systemic bone marrow malignancy rather than a localised
tumour. Radiotherapy is not part of primary treatment; its use is confined to the same
supportive/palliative indications seen in other acute leukaemias, such as symptomatic
extramedullary disease, and is not addressed specifically for MPAL in DeVita's chapter (general
oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Monitoring
- Response is monitored through serial bone marrow assessment for morphologic remission,
  supplemented by measurable/minimal residual disease testing where the leukaemia's
  immunophenotype or molecular marker allows it, analogous to monitoring practice in AML and ALL
  (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Patients who carry BCR-ABL1 should have ongoing molecular monitoring of that transcript through
  and after induction, mirroring monitoring in Philadelphia-positive ALL (general oncology
  standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Standard supportive-care monitoring for cytopenias, infection, and tumour lysis during induction
  applies as it does to other acute leukaemias.

## When to refer
- Any suspected or confirmed MPAL should be referred promptly to a haematology-oncology centre
  with expertise in acute leukaemia and access to comprehensive flow cytometry, cytogenetics, and
  molecular diagnostics, given how easily this entity can be misclassified as AML or ALL on
  limited testing.
- Early referral for transplant evaluation is appropriate once remission status and genetic
  subtype are known, so that donor search and post-remission planning are not delayed.
- Given the rarity of the diagnosis, referral to a centre that can offer clinical trial enrolment
  should be considered wherever feasible (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on
  this disease).

## Deliberately omitted
No numeric outcome statistics (survival rates, remission rates) for MPAL were found in DeVita's
text, so none are given. No specific named regimens, trial names, or drug doses are included, in
line with the dose-free/no-invented-regimen rule; only drug classes already implied by the
grounded ALL/BCR-ABL1 framework above are referenced generically.

Sources: DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of Oncology, 12th ed.
NCCN Guidelines (Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia / Acute Myeloid Leukemia) where general standards
are noted above.
