# Papillary craniopharyngioma (PCP) - Management

Papillary craniopharyngioma is a WHO grade 1 sellar/suprasellar epithelial tumour that occurs almost
exclusively in adults. Nearly all cases carry an activating BRAF V600E mutation, which distinguishes it
biologically and therapeutically from the CTNNB1-driven adamantinomatous type. Management is
multidisciplinary, centred on neurosurgery, radiation oncology, endocrinology and ophthalmology, and is
shaped by the tumour's proximity to the pituitary gland, hypothalamus, optic apparatus and brain stem.

## Treatment approach and intent

There are no phase III trials comparing surgical resection with radiotherapy for craniopharyngioma;
treatment recommendations rest on retrospective single-institution series. In practice, treatment intent
is disease control with preservation of visual, endocrine and hypothalamic function, since these tumours
are histologically benign but sit in a location where aggressive local therapy can cause major morbidity.

- **Primary approach**: maximal safe surgical resection, generally by a microsurgical subfrontal or
  pterional approach, or increasingly an endoscopic endonasal skull-base approach through the sphenoid
  sinus. Larger tumours may need a bifrontal or skull-base approach with supraorbital craniotomy.
- **Complete resection is the optimal surgical goal**, but is deliberately not pursued when there is no
  clear surgical plane between tumour and hypothalamus, because aggressive resection in that setting risks
  devastating hypothalamic injury, pituitary stalk damage, and permanent diabetes insipidus (DI).
- A conservative surgical strategy (biopsy and/or cyst drainage) followed by adjuvant radiotherapy is
  associated with better quality of life, less neuropsychological and psychosocial impairment, and less
  hypothalamic/pituitary dysfunction than an attempted gross total or near-total resection, and is
  considered an optimal balance between local control and treatment-related morbidity.
- For predominantly cystic tumours, an Ommaya reservoir can be placed to allow instillation of sclerosing
  agents into the cyst as an alternative or adjunct to resection.

## Role of radiotherapy

Radiotherapy is a mainstay adjuvant treatment, not a primary alternative to surgery in the absence of
supporting phase III evidence, but the combined approach (maximal safe resection plus adjuvant
radiotherapy) improves local control compared with surgery alone.

- Fractionated radiotherapy is recommended for patients with gross residual disease or recurrence after
  surgery.
- Highly conformal techniques (IMRT, VMAT, image-guided RT, proton therapy) are used to spare the adjacent
  optic apparatus, which is typically the dose-limiting structure.
- Proton therapy is used specifically to reduce long-term risks of vasculopathy, neurocognitive decline
  and secondary malignancy, particularly relevant in younger patients.
- Stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) is an option for small-volume residual or recurrent disease that is
  adequately separated from the optic chiasm, with good reported local control; it is not appropriate when
  the tumour lies close to the chiasm.
- Radioisotope/intracavitary brachytherapy (for example with phosphorus-32) is an option specifically for
  progressive cystic disease, aiming to control the cyst while preserving visual, endocrine and
  neurocognitive function.
- The most frequent late complication of radiotherapy is hypopituitarism; radiation-associated optic
  neuropathy, temporal lobe necrosis, neurocognitive dysfunction and secondary malignancy are described but
  uncommon.

## Systemic/targeted therapy

Because nearly all papillary craniopharyngiomas harbour BRAF V600E, dual BRAF and MEK inhibition is a
targeted option with reported dramatic treatment responses in this histology. This is an evolving,
molecularly matched approach rather than a standard first-line therapy replacing surgery, and is best
considered in a multidisciplinary/skull-base tumour board setting, including for neoadjuvant cytoreduction
before surgery or for recurrent/unresectable disease (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section
on this disease, for the specific neoadjuvant/recurrent-disease positioning; the existence of marked
responses to combined BRAF/MEK inhibition in papillary craniopharyngioma itself is grounded in the DeVita
text). Cytotoxic chemotherapy has no established role in this disease and is not addressed in the source
material, so none is proposed here.

## Endocrine, visual and hypothalamic supportive care

These are not optional adjuncts but core parts of management given the anatomic location of the tumour.

- Lifelong hormone replacement is required for patients who develop hypopituitarism, and inhaled/intranasal
  desmopressin is used for control of diabetes insipidus, which is common after aggressive stalk
  manipulation or resection.
- Baseline and postoperative formal visual acuity, colour vision and perimetry testing with ophthalmology
  follow-up, since visual field defects are a presenting and monitorable feature and most patients with
  preoperative visual loss can expect at least some improvement after surgery.
- Neuropsychological assessment should be anticipated after aggressive resection, since a majority of such
  patients report deficits in short-term memory, attentiveness, executive function and processing speed.
- Ongoing surveillance and management of hypothalamic dysfunction (weight, temperature regulation,
  behaviour) and its metabolic consequences.

## Monitoring

- Surveillance MRI for recurrence or progression; more frequent surveillance imaging has been associated
  with earlier detection of progression in reported series.
- Long-term endocrine and ophthalmologic follow-up is necessary for all patients, given that endocrine,
  visual and neuropsychological dysfunction related to the tumour, surgery and radiation can persist or
  emerge late.
- BRAF V600E testing on resected/biopsied tissue should be performed to confirm eligibility for
  BRAF/MEK-targeted therapy if needed later in the disease course.

## When to refer

- All patients require referral to a multidisciplinary skull-base or neuro-oncology team combining
  neurosurgery, radiation oncology, endocrinology and ophthalmology before a treatment plan is finalised,
  given the absence of a single standard-of-care pathway and the trade-off between extent of resection and
  functional morbidity (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Refer for endocrinology assessment before and after any intervention, and urgently if there are signs of
  adrenal insufficiency or diabetes insipidus.
- Refer for ophthalmology assessment for baseline and serial visual field/acuity testing given chiasmal
  proximity.
- Consider referral to a centre with molecular testing and access to BRAF/MEK inhibitor therapy when
  BRAF V600E is confirmed, particularly for residual, recurrent or surgically difficult disease.

Sources: DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of Oncology, 12th ed.
