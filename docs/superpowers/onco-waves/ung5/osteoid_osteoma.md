## Overall approach

Osteoid osteoma is a benign, self-limited bone-forming lesion that does not metastasise and does
not undergo malignant transformation (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on
this disease). Because the natural history is towards spontaneous involution over years,
management is driven by symptom control and patient preference rather than oncologic urgency
(general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

- Confirm the diagnosis with thin-section CT to localise the nidus before committing to any
  treatment pathway (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Discuss two broad options with the patient: continued medical management awaiting spontaneous
  resolution, or active ablation of the nidus (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's
  section on this disease).
- There is no staging system and no systemic therapy role for osteoid osteoma; it is treated as a
  localised, single-site problem (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this
  disease).

## Lines of therapy

**First line - medical management.** Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, used to blunt the
prostaglandin-driven nocturnal pain that characterises the lesion, can be sufficient definitive
management in patients willing to tolerate symptoms while awaiting spontaneous involution
(general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease). This approach avoids a
procedure but requires sustained anti-inflammatory use over a period of months to years and is
less suitable when pain is poorly controlled, when scoliosis is progressing, or when the patient
prefers a definitive fix (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

**Definitive treatment - image-guided thermal ablation.** CT-guided percutaneous radiofrequency
(or other thermal) ablation of the nidus is the treatment of choice for patients who want
definitive resolution rather than prolonged medical therapy (general oncology standard, not from
DeVita's section on this disease). It is minimally invasive, has a high success rate, and allows
histological sampling of the nidus when the diagnosis needs confirmation (general oncology
standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

**Surgery.** Open surgical excision (en bloc removal or curettage of the nidus) is reserved for
lesions where thermal ablation is unsuitable, chiefly when the nidus lies close to neurovascular
structures, the spinal cord, or a joint surface where thermal injury risk is unacceptable
(general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease). Surgery is also the
default when ablation has failed or when tissue diagnosis and mechanical stabilisation are both
needed at the same sitting (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this
disease).

**Spinal lesions.** Osteoid osteoma of the spine deserves specific attention because it can
present with painful scoliosis, the lesion classically sitting on the concave side of the curve;
treating the nidus (medically or with ablation/excision) is directed at both the pain and the
secondary spinal deformity, and children with a shorter duration of symptoms are more likely to
have curve resolution once the nidus is treated (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's
section on this disease).

## Monitoring

- Clinical response to non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs is itself a useful monitoring tool;
  persistence or recurrence of the characteristic nocturnal pain after treatment suggests
  incomplete ablation or excision of the nidus (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's
  section on this disease).
- Follow-up imaging is used to look for a residual or recurrent nidus after ablation or surgery,
  since incomplete treatment of the nidus is the main cause of persistent symptoms (general
  oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- In children with spinal disease, scoliosis should be followed clinically and radiographically
  after treatment to confirm the curve is resolving rather than progressing (general oncology
  standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Atypical features on follow-up, such as unexpected lesion growth, cortical destruction, or a
  soft-tissue mass, should prompt reconsideration of the diagnosis rather than assuming ongoing
  osteoid osteoma (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## When to refer

- Refer to an orthopaedic oncology service for any suspected osteoid osteoma, both to confirm the
  diagnosis against mimics (osteoblastoma, Brodie abscess, stress fracture, osteomyelitis, Ewing
  sarcoma, or other primary bone malignancy) and to plan ablation versus surgery (general
  oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Refer urgently where imaging is atypical (lesion larger than expected, cortical destruction, a
  soft-tissue mass) or where growth is rapid, since these features raise concern for osteoblastoma
  or a malignant bone tumour rather than osteoid osteoma (general oncology standard, not from
  DeVita's section on this disease).
- Refer spinal lesions with progressive painful scoliosis or any neurological signs promptly,
  given the added complexity of ablation or excision near the spinal cord (general oncology
  standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Refer for surgical opinion specifically when a lesion is adjacent to neurovascular structures
  or the spinal cord, or when a prior ablation attempt has failed (general oncology standard, not
  from DeVita's section on this disease).
