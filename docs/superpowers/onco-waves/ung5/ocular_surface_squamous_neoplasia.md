# Ocular surface squamous neoplasia (OSSN) — management narrative

FLAG: needs manual sourcing. DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of
Oncology, 12th ed. does not contain a dedicated section on ocular surface squamous neoplasia,
conjunctival intraepithelial neoplasia, or conjunctival squamous cell carcinoma. Searches of the
DeVita text for "ocular surface squamous", "OSSN", and "conjunctiv*" return only unrelated hits
(eyelid sebaceous carcinoma, conjunctival melanoma, drug-induced conjunctivitis, orbital invasion
by other tumours) and none address the management of this disease. Every claim below is therefore
marked as general oncology/ophthalmology standard rather than DeVita-sourced, consistent with the
existing KB reference entry for this disease, which itself cites Kanski's Clinical Ophthalmology
and the WHO Classification of Eye Tumours rather than DeVita.

## Treatment approach by stage/intent (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease)

OSSN is a spectrum, and treatment intent tracks where a lesion sits on that spectrum rather than a
formal TNM stage in most clinics.

- **Intraepithelial disease (mild-to-severe dysplasia, carcinoma in situ, basement membrane
  intact):** treated with curative intent, aiming for complete clearance while preserving the
  ocular surface. Both a primarily surgical approach and a primarily medical (topical) approach
  are accepted; choice depends on lesion size, location, and whether margins can be cleared without
  excessive removal of normal conjunctiva.
- **Invasive squamous cell carcinoma (basement membrane breached):** treated with curative intent
  when the disease is confined to the globe/ocular adnexa, escalating the extent of surgery to
  match depth of invasion. The goal shifts from surface clearance to control of a locally invasive
  malignancy with a lower threshold for orbital-sparing or orbit-sacrificing surgery if invasion is
  extensive.
- **Extensive/recurrent disease with deep orbital or intraocular invasion:** treated with intent to
  control local disease and preserve life and orbital structures where possible; this is where
  radical surgery (enucleation or exenteration) and adjunctive radiotherapy come in.
- Regional or distant metastatic disease is rare but, when present, warrants multidisciplinary
  oncology input beyond ophthalmology alone, since OSSN can behave like a cutaneous/mucosal
  squamous cell carcinoma once it spreads.

## Lines of therapy (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease)

- **First-line for intraepithelial disease:** either (a) complete excisional biopsy using a
  no-touch technique with wide clinical margins, combined with adjunctive cryotherapy to the
  conjunctival edges and often alcohol epitheliectomy if the cornea is involved, or (b) primary
  topical chemotherapy/immunotherapy (agents used include interferon alfa-2b, mitomycin C, and
  5-fluorouracil, named here only as drug classes/agents with no dose or schedule implied).
- **Adjuvant use of topical therapy:** topical agents are also used after surgery when margins are
  involved or close, or when disease is diffuse and not amenable to complete excision without
  excessive tissue loss.
- **Second-line/salvage for recurrent or refractory intraepithelial disease:** repeat excision with
  cryotherapy, switching to or adding a different topical agent class, or combining surgery with
  topical therapy.
- **Invasive disease:** wider surgical excision is the mainstay; adjuvant plaque brachytherapy or
  external radiotherapy is added when margins cannot be cleared or invasion is deeper, and surgery
  is escalated to enucleation or exenteration when the tumour extends too far into the globe or
  orbit to be controlled by more conservative resection.
- There is no established role for systemic chemotherapy as routine therapy for localised OSSN;
  systemic treatment is reserved for the rare cases with regional nodal or distant spread, and
  should be approached the way other advanced cutaneous/mucosal squamous cell carcinomas are
  approached, with multidisciplinary tumour board input.

## Role of surgery, radiotherapy, and systemic therapy (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease)

- **Surgery** is central across the spectrum: a no-touch excisional technique minimises seeding of
  tumour cells, and adjunctive cryotherapy to the surgical bed and margins is used to reduce local
  recurrence, which is common after narrow-margin excision alone.
- **Radiotherapy** (plaque brachytherapy or external beam) is an adjunct for invasive disease with
  positive or close margins, or for recurrent disease, rather than a primary modality for
  intraepithelial disease.
- **Radical surgery** (enucleation, exenteration) is reserved for extensive intraocular or orbital
  invasion that cannot otherwise be controlled, and is a last resort given its impact on vision and
  cosmesis.
- **Systemic therapy** has essentially no established role in routine, localised OSSN management;
  its use is confined to the rare metastatic presentation and should be individualised.

## Monitoring (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease)

- Long-term slit-lamp follow-up is required after any treatment modality, since recurrence is
  common, particularly after narrow-margin surgery without cryotherapy or after topical therapy
  alone.
- Vital-dye staining (rose bengal or lissamine green) and anterior-segment optical coherence
  tomography can be used at follow-up visits to detect subclinical recurrence non-invasively before
  it becomes clinically obvious.
- Any suspicious regrowth, new leukoplakia, or a lesion that becomes fixed to the underlying sclera
  should prompt repeat biopsy rather than assuming recurrence is superficial.
- Patients should be counselled on lifelong ultraviolet protection, since ongoing UV exposure is a
  driver of both new lesions and recurrence.
- In patients with aggressive or young-onset disease, or disease that recurs despite adequate
  treatment, evaluation for underlying immunosuppression (including HIV testing) should be pursued
  or repeated, since undiagnosed immunosuppression can explain treatment failure.

## When to refer (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease)

- Any lesion suspicious for OSSN, including atypical or recurrent "pterygium," should be referred
  for biopsy and histological confirmation before destructive or excisional treatment, rather than
  being excised on a purely clinical diagnosis.
- Confirmed or strongly suspected invasive squamous cell carcinoma, disease with scleral fixation,
  or intraocular/orbital extension should be referred promptly to an ophthalmic oncology service for
  multidisciplinary management, since these findings change both the extent of surgery required and
  the need for adjuvant radiotherapy.
- Aggressive or young-onset disease should prompt referral for HIV/immunosuppression work-up
  alongside ophthalmic oncology referral.
- Any regional lymphadenopathy or suspicion of metastatic spread should prompt referral to medical
  oncology for systemic staging and multidisciplinary discussion, since this is outside the scope
  of ophthalmology alone.
