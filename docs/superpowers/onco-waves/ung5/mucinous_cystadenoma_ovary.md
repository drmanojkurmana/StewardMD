# Ovarian mucinous cystadenoma - management

## Grounding note

DeVita's oncology text does not carry a dedicated management section for benign ovarian mucinous cystadenoma. The two hits located mention mucinous cystadenoma only in passing, as the benign counterpart of mucinous tumours of low malignant potential and malignant mucinous carcinoma in a table of ovarian cystic neoplasms, and as part of a general discussion of the benign/borderline/malignant differential for an adnexal mass. Neither passage describes a management pathway for this specific benign entity. Everything below is therefore drawn from general oncology and gynaecological practice standard and is marked accordingly; nothing here is presented as DeVita-sourced.

## Treatment approach by stage or intent

Mucinous cystadenoma is a benign tumour, so there is no "stage" or systemic-therapy intent to discuss in the way a malignancy would require. Management intent is curative excision plus exclusion of an occult malignant or borderline component (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

- Complete surgical removal of the cyst, by cystectomy or unilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, is the definitive treatment (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- The specimen should be removed intact where feasible, avoiding intraoperative rupture or spillage of mucinous contents, because of the theoretical risk of peritoneal seeding associated with mucinous ovarian disease (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- In younger patients who wish to preserve fertility, ovarian cystectomy with preservation of the contralateral ovary and uterus is the preferred approach when the mass is amenable to it (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Extensive histological sampling of the excised specimen is required, since a focal borderline or malignant component can be missed on limited sampling of an otherwise benign-appearing tumour (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- If the tumour is bilateral, or mucinous ascites or peritoneal disease is found, the appendix and bowel should be evaluated for a primary gastrointestinal or appendiceal mucinous neoplasm, since bilateral or metastatic mucinous disease more often originates outside the ovary (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Lines of therapy

Not applicable. There is no systemic or radiotherapeutic "line of therapy" concept for a benign cystadenoma; surgery alone is curative when the lesion is genuinely benign (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Role of surgery, radiotherapy, and systemic therapy

- Surgery is the only treatment modality required for a confirmed benign mucinous cystadenoma (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Radiotherapy has no role in the management of this benign lesion.
- Systemic (cytotoxic or targeted) therapy has no role unless final histology upgrades the diagnosis to a borderline mucinous tumour or invasive mucinous carcinoma, at which point management follows the pathway for that upgraded diagnosis rather than for cystadenoma (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease). DeVita's adnexal-mass discussion notes that tumour markers such as CA125, CEA, and CA19-9 are used preoperatively to help stratify a mass as more or less likely malignant, though it also states CA125 is not expressed by mucinous ovarian tumours and is unreliable for this histology, which is a point genuinely found in the DeVita passage on CA125.

## Monitoring

- Postoperative clinical and imaging follow-up is reasonable after excision, mainly to confirm resolution and detect any recurrence or a missed contralateral lesion (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- No ongoing surveillance regimen analogous to malignancy follow-up is needed once complete excision and adequate benign histology are confirmed (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## When to refer

- Refer to gynaecological oncology if the final histology shows borderline (low malignant potential) or invasive features, if the tumour is bilateral, or if there is associated mucinous ascites or peritoneal disease raising concern for pseudomyxoma peritonei or a gastrointestinal primary (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- A general gynaecologist can appropriately manage a mass with reassuring imaging features and no red-flag findings, reserving oncology referral for atypical, bilateral, or histologically upgraded cases (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

Sources: DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of Oncology, 12th ed.
