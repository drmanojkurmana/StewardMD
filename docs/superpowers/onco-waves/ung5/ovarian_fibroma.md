# Ovarian fibroma (fibroma of the ovary, ovarian fibrothecoma) - management

FLAG: needs manual sourcing. DeVita's ovarian oncology chapter covers epithelial ovarian
cancer, malignant germ cell tumors, and the malignant sex cord-stromal tumors (granulosa
cell tumor, juvenile granulosa cell tumor, Sertoli-Leydig cell tumor), but does not carry a
dedicated section on the benign ovarian fibroma/fibrothecoma. The management below is
guideline-general and is labelled accordingly throughout; it should be checked by a
reviewer against a benign-gynaecologic-pathology source (e.g. WHO Classification of Female
Genital Tumours) before sign-off.

## Overall approach

Ovarian fibroma is a benign sex cord-stromal tumour, so there is no "stage" or "line of
therapy" in the oncologic sense; the entire management question is surgical: confirm the
mass is benign, remove it, and confirm the diagnosis on histology (general oncology
standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Treatment by clinical scenario

- Asymptomatic, imaging-typical solid adnexal mass in a woman past reproductive plans:
  oophorectomy (or salpingo-oophorectomy) is generally curative and avoids leaving residual
  stromal tissue that could later be biopsied unnecessarily (general oncology standard, not
  from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Younger woman wishing to preserve fertility, with an imaging appearance and intraoperative
  findings consistent with a benign fibroma: ovary-sparing tumourectomy/cystectomy is a
  reasonable alternative to oophorectomy, provided enough normal ovarian tissue remains and
  the excised specimen is sent for full histology (general oncology standard, not from
  DeVita's section on this disease).
- Acute presentation with torsion of a pedunculated fibroma: this is a surgical emergency
  managed by detorsion/excision, handled on the same urgent surgical pathway as any adnexal
  torsion rather than as an oncologic problem (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's
  section on this disease).
- Meigs syndrome (fibroma with ascites and a pleural effusion, usually right-sided): the
  correct management is excision of the fibroma, after which the ascites and effusion
  resolve; a large symptomatic effusion can be drained for symptomatic relief while surgery
  is arranged, but the effusion itself is not treated as malignant disease (general oncology
  standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Cellular fibroma or the mitotically active/borderline variant: managed with complete
  surgical excision as for typical fibroma, but flagged for closer histological review and
  clinical follow-up because of a small recognised risk of local recurrence (general
  oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Role of surgery, radiotherapy, and systemic therapy

- Surgery is the sole and definitive treatment. There is no established role for
  radiotherapy or systemic (cytotoxic, hormonal, or targeted) therapy in ovarian fibroma
  (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- This is worth stating explicitly because DeVita's chapter on ovarian sex cord-stromal
  tumours does describe adjuvant chemotherapy (a platinum/etoposide-based regimen) for
  malignant granulosa cell and juvenile granulosa cell tumours, and a gonadotropin-releasing
  hormone analogue for advanced Sertoli-Leydig cell tumours, but those are distinct
  malignant entities and that systemic therapy does not apply to the benign fibroma.

## Preoperative work-up (supports the surgical decision, not a "monitoring" step)

- Transvaginal ultrasound and pelvic MRI to characterise the mass as solid and fibrous
  (low T2 signal, acoustic shadowing) and support a benign impression before surgery.
- Tumour markers, including CA125 (which can be mildly elevated in Meigs syndrome despite
  benign disease), to help exclude a malignant process before committing to a surgical plan.
- Chest imaging if a pleural effusion is present, to characterise a suspected Meigs
  syndrome effusion.
(These work-up items are drawn from the KB's existing reference entry for this disease
rather than from a fresh DeVita citation.)

## Monitoring after surgery

- Once histology confirms an ordinary benign fibroma, no oncologic surveillance is required
  beyond routine gynaecologic follow-up, since the condition is cured by excision (general
  oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Cellular or mitotically active/borderline fibromas warrant a period of clinical follow-up
  given their small recurrence potential (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's
  section on this disease).
- Bilateral or multiple fibromas, particularly in a young patient, should prompt referral
  for genetic assessment for naevoid basal cell carcinoma (Gorlin) syndrome rather than
  repeat imaging surveillance of the ovary alone (general oncology standard, not from
  DeVita's section on this disease).

## When to refer

- Refer to gynaecologic oncology (rather than managing as routine benign gynaecology) when
  imaging shows a solid ovarian mass with ascites and an equivocal or rising CA125, since
  distinguishing Meigs syndrome from an ovarian malignancy with ascites is the key clinical
  risk in this disease.
- Refer for genetic counselling when fibromas are bilateral, multiple, or occur in a young
  patient, to evaluate for Gorlin syndrome.
- Refer urgently for surgery in suspected torsion of a pedunculated fibroma.

Sources: DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of Oncology, 12th ed.
