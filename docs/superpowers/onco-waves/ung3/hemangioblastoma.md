# Haemangioblastoma (VHL) - Management

## Overview and treatment intent

Haemangioblastoma is a benign, highly vascular tumour arising most often in the cerebellum, and also the brainstem and spinal cord. It occurs either sporadically (usually solitary) or as part of von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) disease, in which patients typically harbour multiple lesions across the neuraxis. Management intent is therefore split along two tracks: definitive cure of a solitary symptomatic lesion, versus long-term, tumour-by-tumour surveillance and selective treatment in patients who carry the VHL germline mutation and will keep forming new lesions over their lifetime.

An asymptomatic haemangioblastoma, particularly in a known VHL patient, does not automatically need to be resected or otherwise treated. Observation with surveillance imaging is appropriate unless the lesion shows active growth or becomes symptomatic from mass effect or hydrocephalus, because many VHL patients carry multiple tumours and treating every one is neither necessary nor practical.

## Surgery

Complete surgical resection of the mural tumour nodule is the primary and often curative treatment when a haemangioblastoma is symptomatic or enlarging.
- The lesion is very vascular; biopsy (open or stereotactic) of a suspected haemangioblastoma is generally avoided because of a high risk of haemorrhage.
- Resection is carried out en bloc, staying in the surrounding gliotic (peritumoural) plane and avoiding entry into the tumour itself, since violating the capsule can produce bleeding resembling that from an arteriovenous malformation.
- Preoperative embolisation is rarely safe, because these tumours often draw supply from distal segments of the normal intra-axial circulation rather than a discrete feeding pedicle.
- Cyst formation is common and often drives the mass effect more than the solid tumour does; the cyst wall itself is non-neoplastic reactive tissue and does not need to be excised, only drained, but the mural nodule must be completely removed or the cyst will recur.
- Cerebellar lesions are generally the most straightforward to resect with a low neurologic risk margin; brainstem lesions sit immediately adjacent to critical structures and carry materially higher risk of neurological deficit during dissection.
- Before surgery, patients with hypertension should be evaluated for phaeochromocytoma, since this is a recognised association in VHL disease and matters for anaesthetic and perioperative management.

## Radiation therapy

Radiotherapy, principally stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS), is used when surgery is not the right first move rather than as a routine adjunct after complete resection.
- Indications include surgically unresectable tumours, multifocal disease (typical of VHL), incompletely excised tumours needing adjuvant treatment, and salvage of recurrent disease.
- SRS has been used for residual or progressive lesions, progressive deep-seated tumours, and newly appearing tumours in VHL patients, and reported long-term local control is high (on the order of mid-90s percent at five years, somewhat lower by ten years).
- For tumours judged likely to progress radiologically or become symptomatic because of an eloquent location, earlier SRS should be considered rather than waiting for symptoms to develop.
- Highly conformal fractionated radiotherapy is reserved for situations where neither resection nor SRS is feasible, such as larger tumour volumes adjacent to eloquent structures (spinal cord, brainstem, optic pathways) or extensive intracranial or spinal disease.

## Systemic and targeted therapy

Haemangioblastoma stromal cells secrete VEGF, which has motivated interest in VEGF-receptor-targeted small-molecule inhibitors as medical therapy, particularly for VHL patients who routinely have multiple lesions unsuited to repeated local treatment. However, the extreme heterogeneity of tumour growth, with long periods of spontaneous stability interspersed with slow growth, makes it genuinely difficult to design trials that rigorously demonstrate efficacy of any systemic agent, and a small reported study of a VEGFR2-targeted tyrosine kinase inhibitor in VHL patients showed responses in coexisting renal cell carcinoma but no responses in the haemangioblastomas themselves. In short, systemic VEGF-pathway-directed therapy for haemangioblastoma should be regarded as unproven rather than a validated line of treatment.

A HIF-2-alpha inhibitor is used clinically for VHL-associated tumours (including haemangioblastoma) that are not amenable to local treatment (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease); this reflects that HIF stabilisation downstream of VHL loss is the pathway driving these tumours, but the specific efficacy data for this agent were not found in the DeVita section reviewed and should be corroborated against current guideline sources before being treated as first-line practice.

## Associated problems requiring management

- Corticosteroids and management of raised intracranial pressure or obstructive hydrocephalus should be treated as a neurosurgical emergency when a posterior fossa lesion or its cyst produces mass effect (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Secondary polycythaemia from tumour erythropoietin production should be addressed, with venesection where indicated (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Preoperative hypertension warrants evaluation for phaeochromocytoma given the VHL association.

## Monitoring and surveillance

- Solitary sporadic tumours that are completely resected have an excellent prognosis; ongoing neuraxis imaging surveillance is nonetheless reasonable given the recognised, if lower, potential for multifocal or recurrent disease.
- VHL patients require lifelong surveillance because they continue to develop new lesions in the cerebellum, brainstem, spinal cord and retina over time, with alternating periods of tumour growth and prolonged stability; the timing and choice of treatment for any given lesion should account for this variable natural history rather than reflex intervention at first detection.
- VHL patients should also be screened for the other tumours associated with the syndrome (retinal angiomatosis, polycystic kidneys, pancreatic cysts, phaeochromocytoma, renal cell carcinoma), with genetic testing of the VHL gene available with high accuracy for at-risk individuals or apparent sporadic cases (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease, for the specific surveillance imaging schedule).

## When to refer

- Refer to neurosurgery for any symptomatic or enlarging haemangioblastoma, and urgently for a rapidly enlarging posterior fossa cyst, obstructive hydrocephalus, or new brainstem signs.
- Refer to radiation oncology/neuro-oncology for SRS or fractionated radiotherapy when a lesion is unresectable, multifocal, incompletely excised, or recurrent.
- Refer for clinical genetics evaluation and family screening when disease is multiple, early-onset, or there is a family history suggesting von Hippel-Lindau disease.
- Refer for endocrine work-up (phaeochromocytoma) in any patient with hypertension and a known or suspected haemangioblastoma, particularly before surgery.
