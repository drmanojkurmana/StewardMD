## Overview

Follicular dendritic cell sarcoma is a rare neoplasm of the antigen-presenting stromal cells of lymphoid
follicles. It behaves as a low-to-intermediate-grade sarcoma rather than a lymphoma, so its management
follows soft-tissue sarcoma principles adapted to a rare, often nodal or extranodal, entity (general
oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease). Because it is uncommon, most treatment
recommendations are extrapolated from case series and general sarcoma practice rather than randomised
trials.

## Treatment approach by stage/intent

- **Localised, resectable disease (the majority of presentations):** complete surgical excision with
  clear margins is the primary and usually curative treatment (general oncology standard, not from
  DeVita's section on this disease). Wide local excision is preferred over simple enucleation where
  feasible, given the tumour's tendency to local recurrence.
- **Incompletely excised or high-risk localised disease:** adjuvant radiotherapy is considered after
  positive or close margins, or when there are high-risk histological features such as large size, deep
  location, high mitotic rate, or necrosis (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this
  disease). Radiotherapy can also be used for unresectable but localised/locoregional disease with
  curative or durable local-control intent.
- **Unresectable, recurrent, high-grade, or metastatic disease:** systemic therapy is reserved for this
  setting. There is no established standard regimen specific to FDCS; treatment generally borrows from
  soft-tissue sarcoma systemic therapy principles (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on
  this disease). Drug classes used in this context, without any specific dose or schedule endorsed here,
  include anthracycline-based regimens and platinum-based combinations, as used more broadly in soft
  tissue sarcoma; the evidence for FDCS specifically is limited to case reports and small series.
- **Special variant — EBV-associated inflammatory pseudotumour-like FDCS (often hepatic or splenic):**
  this variant tends to be more indolent and is managed within a specialist pathway; surgical resection
  remains the mainstay where feasible, with systemic therapy considered only for progressive or
  unresectable disease (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- **Coexisting hyaline-vascular Castleman disease:** when present, the associated Castleman disease
  should be identified and addressed as part of the overall treatment plan, since it may represent a
  precursor or coexisting process (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Lines of therapy

There is no established sequential first-line/second-line systemic therapy paradigm specific to FDCS in
the literature reviewed here. In practice, systemic treatment is used only after surgery and radiotherapy
options have been exhausted or are not feasible, and regimen choice is individualised by a sarcoma or
haemato-oncology multidisciplinary team rather than following a fixed line-of-therapy sequence (general
oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease). Targeted or biologic agents have not been
established as a standard line of therapy for this entity based on the material reviewed.

## Role of surgery, radiotherapy, and systemic therapy

- **Surgery** is the primary and most important modality: complete excision with clear margins offers the
  best chance of cure.
- **Radiotherapy** is an adjunct for margin-positive disease, high-risk histology, or as definitive
  local treatment when surgery is not possible.
- **Systemic therapy** plays a limited, salvage-oriented role, reserved for unresectable, recurrent,
  high-grade, or metastatic disease, given the lack of a validated standard regimen for this rare entity
  (all general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Monitoring

- Prolonged surveillance with cross-sectional imaging is warranted given the recognised potential for
  late local recurrence and distant metastasis, particularly to lung and liver.
- Follow-up should continue for an extended period beyond the immediate post-treatment window because
  recurrence can occur late, in keeping with the tumour's variable, sometimes indolent but occasionally
  aggressive, biological behaviour (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## When to refer

- All suspected or confirmed cases should be referred to a specialist centre with sarcoma and
  haemato-oncology multidisciplinary team expertise, given the rarity of the diagnosis and the absence of
  standardised trial-based treatment protocols.
- Refer urgently for large, rapidly enlarging, deeply sited (for example intra-abdominal), or
  high-grade-appearing tumours, and for any case with suspected or confirmed metastasis or local
  recurrence after prior excision (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
