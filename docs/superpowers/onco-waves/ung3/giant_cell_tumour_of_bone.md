# Giant Cell Tumour of Bone (GCTB) - Management Expansion

## Overview of treatment intent

Giant cell tumour of bone is classified as a benign but locally aggressive primary bone neoplasm. Management is therefore staged around local control first, with systemic (RANKL-targeted) therapy reserved for disease that cannot be controlled by surgery alone (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease). DeVita's section on approved targeted antibody agents specifically discusses denosumab in this disease and is the primary grounding for the systemic-therapy component of this note.

## Surgery: the primary modality

For resectable disease, surgical removal of the lesion is the mainstay of treatment (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease). Decision-making around the extent of surgery (intralesional curettage with adjuvant and cavity filling versus wider en-bloc resection) is standard orthopaedic-oncology practice but was not the material found in the DeVita excerpts reviewed for this note, so it is not presented as DeVita-sourced here.

- Surgery remains first-line where the tumour is resectable with acceptable functional morbidity (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Denosumab, per DeVita, is FDA approved specifically for two surgical scenarios: tumours that are surgically unsalvageable, and tumours where resection is anticipated to result in severe morbidity. This frames denosumab as an adjunct to surgical decision-making rather than a universal first-line agent.

## Systemic therapy: denosumab (RANKL inhibition)

DeVita describes denosumab (Xgeva) as a fully human IgG2 monoclonal antibody that neutralises RANK ligand (RANKL). Within its discussion of GCTB, DeVita states:

- Denosumab is FDA approved for adults and skeletally mature adolescents whose giant cell tumour of bone is surgically unsalvageable, or where resection would be expected to cause severe morbidity.
- Approval rested in part on open-label phase II trial data using subcutaneous administration; of 187 patients treated, 47 (25 percent) achieved an objective partial response by modified RECIST criteria.
- More recent experience raises a specific safety and durability concern that DeVita flags explicitly: stopping denosumab has been associated with a higher rate of subsequent local recurrence.
- DeVita notes that questions remain open around the long-term safety, the optimal maintenance dosing frequency, and the overall therapeutic strategy for denosumab in this disease, i.e. the field has not settled these questions.

No specific drug dose, interval, or loading schedule is reproduced here; that information belongs only in structured, access-gated protocol templates elsewhere in the KB.

## When systemic therapy is used relative to surgery

Based on the DeVita-grounded approval indications above, denosumab has two broad roles: as definitive treatment when surgery is not feasible (unresectable or axial/sacral disease), and pre-operatively to downstage a tumour so a more limited surgical procedure can be attempted (the latter reflects general orthopaedic-oncology practice and is not itself detailed in the DeVita excerpt, so it is marked as general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Monitoring

- Radiographic follow-up for local recurrence is standard after definitive local treatment, with particular vigilance at sites known for higher recurrence risk (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Given the DeVita-noted association between denosumab discontinuation and recurrence, patients who stop denosumab warrant closer surveillance for regrowth during and after the cessation period.
- Screening for pulmonary deposits and surveillance for rare malignant transformation are part of standard longitudinal follow-up (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease); DeVita's own tumour classification list separately recognises "giant cell tumour of bone, malignant" as a distinct, more aggressive entity, supporting that a transformation risk is an accepted part of the disease's natural history.

## When to refer

- Any new, suspected, or biopsy-proven giant cell tumour of bone should be referred to an orthopaedic oncology multidisciplinary team before definitive treatment planning (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Tumours that are axial, sacral, or otherwise surgically unsalvageable, or where resection carries a high risk of severe functional morbidity, should prompt consideration of denosumab, per the DeVita-described approval criteria, and this decision should be made within a specialist multidisciplinary setting.
- New or progressive pain, radiographic change after prior radiotherapy, or lesions with atypical or aggressive features should raise concern for malignant transformation and warrant urgent oncology re-referral.

Sources: DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of Oncology, 12th ed.
