# Hypercalcaemia of malignancy — management narrative

## Context and mechanism
Hypercalcaemia of malignancy is a common paraneoplastic and metabolic complication rather than a discrete tumor type, so its treatment approach is staged by acuity and by the underlying malignancy rather than by tumor "stage" in the usual sense. In lung cancer, hypercalcaemia is described as occurring in roughly 10 to 25 percent of patients, most often with advanced squamous cell lung cancer, driven either by extensive bone metastatic involvement or by tumor production of parathyroid hormone-related protein. Presenting symptoms include polyuria, polydipsia, nausea, and dehydration, with severe hypercalcaemia producing mental status change, hypotension, and renal failure.

## Immediate (acute) management
The first priority in a patient with symptomatic hypercalcaemia of malignancy is correction of volume depletion, since polyuria-driven dehydration perpetuates the hypercalcaemia in a self-reinforcing cycle. Restoring intravascular volume improves renal calcium clearance and is the necessary first step before or alongside any calcium-lowering agent. In patients with renal dysfunction or a significant risk of fluid overload, haemodialysis is used instead of aggressive volume repletion.

For rapid initial control of severe, symptomatic hypercalcaemia, calcitonin combined with glucocorticoids can be used, but this combination has only a limited effect after a few days because of tachyphylaxis, so it functions as a short-term bridge rather than a durable treatment.

## Sustained control: systemic agents
- Intravenous bisphosphonates (agents such as pamidronate and zoledronate, named here only as drug classes/agents and without dosing) produce a good short-term calcium response, but patients can become refractory to them over time.
- Cinacalcet, a calcimimetic that acts on the calcium-sensing receptor to reduce parathyroid hormone synthesis and secretion, is the preferred agent specifically for hypercalcaemia associated with inoperable parathyroid carcinoma, where it reduced hypercalcaemia in roughly two-thirds of patients in one multicenter series; it is not a standard intervening step for the more common osteolytic or PTHrP-mediated forms of hypercalcaemia of malignancy, where bisphosphonate or denosumab therapy remains first-line.
- Denosumab, a RANK-ligand inhibitor, is an option when hypercalcaemia is refractory to bisphosphonate therapy, and is also useful when renal impairment limits bisphosphonate use; in parathyroid-carcinoma-associated hypercalcaemia refractory to cinacalcet, denosumab is likewise an option.
- Other older cytotoxic-type calcium-lowering agents (mithramycin/plicamycin, gallium nitrate) have limited practical use because of toxicity.
- For hypercalcaemia mediated by tumor conversion of vitamin D to its active form (described in the lymphoma/granulomatous-disease context), corticosteroids are the mechanism-specific treatment (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease specifically, but consistent with the vitamin-D-mediated mechanism referenced in the reviewed material).

## Role of surgery, radiotherapy, and definitive anticancer therapy
Durable control of hypercalcaemia of malignancy ultimately depends on treating the underlying tumor, since the metabolic disturbance recurs if the cancer is not controlled (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease). Where the hypercalcaemia is related to lytic bone metastases (as described in the breast cancer context), bone-targeted therapy with an intravenous bisphosphonate or denosumab reduces pain and the frequency of skeletal complications, including hypercalcaemia; external-beam radiotherapy to symptomatic bone lesions, and surgical stabilization for pending or completed pathologic fracture, are used for local control of individual sites of bone disease. When hypercalcaemia arises from a resectable or oligometastatic source of PTH/PTHrP production (for example isolated distant metastases from parathyroid carcinoma), resection of the causative lesion can improve both symptoms and calcium levels in a substantial proportion of patients, whereas patients with disseminated disease are less likely to benefit from surgery and are managed with the medical options above.

## Monitoring
Extended courses of bisphosphonate therapy are associated with osteonecrosis of the jaw, so patients on prolonged bone-targeted therapy should be monitored for atypical oral lesions. In malignancies where PTH/PTH-related peptide drives the hypercalcaemia, biochemical monitoring of calcium (and PTH where relevant) is used to detect persistent or recurrent disease, with imaging escalation if biochemical abnormalities recur. More generally, patients being treated for hypercalcaemia of malignancy should have corrected (albumin-adjusted) or ionised calcium, renal function, and cardiac rhythm/QT interval followed, since both severe hypercalcaemia and its treatment (dehydration, renal impairment) affect these parameters (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## When to refer
Patients with severe or refractory hypercalcaemia, evidence of renal impairment or failure, cardiac arrhythmia, or altered mental status warrant urgent oncology involvement, and patients with recurrent or drug-refractory hypercalcaemia despite bisphosphonate and calcimimetic therapy should be referred for consideration of denosumab or other second-line options and for reassessment of the underlying tumor's disease control. Given the metabolic and prognostic seriousness of this syndrome, palliative care involvement is appropriate in patients with advanced, poorly-controlled malignancy in whom hypercalcaemia is recurrent (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
