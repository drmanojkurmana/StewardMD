## Treatment approach by presentation and risk

Unicameral (simple) bone cyst is a benign, fluid-filled cavity of bone that most often
affects the proximal humerus and proximal femur in children and adolescents (general
oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease). Management is stratified by
symptoms, cyst activity, and fracture risk rather than by a "stage" in the malignant-tumor
sense (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

- Asymptomatic, latent cysts at low fracture risk: observation with periodic radiographic
  follow-up is reasonable, since many lesions heal or ossify spontaneously as the skeleton
  matures (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Symptomatic, enlarging, or "active" cysts abutting the growth plate: intervention is
  favored to reduce fracture risk (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on
  this disease).
- Cysts presenting with a pathological fracture: the fracture is generally allowed to unite
  first, with definitive cyst treatment planned before or alongside fracture care (general
  oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Lesions with atypical or aggressive-appearing margins, rapid enlargement, or an associated
  soft-tissue mass warrant biopsy or aspiration to exclude a mimic such as aneurysmal bone
  cyst, fibrous dysplasia, or a malignant process before proceeding with cyst-directed
  treatment (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Lines of therapy / role of surgery, "systemic therapy", and monitoring

There is no cytotoxic systemic therapy or radiotherapy role in unicameral bone cyst; this is
a benign process managed with observation and local, intralesional or surgical measures
(general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

- First-line for symptomatic or at-risk lesions: minimally invasive intralesional treatment,
  such as corticosteroid injection, autologous bone marrow aspirate injection, or cyst
  decompression (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- For lesions refractory to intralesional measures, or at high risk of recurrent fracture:
  curettage with bone grafting is used (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section
  on this disease).
- Recurrence after minimally invasive treatment is not uncommon, and repeat procedures are
  sometimes needed before durable healing; this should be discussed with families as part of
  informed consent (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Monitoring is with serial plain radiographs to track cyst activity, cortical thickening,
  and fracture risk, with imaging frequency guided by lesion activity and treatment response
  (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease). MRI or CT is
  reserved for atypical presentations where fluid content needs confirmation or aggressive
  features need to be excluded (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this
  disease).

## When to refer

- Any lesion with atypical, aggressive, or permeative margins, an associated soft-tissue
  mass, or an unusual location outside the typical metaphyseal long-bone distribution should
  be referred for orthopaedic oncology assessment, with biopsy or aspiration to confirm the
  diagnosis and exclude a mimic (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this
  disease).
- Impending or established pathological fracture in a weight-bearing bone, or a large cyst
  with significant cortical thinning, should prompt referral for surgical planning rather
  than continued observation alone (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on
  this disease).
- Cysts that recur repeatedly after intralesional treatment, or that fail to heal, warrant
  referral for consideration of curettage and bone grafting (general oncology standard, not
  from DeVita's section on this disease).
