# Serrated polyposis syndrome (SPS) - Management

Note on sourcing: DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of Oncology does not
carry a dedicated chapter or section on serrated polyposis syndrome. A search of the text located only
incidental mentions: a diagnostic-criteria line (five or more serrated polyps/lesions proximal to the
rectum as one indication for hereditary cancer genetic counseling) and a few lines in the colorectal
cancer molecular genetics discussion describing the serrated neoplasia pathway (BRAF mutation, sessile
serrated adenomas, right-sided microsatellite-instability-high tumours). There is no DeVita passage that
lays out a management algorithm for SPS itself. Everything below that goes beyond those specific points
is therefore marked as general oncology standard rather than DeVita-sourced, and this content should be
flagged for manual clinician sourcing/review before it is treated as fully authoritative.

## Overview

SPS is not treated with cytotoxic or targeted systemic therapy in the way most cancers are. It is a
polyp-burden and cancer-risk management problem: the "treatment" is endoscopic surveillance and
clearance of serrated lesions, with surgery reserved for cases where that fails or cancer is already
present (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Treatment approach by intent

**Risk-reduction / no cancer yet (the great majority of patients):**
- Regular colonoscopic surveillance is the mainstay, with the explicit goal of complete endoscopic
  clearance of significant serrated polyps at each exam. This is grounded in DeVita's description of
  the serrated neoplasia pathway as a distinct route to colorectal cancer that is driven by
  accumulating, often subtle, serrated lesions rather than classic adenomas.
- Because sessile serrated lesions are flat, pale, and concentrated in the proximal colon, high-quality
  colonoscopy (adequate bowel preparation, longer withdrawal time, image-enhancement/chromoendoscopy
  techniques) is emphasized so lesions are not missed (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's
  section on this disease).
- Surveillance interval is individualized to polyp number, size, and completeness of prior clearance;
  no single fixed interval is specified in the DeVita text located, so exact timing should follow
  current society/NCCN colorectal surveillance guidance rather than being asserted here.

**Established or suspected colorectal cancer arising in SPS:**
- If a discrete mass, advanced histology, or an endoscopically uncontrollable lesion is found, work-up
  and staging proceed as for sporadic colorectal cancer, with tumour mismatch-repair/microsatellite
  status assessed given the syndrome's association with acquired mismatch-repair deficiency arising
  through BRAF mutation and CpG-island promoter methylation, a mechanism described in DeVita's colorectal
  cancer molecular genetics discussion.
- Systemic treatment for an invasive cancer follows standard colorectal cancer therapy lines
  (fluoropyrimidine-based chemotherapy, with biologic or immune checkpoint therapy selection guided by
  RAS/BRAF and mismatch-repair/microsatellite-instability status) rather than any SPS-specific regimen;
  this is general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease, since no SPS-specific
  systemic therapy exists.

## Role of surgery

- Colectomy (or a more limited resection where feasible) is reserved for patients whose polyp burden
  cannot be controlled endoscopically, or once malignancy has developed, rather than being offered
  prophylactically to all patients as in some other polyposis syndromes (general oncology standard, not
  from DeVita's section on this disease, extrapolated from the broader polyposis-surgery principle;
  DeVita's own SPS-specific mentions do not detail surgical indications).
- Where surgery is undertaken for cancer, standard oncologic resection and lymphadenectomy principles for
  colorectal cancer apply (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Role of radiotherapy

- Radiotherapy has no defined role in SPS management itself; if used, it would only be in the context of
  an established rectal cancer arising in an SPS patient, following standard rectal cancer treatment
  principles (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease). DeVita's located
  passages do not address radiotherapy for this condition.

## Lines of therapy

- There is no concept of "first-line" versus "second-line" therapy for SPS itself; surveillance and
  polypectomy are repeated indefinitely. Lines of therapy only become relevant if invasive colorectal
  cancer develops, at which point standard colorectal cancer treatment sequencing applies (general
  oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Monitoring

- Ongoing colonoscopic surveillance with complete clearance of significant serrated lesions is lifelong,
  since risk persists as long as the colon remains and depends on the completeness of each examination.
- Histopathological classification of resected lesions (distinguishing sessile serrated lesions from
  traditional serrated adenomas) and mismatch-repair immunohistochemistry on any carcinoma found should be
  performed to detect acquired deficiency.
- First-degree relatives carry an increased colorectal cancer risk and warrant colonoscopic screening
  starting at an earlier age than average-risk individuals.
- Molecular testing of any tumour for BRAF mutation and CpG-island methylation can support the diagnosis
  of serrated-pathway carcinogenesis when relevant.

## When to refer

- Refer for germline multigene panel testing when clinical features raise suspicion for an alternative
  hereditary syndrome (for example familial adenomatous polyposis and its attenuated form, MUTYH-associated
  polyposis, Lynch syndrome, hereditary mixed polyposis syndrome, or juvenile polyposis syndrome), since
  most SPS cases lack an identified germline mutation and are defined clinically by polyp count and
  distribution rather than a single causative gene.
- Refer to colorectal surgery when polyp burden becomes endoscopically uncontrollable, when a large or
  complex serrated lesion is suspicious for malignancy, or once cancer is confirmed.
- Refer first-degree relatives for earlier, more frequent colonoscopic screening given their elevated risk.
- Advise on general lifestyle risk reduction, including smoking cessation, as an adjunct to surveillance.
