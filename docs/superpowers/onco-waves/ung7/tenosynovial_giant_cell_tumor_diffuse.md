# Diffuse-type tenosynovial giant cell tumour - Management

Aliases: pigmented villonodular synovitis (PVNS), diffuse-type TGCT, CSF1-rearranged giant cell tumour of tendon sheath.

## Overview of treatment intent

Diffuse-type TGCT is a locally aggressive but benign proliferation of synovium driven by a CSF1 gene translocation, most often a COL6A3-CSF1 fusion, that raises CSF1 levels and modulates the inflammatory infiltrate; the fusion is thought to be confined to a minority of tumour cells, with the bulk of the mass made up of recruited, non-neoplastic CSF1-receptor-bearing macrophages (general TGCT biology, not from DeVita's section on this disease). Unlike the nodular (localised) form, the diffuse form has infiltrative borders, most commonly affects large joints such as the knee, wrist, and foot or the surrounding soft tissue, and can be intra-articular. Because of this infiltrative growth, disease that would be curable by simple excision in the localised form instead behaves as a chronic, recurrence-prone condition needing a combined surgical and, when needed, systemic strategy.

## Surgery: the mainstay

- Surgery is the mainstay of treatment for TGCT overall, including the diffuse form.
- The recurrence rate after surgery is substantially higher for diffuse-type tumours (about 18% to 50%) than for nodular tumours (about 10% to 20%), reflecting the infiltrative growth pattern of the diffuse form.
- In practice, diffuse disease is approached with as complete a synovectomy as possible, often combining open and arthroscopic techniques, and sometimes both anterior and posterior approaches in joints such as the knee, to clear disease from recesses that are hard to reach through a single approach (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- End-stage joint destruction from long-standing or repeatedly recurrent disease may ultimately need arthroplasty (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Role of radiotherapy

- Adjuvant radiation is effective at reducing local recurrence, with rates of local recurrence under 10% in the cited experience, but it is rarely used in practice because of the risk of joint fibrosis and secondary malignancies.
- Because of this risk-benefit tradeoff, radiotherapy is generally reserved for cases with recurrent or incompletely resectable disease where the benefit is judged to outweigh the long-term joint and malignancy risk, rather than used routinely after every synovectomy (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Systemic (CSF1R-targeted) therapy

- For recurrent or unresectable disease, CSF1R-receptor-targeted therapy is the systemic option, since the biology of TGCT is driven by CSF1 overexpression recruiting CSF1-receptor-bearing macrophages.
- Imatinib, an inhibitor of the CSF1 receptor, produces disease stabilisation in approximately 75% of patients, but appears less effective in the (rare) diffuse-type tumours that carry a malignant component.
- More potent CSF1R inhibitors, such as pexidartinib, have shown significant activity in diffuse-type TGCT; a phase III trial of pexidartinib versus placebo showed a response rate of 39%. Because few patients with metastatic disease were included in that trial, the relative benefit of pexidartinib specifically in the metastatic setting remains unclear.
- Liver function monitoring is a standard precaution when using a CSF1R inhibitor, given the hepatotoxicity profile of this drug class (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- If a malignant component is identified (malignant TGCT with increased mitotic count and nuclear dysmorphism), localised malignant disease should instead be managed as a high-grade sarcoma, with complete surgical resection remaining the backbone of treatment; malignant TGCT can also metastasise to lung or lymph nodes.

## Monitoring

- Long-term surveillance, typically with MRI, is appropriate given the high and well-documented recurrence rate of diffuse-type disease after surgery (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease; the underlying high recurrence rate itself is DeVita-grounded).
- Liver function should be monitored during CSF1R-inhibitor therapy (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Physiotherapy to restore range of motion and function after synovectomy is standard supportive care (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## When to refer

- Refer to an orthopaedic oncology or sarcoma multidisciplinary team for surgical planning, given the frequent need for combined arthroscopic and open approaches and the difficulty of achieving complete clearance in diffuse disease (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Refer for consideration of CSF1R-targeted systemic therapy when disease is recurrent, unresectable, or extensive.
- If histology raises concern for a malignant component (increased mitotic count, nuclear dysmorphism, or metastasis to lung or lymph nodes), refer for management as a high-grade sarcoma rather than continuing to treat as benign diffuse-type TGCT.

Sources: DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of Oncology, 12th ed.
