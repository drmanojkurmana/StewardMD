## Overview

SCCOHT is a rare, aggressive ovarian malignancy of young women, driven by loss of SMARCA4 (BRG1), and roughly two-thirds of patients present with paraneoplastic hypercalcaemia (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease). Because of its rarity and aggressive behaviour, care is best coordinated by a gynaecological oncology multidisciplinary team from diagnosis onward (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Treatment approach by stage/intent

- **Early-stage, surgically resectable disease (confined to the ovary/pelvis):** primary cytoreductive surgery with formal staging (bilateral assessment, omentum, peritoneal sampling, lymph nodes) followed by adjuvant multi-agent platinum-based chemotherapy is the standard curative-intent approach (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- **Advanced or bulky disease (extra-ovarian spread at presentation):** neoadjuvant chemotherapy is often used to permit subsequent interval cytoreductive surgery, followed by further adjuvant chemotherapy; radiotherapy to the pelvis or sites of residual/high-risk disease may be added given the tumour's aggressive, high-grade behaviour (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- **Consolidation in high-risk or responding patients:** high-dose chemotherapy with autologous stem cell rescue has been used as consolidation after initial response, reflecting the tumour's chemosensitivity but high relapse rate (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- **Relapsed/refractory disease:** treatment is individualised, often within a clinical trial, and may draw on regimens used in other high-grade small round cell or rhabdoid-type tumours; SMARCA4/SWI-SNF-directed investigational agents (e.g. EZH2 inhibitors, exploiting the same synthetic-lethality rationale used in other SMARC-deficient tumours) are being explored in specialist or trial settings (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Lines of therapy

- First line: primary surgery plus adjuvant platinum-based combination chemotherapy (regimen selection and dosing belong in structured protocol templates, not here).
- Consideration of dose-intensified or high-dose chemotherapy with stem cell rescue as consolidation in appropriately fit patients after response to first-line therapy.
- Subsequent lines at relapse are not standardised given rarity; enrolment in a clinical trial should be actively sought, and salvage regimens are chosen case-by-case by the treating oncology team (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Role of surgery, radiotherapy, and systemic therapy

- **Surgery** is the cornerstone wherever resectable, both for primary treatment and for accurate staging that guides adjuvant therapy.
- **Systemic chemotherapy** (multi-agent, platinum-based) is used in essentially all patients given the tumour's aggressiveness, either adjuvantly after surgery or neoadjuvantly before it.
- **Radiotherapy** may be considered for high-grade disease with residual or high-risk features, as an adjunct to surgery and chemotherapy rather than as sole therapy.
- **Hypercalcaemia management** runs in parallel with oncological treatment: intravenous rehydration and bone-resorption inhibitors (bisphosphonates, calcitonin) are used for paraneoplastic hypercalcaemia, which typically resolves as the tumour burden is controlled.
- **Supportive oncological care** (antiemetics, nutritional support, management of treatment-related toxicity) is integral throughout systemic therapy.

## Monitoring

- Surveillance for relapse relies on periodic cross-sectional imaging and serial serum corrected calcium, given both the high recurrence rate and calcium's utility as a paraneoplastic marker of tumour activity.
- Renal function and cardiac status (electrocardiography) should be monitored in patients presenting with or recovering from significant hypercalcaemia.
- Because a subset of patients carry germline SMARCA4 variants linked to an inherited rhabdoid tumour predisposition, genetic counselling and testing (with cascade testing of at-risk relatives) should be arranged and revisited over follow-up.

## When to refer

- Any suspected or confirmed case should be referred promptly to a gynaecological oncology multidisciplinary team, given the rarity, aggressiveness, and need for coordinated surgical and systemic management.
- Refer for medical oncology input for chemotherapy planning (including consideration of high-dose/stem-cell-supported approaches) and for clinical trial availability, particularly at relapse.
- Refer for clinical genetics assessment given the association with germline SMARCA4 variants and rhabdoid tumour predisposition.
- Involve palliative care early for symptom control and supportive decision-making in advanced or relapsed disease.
