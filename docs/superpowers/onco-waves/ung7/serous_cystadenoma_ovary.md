# Ovarian serous cystadenoma - management

## Note on grounding

DeVita's dedicated chapter for this area is "Ovarian Cancer," which focuses on epithelial ovarian
carcinoma. Serous cystadenoma is mentioned within that chapter only as the benign counterpart of
serous carcinoma and within the adnexal mass work-up, not as a disease with its own dedicated
management section. The passages below are the DeVita-grounded material that is directly relevant
(the benign/borderline/malignant spectrum and the adnexal mass evaluation and referral framework);
the specific surgical management steps for a benign cyst are marked as general standard rather than
DeVita-sourced, per the sourcing rule.

## Nature of the disease and why "management" here means triage, not oncologic treatment

DeVita's classification of ovarian epithelial tumours explicitly separates a spectrum of behaviour
for serous tumours: benign lesions (serous cystadenoma), tumours of low malignant potential
(borderline), and frank serous carcinoma. Because serous cystadenoma sits at the benign end of this
spectrum, its management is not staged, systemic-therapy-driven oncologic treatment; it is confident
benign diagnosis, appropriate observation or surgical removal, and exclusion of a borderline or
malignant look-alike. There is no "line of therapy," chemotherapy, or radiotherapy role for this
diagnosis (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Diagnostic framework that determines management (DeVita-grounded)

DeVita lays out the adnexal mass evaluation that determines whether a lesion can be managed as
presumed-benign or needs surgical/oncologic referral:

- Transvaginal ultrasonography is the primary tool to determine whether a mass is solid or cystic,
  and if cystic, whether it is simple or complex (septations, excrescences, solid components).
- Features raising concern for malignancy rather than a simple benign cystadenoma include bilateral
  disease, complex masses with solid areas, thick septations or mural nodules, a persistent or
  enlarging complex mass in a premenopausal patient, a complex mass of any size in a postmenopausal
  patient, a mass with elevated tumour markers, or a symptomatic mass.
- CA125 is useful in this context as one of the accepted uses DeVita lists: helping determine whether
  a pelvic mass is malignant. However, CA125 is expressed in only about 75% of epithelial ovarian
  cancers (principally the serous subtype) and can be elevated in benign gynaecologic and
  non-gynaecologic conditions, so it is not by itself diagnostic and a normal level does not exclude
  malignancy.
- DeVita cites the ACOG/SGO referral guidance: a premenopausal woman with a suspicious pelvic mass
  should be referred to a gynaecologic oncologist if there is a CA125 level greater than 200 U/mL,
  ascites, abdominal or distant metastases, or a first-degree relative with breast or ovarian cancer.
  A postmenopausal woman with a pelvic mass should be referred for any CA125 elevation, ascites,
  nodularity or limited mobility, evidence of metastasis, or a first-degree relative with breast or
  ovarian cancer.
- Adjunct multivariate serum tests referenced by DeVita for triage of an adnexal mass (not for
  screening) include OVA1, which combines several biomarkers to help identify whether a mass needs
  surgical referral, and ROMA, which combines CA125 with HE4.

## Practical management approach by presentation

**Asymptomatic, sonographically simple, unilocular thin-walled cyst with clear fluid, normal tumour
markers, no concerning ACOG/SGO referral criteria:** managed conservatively, typically with interval
ultrasound surveillance rather than immediate surgery (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's
section on this disease).

**Mass meeting any ACOG/SGO referral criterion above (elevated CA125, ascites, nodularity/limited
mobility, evidence of metastasis, concerning family history), or a complex mass on imaging:** refer to
a gynaecologic oncologist before proceeding, since this pattern is what DeVita's referral framework is
designed to trigger, so that a borderline or malignant lesion is not managed as a simple cystadenoma.

**Large, symptomatic, or diagnostically indeterminate cyst without high-risk features:** surgical
removal (cystectomy or oophorectomy, generally by a minimally invasive approach when feasible) is the
standard route to both relieve symptoms and obtain definitive histology (general oncology standard,
not from DeVita's section on this disease); DeVita does not describe a surgical technique for benign
cystadenoma specifically.

**Acute presentation with severe pain:** evaluate urgently for torsion or rupture, which are surgical
emergencies (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease); DeVita does not
detail management of torsion in this chapter.

## Role of surgery, radiotherapy, and systemic therapy

- Surgery (cystectomy or oophorectomy, fertility-sparing where possible) is the only definitive
  treatment when removal is indicated, and it is also how the diagnosis is confirmed histologically
  and a borderline or malignant counterpart excluded (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's
  section on this disease).
- Radiotherapy has no role in benign serous cystadenoma.
- Systemic/cytotoxic therapy has no role in benign serous cystadenoma; DeVita's discussion of
  chemotherapy in this chapter applies to invasive epithelial ovarian carcinoma, not to the benign
  cystadenoma counterpart.

## Monitoring

- For lesions managed non-operatively, serial ultrasound to confirm stability in size and
  morphology, watching specifically for the complex features (solid components, thickening
  septations, growth) that DeVita flags as concerning for malignancy (general oncology standard for
  the surveillance interval itself, though the concerning features are DeVita-grounded above).
- After surgical removal, histologic confirmation of a benign serous lining without atypia or
  invasion is the endpoint; DeVita notes that in this classification, absence of epithelial
  proliferation, atypia, and stromal invasion is what separates a benign cystadenoma from a
  borderline tumour or carcinoma.
- No tumour marker or imaging surveillance program analogous to post-cancer follow-up is indicated
  once a benign cystadenoma is excised and confirmed, since the entity does not recur or spread in the
  way a carcinoma does (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## When to refer

- Refer to a gynaecologic oncologist whenever the adnexal mass meets the ACOG/SGO criteria above
  (CA125 elevation, ascites, nodularity/fixation, metastasis, or concerning family history), since
  these are the exact triggers DeVita describes for escalation beyond general gynaecology.
- Refer for surgical planning (gynaecology or gynaecologic oncology depending on risk stratification)
  when a cyst is large, persistent, enlarging, or symptomatic despite reassuring markers and imaging.
- In a woman with a strong family history of breast or ovarian cancer or a known BRCA1/2 or Lynch
  syndrome mutation, DeVita's discussion of risk-reducing salpingo-oophorectomy and the elevated rate
  of occult serous carcinoma found at prophylactic surgery in mutation carriers means any adnexal
  finding in that population should prompt genetics/gynaecologic-oncology involvement rather than
  routine community management (general oncology standard for extending this to an incidental
  cystadenoma finding specifically, though the underlying risk data are DeVita-grounded).

## What DeVita does not address for this disease

DeVita's chapter does not give a dedicated management protocol, surgical technique comparison, or
surveillance interval specifically for serous cystadenoma; those points above are marked as general
oncology standard rather than attributed to DeVita, and no drug regimen, trial, or statistic is
invented for this benign entity.

Sources: DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of Oncology, 12th ed.
