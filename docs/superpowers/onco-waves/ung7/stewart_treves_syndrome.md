# Stewart-Treves Syndrome (Lymphedema-Associated Angiosarcoma) - Management

Stewart-Treves syndrome is angiosarcoma arising in a chronically lymphedematous limb, classically the arm after mastectomy and axillary dissection for breast cancer, though it is also described after axillary dissection for melanoma and in congenital, filarial, or other chronic lymphedema. It is one of the four recognized clinical variants of cutaneous angiosarcoma (the others being head-and-neck/idiopathic, radiation-induced, and epithelioid), and all four share an aggressive course and poor outcome. Because of the tendency to multifocality and early hematogenous spread, a thorough imaging workup for occult regional and distant disease is indicated at presentation in every patient.

## Local and locoregional disease

- Wide surgical excision is the treatment of choice when the disease is resectable. Complete excision, when feasible, is associated with lower recurrence, but even histologically negative margins do not reliably prevent local recurrence because of the tumor's tendency toward multifocality within the lymphedematous field.
- Because lymphedema-associated angiosarcoma extends well beyond what is clinically visible and surgical margins are hard to control in an already lymphedematous, poorly healing limb, amputation (including shoulder disarticulation for the arm, or hemipelvectomy for lower-limb disease) is recommended when the tumor involves an extremity and cannot be adequately controlled with limb-sparing excision.
- Postoperative radiotherapy should be considered in essentially all resected cases to improve local control; retrospective data suggest improved survival with adjuvant radiotherapy after surgery.
- Poor wound healing in the chronically lymphedematous limb is a recognized barrier to surgery and a reason limb-sparing resection is often not achievable.

## Systemic therapy

- Angiosarcoma, including the lymphedema-associated variant, is relatively chemosensitive, at least initially, to anthracycline-based regimens and to taxanes.
- Patients with isolated lymphatic spread who are treated with a taxane (paclitaxel)-based regimen have been reported to have a favorable response.
- In the metastatic setting, both chemotherapy and radical radiotherapy are used with palliative intent; neither has been shown to improve overall survival in this disease.
- (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease) Antiangiogenic or targeted approaches directed at VEGF-pathway signaling have biologic rationale given the vascular endothelial origin of angiosarcoma and reported alterations in genes such as KDR, FLT4, and MYC in some angiosarcomas, but DeVita's discussion of these findings is presented as molecular background rather than as an established treatment recommendation for Stewart-Treves syndrome specifically, so this should be regarded as an area for specialist-directed, trial-informed therapy rather than a defined line of treatment.

## Sequencing / intent of treatment

- For localized, resectable disease: surgery (wide excision or amputation as dictated by extent and limb salvageability) followed by adjuvant radiotherapy.
- For disease not amenable to complete resection, or for metastatic disease: systemic therapy (anthracycline-based or taxane-based) with palliative intent, with radiotherapy as an additional palliative tool for symptomatic or bulky sites.
- There is no universally accepted staging system for this tumor, which limits standardized stage-based sequencing beyond the resectable-versus-unresectable/metastatic framework above.

## Monitoring

- Because metastatic potential is high, with spread to lung, liver, lymph nodes, spleen, and brain reported, surveillance imaging of the chest (and other sites as clinically indicated) is warranted after treatment, alongside close clinical follow-up of the affected limb and surrounding lymphedematous field for new or recurrent lesions.
- Any new violaceous, bruise-like, or nodular change in a chronically lymphedematous limb after treatment warrants prompt re-biopsy rather than reassurance, given the propensity for multifocal and recurrent disease.

## When to refer

- All patients with a confirmed or suspected diagnosis should be referred promptly to a center with soft-tissue sarcoma/surgical oncology and radiation oncology expertise, given the need for wide or amputative surgery, individualized radiotherapy planning, and specialist judgment on limb salvage versus amputation.
- Refer for medical oncology input whenever systemic therapy is being considered (unresectable, multifocal, or metastatic disease), and for consideration of clinical trials given the poor outcomes with standard approaches.
- Reported prognostic correlations (lesion size at diagnosis, depth of invasion, mitotic rate, and older age among others) support early specialist involvement rather than a period of watchful observation once the diagnosis is suspected.

## Prognosis (context for treatment discussions)

- Outcomes for cutaneous angiosarcoma overall are poor, with reported mortality around 50% by 15 months from diagnosis and 5-year survival in roughly the 10% to 30% range; smaller lesions at diagnosis (under about 5 cm) are associated with better survival. These figures are reported for cutaneous angiosarcoma broadly rather than as Stewart-Treves-specific statistics.

Sources: DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of Oncology, 12th ed.
