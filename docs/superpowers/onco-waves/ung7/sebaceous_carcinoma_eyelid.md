# Eyelid sebaceous gland carcinoma (sebaceous carcinoma, SC) - Management

## Overview and staging context

Periocular sebaceous carcinoma accounts for roughly three-quarters of all sebaceous carcinoma cases and most often arises from the Meibomian glands of the tarsus, with the upper eyelid the most frequently involved site. It is the second most common eyelid malignancy after basal cell carcinoma and the second most lethal eyelid malignancy after melanoma. Because periocular SC is classified as an eyelid carcinoma, it is staged using the AJCC8 eyelid carcinoma staging system rather than a dedicated sebaceous-carcinoma-specific system; no separate staging system exists for extraocular SC. Lower-eyelid tumors and those with orbital extension have been associated with higher mortality than tumors at other periocular sites.

## Diagnosis and workup before treatment

- Biopsy with attention to depth and margins; because SC frequently shows pagetoid spread (intraepithelial spread of tumor cells into the overlying conjunctival or eyelid epithelium), map biopsies of the surrounding conjunctiva may be needed to define the true extent of disease before definitive surgery. Long-standing tumors can also be multicentric with discontinuous foci, which is a source of underestimated margins on routine paraffin sections.
- Special stains (lipid stains such as Oil Red O or Sudan IV on fresh tissue) and immunohistochemistry (for example epithelial membrane antigen or LeuM1) support the diagnosis on pathology.
- All patients with sebaceous carcinoma should be screened for Muir-Torre syndrome using the Mayo MTS risk scoring system. Risk factors that raise suspicion include age under 60, more than one sebaceous neoplasm, and a personal or family history of a Lynch-related cancer; a score of 3 or more warrants referral for clinical genetics consultation, since Muir-Torre syndrome (a Lynch-syndrome variant from mismatch-repair gene mutations) carries an increased risk of colorectal, urogenital, and other internal malignancies.
- Tumors staged T2c or higher on the AJCC8 eyelid system can be considered for sentinel lymph node biopsy, reflecting the tumor's capacity to spread by lymphatic or hematogenous routes or by direct extension.

## Treatment approach by intent

### Localized, resectable disease (primary treatment)
Surgery is the mainstay of treatment for localized eyelid SC:
- Wide local excision with 5 mm to 6 mm margins, or
- Extirpation by Mohs micrographic surgery (MMS), or
- Other excision techniques that allow complete circumferential peripheral and deep margin assessment.

Margin width matters clinically: in one series of SC excised with frozen-section margin control, recurrences occurred with margins of only 1 to 3 mm, while no recurrences occurred with 5 mm margins. MMS has compared favorably with wide local excision, with reported local recurrence rates of 12% or less in published series, versus local recurrence rates as high as 36% at 5 years after wide local excision (with an associated 5-year mortality of about 18% in that setting). Given the tendency toward multicentricity and pagetoid spread that is hard to detect even on high-quality paraffin sections, a technique with complete margin assessment (MMS or an equivalent frozen-section-controlled excision) is preferred over standard wide excision where feasible (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease, though the margin-recurrence data above are DeVita-sourced).

### Adjuvant or definitive radiotherapy
Radiotherapy has a role in selected cases, including a reported series of poorly differentiated SC successfully treated with radiotherapy. RT is generally reserved for patients who are not surgical candidates, have positive or inadequate margins, or have more aggressive/poorly differentiated histology, and orbital exenteration or adjuvant RT may be considered for orbital invasion (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

### Regional and metastatic disease
Because SC can spread by lymphatic or hematogenous routes or by direct extension, and distant metastases occur in up to 20% of cases (most often to lungs, liver, brain, bones, and lymph nodes), patients found to have nodal or distant disease require multidisciplinary management. Systemic therapy options for advanced or metastatic sebaceous carcinoma are not detailed in this DeVita section; decisions about systemic treatment (including any role for chemotherapy, targeted therapy, or immunotherapy) should be individualized in a multidisciplinary tumor board and are not specified here for lack of grounding in this source.

### Lines of therapy
This DeVita section describes primary local treatment (surgery, with radiotherapy in selected cases) but does not lay out a sequence of systemic lines of therapy for sebaceous carcinoma. No claims about a specific first-line, second-line, or later-line systemic regimen are made here because none were found in the grounding text; any such treatment should be guided by current NCCN guidance and multidisciplinary discussion (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Prognosis, drivers of outcome, and monitoring

- Periocular SC has a high rate of local recurrence and metastatic potential; extraocular SC generally carries a better prognosis. Small primary lesions under 1 cm in diameter appear to have a more favorable prognosis, analogous to early-stage squamous cell carcinoma.
- Reported mortality for SC overall ranges from 9% to 50%; extraocular SC has a reported local recurrence rate of 29% and a metastatic rate of 21%, though the literature may carry a bias toward reporting more advanced disease.
- Given the potential for late and locally recurrent disease, and the risk of nodal or distant spread, long-term clinical follow-up of the eyelid, periocular region, and regional lymph nodes is warranted after treatment (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease, by analogy to the lifelong follow-up recommended for other aggressive periocular/cutaneous carcinomas in the same source).

## When to refer

- Refer to an oculoplastic or ophthalmic oncology surgeon (or a Mohs surgeon experienced with periocular tumors) for any suspected or biopsy-confirmed eyelid SC, given the need for margin-controlled excision and the difficulty of clinically distinguishing pagetoid spread.
- Refer to clinical genetics when the Mayo MTS risk score is 3 or higher, given the association with Muir-Torre syndrome and its link to colorectal, urogenital, and other internal malignancies.
- Refer to radiation oncology for patients who are poor surgical candidates, have positive/inadequate margins, or have poorly differentiated histology.
- Refer to medical oncology and a multidisciplinary tumor board for any regional nodal or distant metastatic disease.
