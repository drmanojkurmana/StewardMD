# Synovial sarcoma (SS18-SSX) - Management

## Overview and treatment intent
Synovial sarcoma is a translocation-associated soft tissue sarcoma (t(X;18) producing an SS18-SSX
fusion) that most often arises in the deep soft tissue of an extremity in adolescents and young
adults, though it can occur at any age or site. Treatment intent is defined by resectability and
extent of disease: localized, potentially curable disease is treated with a multimodality approach
built around surgery, while metastatic disease is managed with systemic therapy, since synovial
sarcoma is among the more chemotherapy-responsive adult sarcoma subtypes.

## Localized disease

### Surgery
Wide en-bloc surgical excision with negative margins, limb-sparing where feasible, is the mainstay
of curative treatment for localized synovial sarcoma, as it is for soft tissue sarcoma generally.
Because synovial sarcoma can arise from or encase a major nerve, function-sparing dissection is
attempted, but resection of the involved nerve may be required for high-grade tumors. Planned
excision of the prior biopsy tract is part of adequate surgery.

### Radiotherapy
Radiotherapy is added to surgery for local control in high-grade, deep, or large tumors, or when
margins are close or positive, following the general soft tissue sarcoma paradigm of perioperative
(pre- or postoperative) radiation for higher-risk lesions.

### Perioperative (neoadjuvant or adjuvant) chemotherapy
Synovial sarcoma is specifically named, together with round cell/pleomorphic liposarcoma and
undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma, as a chemotherapy-responsive subtype for which neoadjuvant
or adjuvant chemotherapy should be considered, particularly for high-grade tumors greater than
10 cm or for synovial sarcoma or myxoid/round cell liposarcoma of any high-risk size. A dedicated
prognostic nomogram for primary synovial sarcoma helps identify patients likely to benefit from
adjuvant or neoadjuvant chemotherapy. In a retrospective study of adults with primary extremity
synovial sarcoma of 5 cm or larger, ifosfamide-based chemotherapy was independently associated
with improved disease-specific survival compared with no chemotherapy, and this approach should
therefore be considered for high-risk primary extremity disease. Ifosfamide-based chemotherapy
(such as an anthracycline-ifosfamide combination) is the systemic regimen most specifically
supported for this subtype; anthracycline monotherapy or an anthracycline-based doublet is used
more broadly across soft tissue sarcoma histologies when chemotherapy is given perioperatively
(general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Metastatic or unresectable disease

### Systemic therapy, first line
Synovial sarcoma has relatively high response rates to chemotherapy overall, an effect that may be
confounded by patients tending to be younger with better performance status. Ifosfamide is
specifically noted as particularly active in synovial sarcoma, with high response rates reported at
high doses in advanced disease. An anthracycline-ifosfamide combination is favored when a
clinical response is needed (for example, to render oligometastatic or borderline-resectable
disease operable), while single-agent anthracycline remains a reasonable option for patients with
poorer performance status.

### Subsequent lines
After first-line anthracycline-based therapy, options include gemcitabine plus docetaxel,
single-agent ifosfamide, pazopanib, or dacarbazine. Ifosfamide is specifically useful in synovial
sarcoma (more so than in leiomyosarcoma). Trabectedin has reported activity against synovial
sarcoma. Among targeted tyrosine kinase inhibitors, pazopanib has shown a modest response rate in
synovial sarcoma, while sorafenib has shown essentially no activity; sunitinib and mTOR inhibitors
are not well studied in this subtype.

### Emerging / trial-based options
Synovial sarcoma commonly expresses the cancer-germ cell antigens NY-ESO-1 and MAGE-A4, and
engineered T-cell receptor therapies directed against these antigens (HLA-restricted) have shown
meaningful objective response rates in early-phase studies of metastatic synovial sarcoma, with
durable responses in a subset of patients. Checkpoint inhibitor immunotherapy has shown activity
across soft tissue sarcoma broadly, though the specific subtypes most likely to respond to
single-agent checkpoint blockade (such as undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma) are better
supported than synovial sarcoma specifically. Given the limited number of approved options, patients
with advanced synovial sarcoma are reasonable candidates for enrollment in clinical trials of novel
and targeted agents, including antigen-directed cellular therapies.

## Monitoring and follow-up
Synovial sarcoma has a tendency toward late local recurrence and late distant (chiefly pulmonary)
metastasis, with disease-specific survival falling from the 5-year to the 15-year mark because of
this late relapse pattern. Long-term surveillance with imaging of the primary site and periodic
chest imaging is therefore required well beyond the typical 5-year follow-up horizon used for many
other cancers (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease, though
consistent with the late-recurrence biology described in DeVita).

### Management of pulmonary metastases
For isolated or oligometastatic pulmonary recurrence, surgical metastasectomy is associated with
prolonged survival, and repeat resection can be considered for further pulmonary recurrences in
selected patients, with prognosis at repeat resection influenced by the number and size of lesions
and by histologic grade.

## When to refer
Given the rarity of synovial sarcoma, its capacity to encase major nerves or vessels, and the
central role of a correctly planned biopsy and margin-negative resection in outcome, management
from the point of diagnosis should occur within a specialist sarcoma multidisciplinary team,
including surgical oncology, radiation oncology, and medical oncology, before any biopsy or
resection is undertaken. Patients with advanced or refractory disease, or those who may be
candidates for antigen-directed cellular therapy or other trials, should be referred to a center
running relevant sarcoma clinical trials.

Sources: DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of Oncology, 12th ed.
