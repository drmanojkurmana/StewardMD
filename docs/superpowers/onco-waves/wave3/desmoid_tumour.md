# Desmoid tumour (desmoid-type fibromatosis, aggressive fibromatosis) — management

## Overall approach

Desmoid tumours never metastasise, and individual tumours show a genuinely
unpredictable natural history: some enlarge and cause symptoms, some remain
stable for years, and some regress spontaneously without any treatment. Because
of this, and because attempts at complete wide surgical excision have
historically carried high morbidity and high local recurrence rates, surgery
is no longer considered the default first-line treatment. The modern approach
is to match the intensity of treatment to whether the patient is symptomatic
or progressing, rather than to treat every tumour aggressively at diagnosis.

## Initial management: active surveillance

- In an asymptomatic patient with a newly diagnosed or recurrent desmoid,
  an initial period of active observation (serial clinical exam and MRI) is
  the generally preferred first step.
- Comparative data show similar longer-term progression-free outcomes between
  a wait-and-see strategy and immediate treatment with hormonal therapy or
  chemotherapy, supporting observation as a safe first move for many patients
  with primary or locally recurrent disease.
- Surveillance avoids the morbidity of surgery or radiotherapy in patients
  whose disease may never require intervention.

## When treatment is triggered: symptomatic or progressive disease

If a patient becomes symptomatic (pain, functional loss, compression, or
obstruction) or shows clear progression on surveillance imaging, treatment
options are systemic therapy, surgery, or (less often) radiation. The choice
among these should be guided by:

- Tumour size (larger lesions, greater than roughly 5 cm, carry higher
  recurrence risk after resection)
- Site (chest wall and extremity tumours recur more often than small abdominal
  wall tumours)
- Younger age at presentation (associated with higher recurrence risk)
- Microscopic margin status is a poor predictor of recurrence and should not
  by itself drive the decision to operate or re-operate

### Role of surgery

- Surgery remains a reasonable option for small, resectable abdominal wall
  tumours that are growing or painful, where cure rates after resection are
  high.
- For intra-abdominal (mesenteric) desmoids, surgery is an option when the
  tumour is separable from central mesenteric vessels, since these lesions can
  cause life-threatening complications such as bowel fistulisation; recurrence
  risk after resection is still substantial.
- Large extremity or chest wall desmoids in young patients have a high
  recurrence rate after surgery, favouring a systemic-therapy-first strategy in
  this group rather than upfront resection.
- A positive microscopic margin alone does not mandate re-excision; local
  control after surgery alone even with positive margins is achieved in a
  meaningful proportion of patients.

### Role of systemic therapy

Systemic therapy is used for tumours with a high risk of recurrence after
surgery, for unresectable disease, or as an alternative to morbid resection.
Options, in roughly increasing order of intended anti-tumour effect, include:

- Anti-inflammatory agents such as sulindac or other NSAIDs, which have
  produced reported responses and are typically used for less aggressive or
  lower-risk disease given their favourable toxicity profile
- Hormonal manipulation (for example tamoxifen, gonadotropin-releasing hormone
  agonists, or aromatase inhibitors), supported mainly by anecdotal response
  data
- Cytotoxic chemotherapy, including anthracycline-based regimens (single-agent
  or liposomal formulations) or combination chemotherapy, reserved for more
  aggressive or refractory disease
- Tyrosine kinase inhibitors, which have shown clinical benefit in this
  disease; a placebo-controlled randomised trial confirmed improved
  progression-free survival with a multitargeted tyrosine kinase inhibitor
  compared with placebo, and this class is a reasonable systemic option when a
  more predictable response is needed
- A gamma-secretase inhibitor (targeting Notch signalling, which cross-talks
  with the Wnt/beta-catenin pathway driving desmoid biology) is under
  investigation on this mechanistic basis as a targeted option in refractory
  disease

A key counselling point for all systemic options: responses are typically
slow, sometimes taking many months to years to reach maximum benefit, and true
complete responses are rare. Stable disease on a systemic agent should
generally not prompt discontinuation; treatment changes should be driven by
toxicity or clear progression rather than by the pace of response.

### Role of radiation therapy

- The role of adjuvant radiation after surgery is controversial. Most
  clinicians recommend against postoperative radiation when resection margins
  are negative.
- Its use with microscopically positive margins is debated, since surgery
  alone (without radiation) still achieves local control in a majority of such
  patients, and the tumours are in a young population where radiation carries
  a small but real long-term risk of radiation-associated secondary sarcoma
  and joint fibrosis.
- Definitive (non-operative) radiation is an emerging alternative to surgery,
  particularly when surgical resection would compromise function, and has
  achieved meaningful rates of local control for gross disease in this
  setting.

### Regional/ablative therapies

For selected extra-abdominal desmoids, image-guided regional therapies
(high-intensity focused ultrasound ablation, cryoablation, or transarterial
chemoembolisation) have been described as alternatives to surgery or
radiation, particularly where long-term treatment-related side effects are a
concern. Tumour size and vascular access are practical factors that determine
whether a given lesion is suitable for these techniques; long-term outcome
data are still limited.

## Monitoring

- Patients managed with active surveillance require serial clinical
  assessment and interval MRI to detect growth or evolving symptoms early.
- Patients on systemic therapy should be monitored for both treatment
  toxicity and disease response, recognising that benefit may only become
  apparent after a prolonged treatment course.
- After surgery, ongoing follow-up is warranted given the substantial risk of
  local recurrence, particularly at extremity, chest wall, and mesenteric
  sites.
- A new mesenteric or multifocal desmoid should prompt evaluation for
  underlying familial adenomatous polyposis.

## When to refer

- Refer to a sarcoma/soft-tissue tumour multidisciplinary team for any
  suspected or confirmed desmoid tumour, given the need to weigh
  surveillance, systemic therapy, surgery, and radiation options against each
  other for an individual patient.
- Refer urgently for surgical or interventional radiology assessment when
  there is bowel obstruction, fistulisation, or compression of vital
  structures (especially in head and neck or intra-abdominal disease).
- Refer for genetics/colorectal assessment when a mesenteric or multifocal
  desmoid raises suspicion of familial adenomatous polyposis.

DeVita is silent on a specific, validated systemic therapy sequencing order
(that is, which class to try first, second, third) beyond describing the
range of agents used and the general move away from upfront surgery; that
detail is intentionally left as guideline-general judgement.

Sources: DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of
Oncology, 12th ed.
