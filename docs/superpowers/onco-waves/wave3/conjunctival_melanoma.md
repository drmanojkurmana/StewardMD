# Conjunctival melanoma — management expansion

## Treatment approach by stage and intent

Conjunctival melanoma is treated primarily with surgery, and the intent shifts with how the
tumor presents.

- **Localized bulbar disease** (thin, well-defined, limited to the bulbar conjunctiva): treated
  with curative intent using complete surgical excision. The goal is clear margins in one
  procedure, since incomplete excision or biopsy-then-wait approaches raise recurrence risk.
- **Extrabulbar or diffuse disease** (palpebral, forniceal, caruncular, or tarsal involvement, or
  diffuse pigmentation from primary acquired melanosis with atypia): treated with a combined
  surgical and topical or radiation approach, still with curative intent where feasible, but with
  closer surveillance given a higher baseline recurrence risk.
- **Regional nodal disease**: intent remains curative when nodal disease is resectable, with
  surgical clearance of the involved regional basin and consideration of adjuvant radiotherapy.
- **Metastatic (distant) disease**: intent shifts to systemic disease control. This is managed as
  systemic melanoma, referred to medical oncology.

## Role of surgery

Surgery is the backbone of local treatment.

- Wide local excision using a no-touch technique (avoiding direct instrument contact with tumor
  to reduce seeding) is the standard primary procedure.
- Incisional or partial biopsy of a resectable primary lesion is discouraged; the preference is to
  excise the whole lesion when feasible so that margin status can be assessed properly.
- Map biopsies (multiple separate conjunctival biopsies) are used when there is diffuse primary
  acquired melanosis with atypia, to define how far the atypical melanocytic change extends beyond
  the visible tumor.
- Adjunctive cryotherapy is applied to the surgical margins after excision to reduce local
  recurrence.
- Regional lymph node assessment (preauricular and cervical basins) is part of staging; sentinel
  lymph node biopsy is used selectively in thicker or higher-risk tumors to detect occult nodal
  spread.

## Role of radiotherapy

Radiotherapy is adjuvant rather than primary treatment in most cases.

- Used for tumors that are thick, have close or involved margins after excision, or have
  recurred locally.
- Plaque brachytherapy and proton beam radiotherapy are the modalities described for this
  purpose; the choice between them is an ophthalmic oncology decision based on tumor location and
  thickness.
- Radiotherapy does not replace excision as the primary local treatment; it is added when surgery
  alone leaves an elevated risk of local recurrence.

## Role of systemic and topical therapy

- Topical chemotherapy (an agent such as mitomycin C, without specifying a dose or schedule here)
  is used for diffuse primary acquired melanosis with atypia, either as an adjunct after excision
  of an invasive component or to treat extensive atypical melanocytic change that is not amenable
  to excision alone.
- For confirmed metastatic or unresectable regional disease, treatment moves to systemic
  oncological therapy. Immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy is the modality referenced for
  metastatic disease; specific regimen selection belongs to medical oncology and is outside the
  scope of this note.
- There is no established role for cytotoxic chemotherapy as primary treatment of localized
  conjunctival melanoma.

## Lines of therapy

A distinct "line of therapy" framework (first line, second line, and so on) is not well
established for conjunctival melanoma in the way it is for systemic cancers, because the disease
is managed sequentially by anatomic problem rather than by drug regimen:

1. Complete surgical excision (with cryotherapy) is the first and usually only local intervention
   for confined disease.
2. Adjuvant radiotherapy is added when margins or thickness raise recurrence risk.
3. Recurrent or progressive local/regional disease prompts re-excision, additional radiotherapy,
   or nodal surgery as applicable.
4. Distant metastatic disease is referred for systemic therapy (checkpoint inhibitor-based),
   managed as for systemic melanoma.

## Monitoring

- Lifelong slit-lamp surveillance is required, watching for local recurrence and for new areas of
  primary acquired melanosis, since both can occur years after apparently successful excision.
- Regional lymph node examination should be part of routine follow-up, given that preauricular and
  cervical nodes are the first site of metastatic spread.
- Higher-risk or recurrent tumors warrant periodic systemic staging imaging to detect distant
  metastasis.
- Follow-up intervals and duration should be set by the managing ophthalmic oncology team based on
  tumor thickness, location, and margin status at excision.

## When to refer

- Any lesion with new nodularity, rapid growth, increasing vascularity, or extrabulbar extension
  (fornix, palpebral conjunctiva, caruncle, tarsus) should be referred to an ophthalmic oncology
  service rather than managed with observation alone.
- Palpable preauricular or cervical lymphadenopathy warrants prompt referral for nodal evaluation.
- Recurrence after prior treatment, or extensive diffuse primary acquired melanosis with atypia,
  should be managed within a multidisciplinary ophthalmic oncology team.
- Confirmed regional nodal or distant metastatic disease should be referred to medical oncology
  for systemic therapy planning, alongside continued ophthalmic oncology involvement for local
  disease control.
