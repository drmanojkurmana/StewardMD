# Cutaneous angiosarcoma — management

Cutaneous angiosarcoma (including tumors previously called lymphangiosarcoma, and the lymphoedema-associated form known as Stewart-Treves syndrome) is a rare, aggressive vascular malignancy. Management is shaped heavily by anatomic context (head and neck in elderly patients, post-irradiation, or lymphoedema-associated) because these settings differ in resectability, wound healing, and expected behaviour.

## Localized disease: surgery and radiotherapy

- Wide surgical resection with clear margins is the treatment of choice when the disease is localized and resectable, but multifocal, subclinical spread along dermal and subcutaneous planes makes achieving clear margins genuinely difficult, and local recurrence is common even after apparently complete excision.
- Lymphoedema-associated (Stewart-Treves) angiosarcoma is particularly hard to manage surgically because the chronically lymphoedematous limb has poor wound healing, which can compromise the ability to spare the limb and may push toward amputation in some cases.
- Radiotherapy has an established role for angiosarcoma of the skin, used both as an adjunct to surgery to reduce local recurrence and, in some patients, as a primary modality when resection is not feasible.
- Because surgical resection alone is rarely curative for this disease, most patients with localized disease are treated with a combined surgery-plus-radiotherapy approach rather than surgery alone, and the whole affected field should be considered at risk given the tendency to multifocal, subclinical spread.

## Systemic therapy

- Angiosarcoma differs from most other soft tissue sarcomas in that it retains meaningful, at least initial, sensitivity to anthracycline-based chemotherapy and to taxanes; taxanes have little activity across sarcomas generally but are a relative area of activity specifically in angiosarcoma.
- Systemic therapy is used for disease that is unresectable, multifocal beyond what surgery/radiotherapy can address, or metastatic (angiosarcoma has a high rate of haematogenous metastasis, particularly to lung).
- Antiangiogenic tyrosine kinase inhibitors and VEGF-pathway-directed antiangiogenic agents have been explored specifically because angiosarcoma is a tumour of vascular origin and because of molecular findings such as KDR mutations and MYC/FLT4 amplification; response activity has been described with this class of agent, more so than has been seen for most other angiogenesis inhibitors tried across sarcomas broadly.
- A combination approach pairing a beta-blocker (propranolol) with metronomic vinblastine-based chemotherapy has been reported to produce responses in patients with inoperable angiosarcoma in a small clinical study; this remains an emerging strategy rather than an established standard.
- Immune checkpoint blockade is an active area of investigation in angiosarcoma. Head-and-neck cutaneous angiosarcomas in particular have been reported to carry a higher tumour mutational burden than other angiosarcomas, and a trial of combination checkpoint blockade (a PD-1 antibody plus a CTLA-4 antibody) reported objective responses in a meaningful proportion of patients with cutaneous head-and-neck angiosarcoma. This supports considering checkpoint inhibition, particularly in that subgroup, when other options are exhausted or in the context of a trial.
- For advanced/metastatic disease, treatment is generally sequential: an anthracycline- or taxane-based approach for a first line of systemic therapy, followed by an antiangiogenic tyrosine kinase inhibitor or, in appropriate patients (especially head-and-neck disease with expected high mutational burden), immune checkpoint blockade in a later line.

## Prognosis and monitoring

- Outcomes are generally poor: median time to disease-specific death has been reported as short as a few years in some patient subsets. Factors associated with worse outcome include older patient age, greater tumour depth, and larger tumour size.
- Multicentric head-and-neck angiosarcoma in elderly patients tends to show relentless local progression, with a risk of severe ulceration, secondary infection, and eventual metastasis, which argues for close clinical surveillance of the primary site as well as for metastatic disease.
- Given the high rates of both local and distant recurrence, follow-up should include regular skin/field examination for local recurrence and periodic imaging surveillance for pulmonary and other distant metastases, at a frequency guided by multidisciplinary sarcoma-team judgment.

## When to refer

- Any suspected or confirmed cutaneous angiosarcoma should be referred promptly to a sarcoma multidisciplinary team (surgical oncology with plastic/reconstructive input, radiation oncology, and medical oncology) given the rarity of the disease, the difficulty of achieving clear margins, and the specialised radiotherapy planning often required.
- Lymphoedema-associated (Stewart-Treves) disease and cases where limb-sparing surgery is in question warrant early involvement of a reconstructive/limb-salvage surgical team alongside oncology.
- Metastatic, unresectable, or recurrent disease, or any case being considered for antiangiogenic therapy, checkpoint blockade, or a beta-blocker/metronomic chemotherapy approach, should be managed by or referred to a sarcoma medical oncology service, ideally with access to clinical trials given the rarity of this tumour and the still-evolving evidence base.
