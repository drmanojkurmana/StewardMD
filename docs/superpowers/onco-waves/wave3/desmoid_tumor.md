# Desmoid Tumor (Desmoid-type Fibromatosis): Management

## Overview and treatment philosophy

Desmoid tumor (deep or aggressive fibromatosis) is a rare, clonal myofibroblastic neoplasm that infiltrates locally but does not metastasize. It arises sporadically (driven almost always by an activating CTNNB1 mutation) or in the setting of an inherited APC mutation (Gardner syndrome / familial adenomatous polyposis). Behavior is highly variable: some lesions grow and cause pain, joint contracture, or bowel obstruction, while others remain stable for years or regress spontaneously without any treatment. Death from desmoid tumor is rare and, when it occurs, is usually from local compression of vital structures (especially head and neck disease), bowel fistulization from intra-abdominal lesions, or complications of aggressive resection rather than from the disease itself.

Because of this unpredictable, often self-limited natural history, and because historic surgical series showed high local recurrence with significant morbidity, management has shifted away from uniform upfront surgery toward a strategy tailored to symptoms and growth behavior.

## First-line approach: active observation

In asymptomatic patients, or those with stable or slowly regressing disease, an initial period of active observation (watch and wait) is the preferred first-line strategy, supported by comparative outcome data showing similar progression-free survival with observation versus immediate hormonal or chemotherapy in a mixed population of primary and locally recurrent desmoids. Observation avoids the morbidity of surgery or radiotherapy in patients whose disease may never require treatment. Regular clinical reassessment and imaging (see Monitoring below) are used to detect growth or the onset of symptoms that would prompt escalation to active treatment.

## When to escalate to active treatment

Escalation from observation to active treatment is warranted for:
- Symptomatic disease (pain, functional impairment, joint contracture, bowel obstruction or threatened fistulization)
- Documented growth or progression on serial imaging
- Anatomic sites where growth threatens vital structures (e.g., mesenteric lesions abutting central vessels, head and neck disease)

Selection among surgery, systemic therapy, and (rarely) radiation at this point should be guided by factors associated with a higher chance of recurrence or a more difficult surgical course, including larger tumor size, extremity or chest wall location, and younger age at presentation; microscopic margin status alone is not a reliable predictor.

## Role of surgery

Surgery is no longer considered the default, gold-standard first step for most desmoid tumors, because complete wide excision can cause significant morbidity and historic recurrence rates were high. Its role is now more selective:
- Small abdominal wall desmoids have favorable surgical cure rates and surgery is a reasonable option if the tumor is growing or painful.
- Large extremity desmoids in younger patients recur very frequently after resection, favoring systemic therapy over surgery when the disease is not immediately threatening.
- Intra-abdominal (mesenteric) lesions carry a meaningful recurrence risk after resection, but surgery remains an option when the tumor threatens fistulization or appears separable from central mesenteric vessels.
- Adjuvant radiation after resection is generally omitted when margins are negative; its use with microscopically positive margins is debated, since surgery alone in that setting still achieves local control in roughly half of patients, and radiation carries a small but serious long-term risk of radiation-associated sarcoma in what is typically a young patient population.

## Role of radiation

Adjuvant postoperative radiation is used selectively, mainly when margins are positive, and remains controversial given the young age of many patients and the fact that desmoid tumors never metastasize. Definitive (non-operative) radiation has emerged as an alternative to surgery in situations where resection would compromise function, with reported local control rates in the range typically cited for gross disease treated with definitive dosing; long-term risks of secondary malignancy and joint fibrosis must be weighed against this benefit, particularly in younger patients.

## Role of systemic therapy

Systemic therapy is used for symptomatic or progressive desmoid tumors that are not straightforward surgical candidates, or when surgery would require morbid resection. Options, in rough order of toxicity, include:
- NSAIDs (e.g., sulindac-class agents), which have produced well-documented responses and are generally the lowest-toxicity option.
- Hormonal manipulation (anti-estrogen agents, GnRH agonists, aromatase inhibitors), supported mainly by anecdotal responses.
- Cytotoxic chemotherapy, including anthracycline-based approaches (single-agent or liposomal formulations) and combination chemotherapy regimens, reserved for more symptomatic or refractory disease.
- Tyrosine kinase inhibitors targeting angiogenic/PDGF-receptor signaling, which have shown clinically meaningful benefit; a placebo-controlled trial in patients with documented disease progression demonstrated a clear progression-free survival advantage over placebo, and retrospective experience describes frequent disease stabilization, a meaningful partial response rate, and rapid symptomatic improvement (sometimes within two weeks of starting therapy).
- Notch-pathway-directed therapy (an oral gamma-secretase inhibitor) has been studied based on cross-talk between Wnt and Notch signaling in desmoid biology; this reflects the Wnt/beta-catenin driver biology of the disease (CTNNB1 mutation in the large majority of sporadic tumors, APC loss in FAP-associated disease) rather than an established first-line standard.

Responses to systemic therapy in desmoid tumor tend to be slow, sometimes taking months to years to reach maximum benefit, and complete responses are uncommon. Therapy should generally not be abandoned for stable disease alone; changes should be prompted by toxicity or unambiguous progression. Clinicians should also be aware that some historic series treated patients before the natural history of spontaneous stabilization/regression was well recognized, which may have overestimated apparent treatment benefit.

## Other regional/ablative options

For lesions not well suited to surgery or radiation, image-guided ablative techniques (e.g., cryoablation, high-intensity focused ultrasound, and chemoembolization) have been described, taking advantage of the tumor's lack of metastatic potential to justify a purely local, function-preserving approach. Larger tumor size has been associated with a higher chance of recurrence after ablative treatment, so patient and lesion selection matters.

## Monitoring

Because many desmoid tumors are managed expectantly, monitoring is central to safe care:
- Regular clinical assessment for new or worsening symptoms (pain, functional limitation, signs of bowel involvement)
- Serial cross-sectional imaging at intervals appropriate to the site and behavior of the tumor, to detect growth before it causes irreversible functional loss or a surgical emergency
- In patients on systemic therapy, monitoring should extend over a prolonged period given the slow tempo of response, with escalation reserved for clear progression or intolerable toxicity rather than lack of rapid response

## When to refer

Given the rarity of desmoid tumor and the range of modality-specific expertise needed to weigh observation against surgery, radiation, systemic therapy, or ablative options, patients with growing, symptomatic, or anatomically high-risk disease (chest wall, extremity, retroperitoneal/mesenteric, or head and neck involvement) should be referred to a sarcoma center or multidisciplinary sarcoma tumor board. Patients with multifocal disease, disease onset at a young age, or a personal/family history suggestive of familial adenomatous polyposis (Gardner syndrome) should also be referred for genetics evaluation given the association between desmoid tumor and germline APC mutation.
