# Elastofibroma (elastofibroma dorsi) — Management

## Nature of the lesion and why this shapes treatment
Elastofibroma is a rare, slow-growing benign soft-tissue lesion, most characteristically found deep to the scapula between the chest wall and the lower scapular tip. It can occur bilaterally and can grow large. Because it is benign with no malignant potential, management intent is never curative-oncologic in the sarcoma sense; it is a decision about observation versus symptom-directed local treatment.

## Diagnosis before treatment decisions
Up to a third of cases are familial, and in that setting bilateral subscapular lesions can be diagnosed on history and imaging alone, without biopsy. For unilateral, sporadic disease, biopsy is commonly used to confirm the diagnosis before deciding on treatment, given the differential includes other soft-tissue masses of the chest wall/back. When imaging and clinical context are classic, a confident diagnosis without biopsy is reasonable; biopsy should be reserved for atypical presentations (atypical location, rapid growth, or diagnostic uncertainty).

## Treatment approach by presentation
- Asymptomatic, radiologically typical lesion: observation is appropriate. No systemic therapy, radiotherapy, or urgent surgery is indicated for a benign, non-growing, asymptomatic mass.
- Symptomatic lesion (pain, mechanical snapping/clicking with shoulder movement, restricted motion) with a definitive diagnosis: surgical resection can be reserved for this group. Marginal excision is the surgical approach used, and it is generally curative for symptomatic disease with a low recurrence rate.
- Atypical or diagnostically uncertain lesion: work-up (imaging plus biopsy if needed) to exclude sarcoma or other differentials before any decision on excision, since misreading the mass as elastofibroma without confirmation risks missing a true soft-tissue sarcoma of the chest wall.

## Lines of therapy
There are no lines of systemic therapy for elastofibroma. It is not a malignancy, and DeVita's discussion of it (within the soft-tissue sarcoma chapter, as a benign mimic) does not describe any drug treatment. The only interventions are observation and surgery; radiotherapy and systemic anticancer therapy have no role.

## Role of surgery
Surgery (marginal excision) is reserved for symptomatic cases once the diagnosis is definitive. It is not offered routinely to asymptomatic patients because the lesion is benign and slow-growing. Attention to surgical technique (adequate drainage/compression of the resulting cavity) matters because seroma or haematoma is the main complication of excision in this deep, often large, periscapular space.

## Role of radiotherapy and systemic therapy
Neither radiotherapy nor systemic therapy has an established role in elastofibroma; the condition is benign and grounded management is surgical (for symptomatic disease) or observational (for asymptomatic disease). No specific regimen, drug class, or radiation indication is described for this entity, and none should be inferred.

## Monitoring
For lesions managed with observation, follow-up is clinical, watching for development of symptoms (pain, mechanical snapping, restricted shoulder movement) that would prompt reconsideration of excision. Because contralateral lesions are common, especially in familial disease, bilateral assessment on imaging is reasonable at the time of diagnosis. After excision, monitoring is for local recurrence and for the early postoperative complications of seroma or haematoma; recurrence after marginal excision is uncommon.

## When to refer
Refer to a soft-tissue/orthopaedic oncology or surgical service when a periscapular mass is symptomatic and excision is being considered, when the presentation is atypical (unusual location, rapid growth, or imaging that does not fit the classic elastofibroma appearance) and a soft-tissue sarcoma cannot be confidently excluded, or when biopsy is needed to confirm the diagnosis in unilateral, non-familial disease.

## What is deliberately not covered here
DeVita does not describe any drug therapy, radiotherapy protocol, or staged/lines-of-therapy framework for elastofibroma because it is a benign, non-neoplastic-behaving lesion; this note does not invent one. No numeric outcome statistics beyond what is stated above (rarity, benign course, low recurrence) were found in the grounding text, so none are given.

Sources: DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of Oncology, 12th ed.
