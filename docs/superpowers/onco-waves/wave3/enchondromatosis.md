# Enchondromatosis (Ollier disease, Maffucci syndrome) - Management Expansion

## Framing

Enchondromatosis itself is a benign, non-malignant condition of multiple enchondromas driven by
mosaic IDH1/2 mutations. There is no oncologic "treatment" of the enchondromatosis in the way a
cancer is treated. The management problem for oncology is twofold: (1) orthopaedic management of
the benign lesion burden and its deformity, and (2) lifelong surveillance for malignant
transformation to chondrosarcoma, which is the principal reason this condition sits in an oncology
knowledge base at all. DeVita groups Ollier disease and Maffucci syndrome with hereditary multiple
exostoses as the recognised genetic predisposition syndromes for chondrosarcoma, and discusses
their management largely through that surveillance and transformation lens rather than as a
disease with its own dedicated treatment algorithm.

## Approach by clinical scenario

### Stable, asymptomatic enchondromas (the default state)
- No systemic anticancer therapy is indicated. Enchondromas are common benign cartilage lesions
  and the great majority can simply be followed clinically and radiographically without
  intervention.
- Orthopaedic surgery is reserved for functional problems: correction of limb-length discrepancy,
  angular deformity, or a pathological fracture, not for the presence of lesions per se.

### Suspected malignant transformation to chondrosarcoma
- New or increasing pain, rest or night pain, rapid enlargement (especially in an adult), cortical
  breakthrough on imaging, or an associated soft-tissue mass should trigger work-up for
  transformation rather than reassurance, because the distinction between a benign enchondroma, an
  atypical cartilaginous neoplasm, and a low-grade chondrosarcoma is notoriously difficult on
  imaging and even histology alone.
- Work-up favoured in this setting includes cross-sectional imaging (MRI to assess cortical and
  soft-tissue extension), PET/CT, image-guided biopsy, and intraoperative frozen section to guide
  the surgical plan.
- Once a chondrosarcoma is diagnosed, treatment follows chondrosarcoma management by grade, which
  is where enchondromatosis-associated malignancy is actually treated:
  - Localised, low-grade (grade 1) intraosseous lesions can be managed with aggressive intralesional
    surgery (curettage and burring with adjuvant local measures) rather than wide resection.
  - Grade 2 and higher-grade, dedifferentiated, mesenchymal, or clear cell chondrosarcomas are
    generally treated with wide resection and reconstruction (allograft, endoprosthesis, or
    vascularised graft depending on site), with amputation reserved for lesions where limb salvage
    is not feasible.
  - Chondrosarcoma, including the low- to intermediate-grade disease that arises from enchondromatosis,
    is characteristically resistant to standard cytotoxic chemotherapy, so systemic chemotherapy has
    a limited and non-standard role; it is not part of routine management the way it is for
    osteosarcoma or Ewing sarcoma. Radiation therapy has a role chiefly where surgical margins cannot
    be obtained or the site (e.g. axial or skull base) precludes complete resection.
  - Given the rarity and complexity of bone sarcoma care, management of a transformed lesion should
    happen at, or in direct consultation with, a centre with multidisciplinary bone tumour expertise.

### Maffucci syndrome specifically
- Because Maffucci syndrome carries a greater risk of malignant transformation than Ollier disease,
  and also predisposes to non-skeletal tumours, surveillance needs to extend beyond the skeleton.
- New neurological symptoms should prompt evaluation for an intracranial process, and monitoring
  should include attention to the described association with visceral tumours, in addition to
  skeletal surveillance.

## Monitoring / surveillance

There is no population-based screening test for bone sarcoma, but enchondromatosis is one of the
recognised orthopaedic-oncology conditions for which practical surveillance guidelines exist:
- Yearly plain radiographs of moderately to severely affected bones.
- A limited skeletal survey of involved regions roughly every two years.
- Additional MRI, CT, or PET based on personal and family history and the behaviour of individual
  lesions, with care taken to limit cumulative radiation exposure from screening itself.
- Surveillance should not be relaxed with age; sarcoma risk in these predisposition syndromes
  increases as patients get older, and deep-seated sites such as the pelvis and shoulder girdle
  deserve particular vigilance because tumours there can grow substantially before becoming
  symptomatic.
- In Maffucci syndrome, surveillance is broadened to include the described risk of intracranial and
  visceral tumours, not skeletal imaging alone.

## When to refer

- Any lesion with red-flag features (new or worsening pain, rapid growth, cortical destruction,
  soft-tissue mass, or new neurological signs in Maffucci syndrome) should be referred promptly for
  orthopaedic oncology assessment and biopsy planning rather than managed with imaging alone in
  general practice.
- Because of the rarity of these tumours and the complexity and stakes of limb-salvage surgery,
  confirmed or strongly suspected malignant transformation should be managed at, or with direct
  input from, a specialist multidisciplinary bone tumour centre.

## What DeVita does not specify (deliberately omitted)

DeVita does not give a dedicated, enchondromatosis-specific treatment algorithm, a preferred
systemic regimen for transformed disease, or a numerically defined surveillance interval that is
specific to this syndrome versus other cartilage-lesion predisposition conditions; those specifics
have been left out rather than inferred. No survival statistics, response rates, or genotype-directed
drug claims are made here because they were not found grounded to enchondromatosis in the source
text.

Sources: DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of Oncology, 12th ed.
