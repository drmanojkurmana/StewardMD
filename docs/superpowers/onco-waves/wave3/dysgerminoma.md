# Dysgerminoma: Management

Dysgerminoma is the ovarian counterpart of testicular seminoma and the most common malignant
ovarian germ-cell tumour, presenting mainly in adolescents and young women. It belongs to the
broader malignant germ-cell tumour group of the ovary, which accounts for a small minority of
all ovarian malignancies and typically presents in the late teens to early twenties. Disease is
almost always unilateral (bilateral involvement is described in a minority of dysgerminomas), and
the tumour is markedly chemosensitive and radiosensitive, which shapes the whole treatment
strategy toward fertility preservation even when disease has spread.

## Treatment approach by stage and intent

- **Early, surgically confined disease (FIGO stage IA dysgerminoma):** Fertility-sparing surgery,
  namely unilateral salpingo-oophorectomy with formal surgical staging, is the mainstay. This is
  the one subgroup of malignant ovarian germ-cell tumour that can be observed after surgery
  alone, without routine adjuvant chemotherapy, given the excellent outcomes in this stage.
- **Higher stage, metastatic, or recurrent disease:** Because germ-cell tumours are exquisitely
  chemosensitive, fertility-preserving surgery (removing the involved ovary while conserving the
  uterus and the contralateral ovary) remains an option even in the presence of metastases.
  Surgery is followed by adjuvant platinum-based combination chemotherapy.
- **Bilateral disease or gonadal dysgenesis:** In young patients, particularly those with features
  of gonadal dysgenesis or a Y-chromosome-bearing karyotype, the contralateral ovary needs
  careful assessment; a dysgenetic gonad harbouring a gonadoblastoma precursor may warrant
  bilateral gonadectomy because of the malignancy risk, and karyotyping should be considered in
  this population (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Lines of therapy

- Adjuvant systemic treatment for stage beyond IA is a platinum-based combination regimen
  built on bleomycin, etoposide, and cisplatin (the regimen historically extrapolated from
  testicular germ-cell tumour experience). No specific second-line regimen for dysgerminoma is
  described here; relapse or progression after a platinum-based regimen should prompt referral
  to a specialist germ-cell/gynaecologic oncology tumour board rather than empirical substitution.
- Radiotherapy is not part of first-line treatment in patients wishing to preserve fertility, since
  it would compromise ovarian function, but the tumour's marked radiosensitivity means it can be
  considered for selected chemoresistant or non-fertility-preservation situations.

## Role of surgery, radiotherapy, and systemic therapy

- **Surgery** is both diagnostic (staging) and the primary therapeutic step in essentially all
  patients. Fertility-sparing unilateral salpingo-oophorectomy with careful surgical staging is
  preferred over more radical resection whenever technically feasible, reflecting the young age of
  most patients and the tumour's chemosensitivity, which permits conservative surgery even with
  more advanced disease.
- **Systemic chemotherapy** (platinum-based, bleomycin-etoposide-cisplatin-based) is reserved for
  disease beyond stage IA, or for metastatic/recurrent presentations, and is highly effective.
- **Radiotherapy** has a narrow, secondary role, generally reserved for chemoresistant disease,
  given both the tumour's radiosensitivity and the wish to avoid compromising fertility and
  ovarian/uterine function with radiation exposure in young patients.

## Monitoring

- Serial tumour markers, specifically lactate dehydrogenase and alkaline phosphatase, are used to
  track treatment response and to detect relapse; AFP is characteristically normal in pure
  dysgerminoma. Dysgerminoma can also show modest beta-hCG elevation from syncytiotrophoblast-like
  giant cells (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Surveillance imaging complements marker follow-up.
- Return of normal menstrual function is the expected course after fertility-sparing treatment,
  and successful pregnancies are well documented in survivors, supporting ongoing reproductive
  counselling rather than presumption of infertility.
- A rising AFP during follow-up should raise concern for a mixed germ-cell tumour component
  rather than pure dysgerminoma, prompting re-evaluation.

## When to refer

- All patients with a malignant ovarian germ-cell tumour, including dysgerminoma, should be
  managed within a gynaecological oncology multidisciplinary team; outcomes in ovarian cancer more
  broadly are documented to be better when care follows staging/management guidelines and is
  delivered by high-volume gynaecologic oncology surgeons.
- Any patient with features suggesting gonadal dysgenesis (for example a Y-chromosome-bearing
  karyotype) should be referred for karyotyping and specialist discussion of gonadectomy risk versus
  fertility, given the association with gonadoblastoma (general oncology standard, not from
  DeVita's section on this disease).
- Young patients wishing to preserve fertility should be counselled and referred for fertility
  preservation discussion before treatment starts.
- Relapse, chemoresistance, or an atypical marker pattern (for example a rising AFP) should prompt
  referral back to a specialist germ-cell tumour board rather than continued treatment in a
  non-specialist setting.

## What DeVita is silent on (deliberately omitted)

DeVita's discussion of malignant ovarian germ-cell tumours as a group does not specify a distinct
second-line or salvage regimen for relapsed dysgerminoma, nor a fixed number of treatment cycles
or a defined surveillance interval/schedule, so none of these have been stated above; that detail
is left to specialist protocol-level guidance rather than invented here.

Sources: DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of Oncology, 12th ed.
