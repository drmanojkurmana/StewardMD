# Epithelioid sarcoma — Management

Epithelioid sarcoma is a rare soft-tissue sarcoma that arises most often in the distal extremities
(the classic, distal type on the hand, forearm or foot) or, less commonly, as a proximal-type
variant in the perineum, groin, thigh, buttock or axilla. It spreads along fascial planes and has an
unusually strong tendency, for a sarcoma, to involve regional lymph nodes. Loss of nuclear
SMARCB1 (INI1) expression is seen in the large majority of cases and drives upregulation of EZH2,
which is now a therapeutic target. All patients should be managed at, or in close consultation with,
a specialist sarcoma multidisciplinary centre from the point of diagnosis.

## Localized disease

- Wide local excision with clear, negative margins is the mainstay of curative treatment. Because
  the tumor infiltrates along fascia, tendons and nerves, adequate margins often require an
  extensive resection rather than a simple local excision.
- Adjuvant radiotherapy is used after limb-sparing surgery to reduce the high risk of local
  recurrence, particularly when margins are close or the tumor is deep.
- Amputation is reserved for extensive distal (hand/forearm/foot) disease where margin-negative
  limb-sparing surgery is not achievable.
- Because lymph node metastasis occurs in a meaningful minority of patients (DeVita cites roughly
  one in five), regional nodal basins should be assessed clinically and radiologically. Grossly
  involved nodes should be biopsied, and if nodal disease is confirmed without evidence of distant
  metastasis, a complete lymph node dissection should be considered. Sentinel node biopsy has not
  been shown to change outcome but may carry prognostic value.
- The proximal-type variant behaves more aggressively and is more resistant to both radiotherapy
  and chemotherapy than the distal (conventional) type, and this should inform counseling on
  prognosis and the intensity of local and systemic management.

## Locally advanced, unresectable, or metastatic disease

- Epithelioid sarcoma is only moderately sensitive to chemotherapy, and systemic responses are
  typically short-lived. An anthracycline-based systemic regimen is a standard cytotoxic option
  considered for unresectable or metastatic disease.
- The discovery that epithelioid sarcoma is characterized by near-universal loss of SMARCB1/INI1,
  leading to oncogenic EZH2 upregulation, led to trials of EZH2 inhibition. In a phase 2 basket
  study, the oral EZH2 inhibitor tazemetostat produced objective responses in a minority of
  patients with INI1-loss or biallelic SMARCB1-loss epithelioid sarcoma, which supported accelerated
  approval of tazemetostat for metastatic or unresectable epithelioid sarcoma. A confirmatory
  frontline study combining an EZH2 inhibitor with an anthracycline-based regimen is underway, so
  this combination should currently be considered investigational rather than standard outside a
  trial.
- For patients with unresectable or metastatic disease and confirmed INI1/SMARCB1 loss, an EZH2
  inhibitor (tazemetostat) is a genotype-directed systemic option; anthracycline-based chemotherapy
  remains an alternative or sequential option given the modest and often transient responses of this
  histology to cytotoxic therapy.
- Because response durability with either approach is limited, patients with locally advanced or
  metastatic disease should be evaluated for clinical trial enrollment, ideally at a sarcoma referral
  center.

## Monitoring and follow-up

- Prognosis is generally guarded: reported series show meaningful rates of local recurrence and
  distant relapse even after localized-disease treatment, with higher tumor grade and deep
  anatomic location associated with worse survival.
- Because both local recurrence and delayed nodal or distant metastasis are common, patients need
  prolonged surveillance with cross-sectional imaging of the primary site, regional nodal basins,
  and the chest (and other likely metastatic sites) well beyond the first few years after treatment.
- Any new nodule, non-healing lesion, or regional lymphadenopathy during follow-up should prompt
  prompt re-biopsy or imaging, given the tumor's propensity for both local and nodal recurrence.

## When to refer

- Refer to a sarcoma-specialist surgical/oncology multidisciplinary team at diagnosis for margin
  planning, given the need for extensive excision along fascial planes.
- Refer for confirmatory INI1 (SMARCB1) immunohistochemistry (or molecular confirmation of
  SMARCB1 loss) whenever the diagnosis is suspected, since this determines eligibility for EZH2-
  inhibitor therapy.
- Refer to medical oncology (ideally with sarcoma trial access) for any unresectable, recurrent, or
  metastatic disease, given the limited durability of currently available systemic options.

DeVita is silent on optimal surveillance imaging intervals and on any adjuvant/neoadjuvant
chemotherapy regimen specific to localized epithelioid sarcoma; those points are intentionally not
stated as specific recommendations here.

Sources: DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of Oncology, 12th ed.
