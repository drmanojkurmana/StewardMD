# Merkel Cell Carcinoma (MCC) — Management Narrative

## Overview and staging framework

Merkel cell carcinoma is an aggressive cutaneous neuroendocrine cancer with a marked propensity for local recurrence, regional nodal spread, and distant metastasis, so it warrants aggressive multidisciplinary management and a systemic workup for disseminated disease from the outset. Baseline evaluation should include a full-body skin and lymph node examination, complete blood count, and liver function tests, with cross-sectional or PET imaging used to assess regional and distant spread. AJCC8 staging separates localized disease with no evidence of regional or distant metastasis (stage I for tumors up to 2 cm, stage IIA/IIB for larger or deeply invasive tumors) from any nodal involvement (stage III) and distant metastatic disease (stage IV); prognosis tracks closely with pathologic stage, and the gap in survival between node-negative and pathologically node-positive disease underlines why staging the nodal basin properly changes management.

## Local and regional therapy

- Complete surgical excision of the primary tumor, generally wide local excision with margins in the 1 to 2 cm range, is the mainstay of treating localized disease; clear margins are associated with better local control, though the truly optimal margin width is not firmly established.
- Mohs micrographic surgery has been proposed as an alternative to wide local excision, particularly in cosmetically sensitive sites, with local control that appears comparable, though it has been used in comparatively few reported cases and has not been tested in randomized trials.
- Sentinel lymph node biopsy is recommended in clinically and radiologically node-negative disease because a meaningful proportion of patients are otherwise understaged, and a positive sentinel node carries substantially worse recurrence-free survival; definitive excision should be coordinated with, not before, sentinel node biopsy so the surgery does not alter lymphatic drainage.
- For patients with regional nodal disease, decisions about completion lymph node dissection versus nodal radiotherapy (or both) should go through multidisciplinary tumor board discussion; outcomes with radiation and with complete nodal dissection appear broadly similar, and adding adjuvant radiation after a complete dissection has not shown a clear additional benefit. Patients who decline or are not candidates for sentinel node biopsy should be considered for adjuvant nodal radiotherapy instead.
- Adjuvant radiotherapy to the primary site is considered for tumors with a significant risk of local recurrence and for those with positive margins, reflecting the tumor's radiosensitivity; radiation alone (without surgery), reserved for patients who are not surgical candidates, achieves site control less reliably and is associated with a higher risk of distant recurrence and worse survival than complete surgical resection.

## Systemic therapy

- For metastatic or unresectable disease, PD-1/PD-L1 checkpoint immunotherapy is first-line, standard-of-care systemic treatment; response rates are higher in treatment-naive patients than in those who have already had chemotherapy. Anti-PD-L1 (avelumab) and anti-PD-1 (pembrolizumab) antibodies have both shown durable objective responses in this setting, including complete responses in a meaningful minority of patients, with immune-related adverse events an expected trade-off.
- Cytotoxic chemotherapy (regimens drawing on agents used for small-cell lung carcinoma, i.e. platinum- and anthracycline-based combinations) is used mainly for palliation in patients who are not candidates for or have progressed on checkpoint immunotherapy; it has not been shown to confer a survival benefit, and reported response rates and survival at three years are considerably worse than what checkpoint inhibition achieves in comparable populations.
- Neoadjuvant checkpoint immunotherapy before surgery is an area of active investigation for resectable disease, with early studies showing a high rate of pathologic complete response, but larger phase III comparisons against surgery-plus-adjuvant-therapy alone are still needed before this becomes a standard approach (general oncology standard framing of this as investigational, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Monitoring and follow-up

- Because MCC carries a high risk of local, regional, and distant recurrence, follow-up should include skin and lymph node examination on a schedule of every few months for the first two to three years, spacing out to roughly annual or semiannual visits thereafter.
- Merkel-cell polyomavirus oncoprotein antibody titers, when elevated at baseline, fall after effective treatment; a rising titer on surveillance has a reasonably strong positive predictive value for clinically evident recurrence and can prompt earlier imaging.
- Reduction of immunosuppression where feasible, and evaluation for underlying immunosuppressive conditions (HIV, chronic lymphocytic leukemia, transplant status), is part of ongoing management given how strongly immune status drives both risk and behavior of this cancer (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease, though consistent with the immunosuppression risk factors DeVita's section does describe).

## When to refer

- Any biopsy-confirmed MCC should prompt referral to a multidisciplinary skin cancer or surgical oncology service before definitive treatment, given the need to sequence sentinel node biopsy correctly relative to excision and to plan margins.
- Any nodal involvement (clinically, radiologically, or on sentinel node biopsy) or deep invasion (muscle, fascia, cartilage, or bone) should trigger tumor board discussion involving surgical oncology and radiation oncology before a nodal-basin treatment plan is finalized.
- Locally advanced, unresectable, or metastatic disease should be referred promptly to medical oncology for consideration of checkpoint immunotherapy, since outcomes are meaningfully better with earlier, treatment-naive use of these agents.
- Patients with unexplained rapidly growing skin nodules who are immunosuppressed (transplant recipients, HIV, hematologic malignancy) warrant an expedited biopsy and referral given the disproportionately aggressive course of MCC in this group.
