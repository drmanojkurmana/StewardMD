# Mesothelioma: Management

## Overview and initial approach

Management of malignant pleural mesothelioma (MPM) is genuinely controversial, and every guideline
acknowledges disagreement between consensus statements on the role of aggressive surgery. Because of
this, all patients should undergo multidisciplinary discussion involving medical oncology, thoracic
surgery, and radiation oncology, with consideration of referral to a high-volume mesothelioma center.
Patient selection for any surgical approach is driven mainly by performance status, cardiopulmonary
reserve, and histologic subtype, since clinical staging (imaging, endobronchial ultrasound,
mediastinoscopy) correlates poorly with actual pathologic stage.

Treatment intent and modality selection depend heavily on three factors: whether disease is resectable
to a macroscopic complete resection, tumor histology (epithelioid versus biphasic/sarcomatoid), and
whether treatment is being delivered with curative-multimodality intent versus for unresectable/
metastatic disease.

## Role of surgery

Two operations dominate: extrapleural pneumonectomy (EPP), which removes the pleura, involved lung,
and usually the pericardium and diaphragm, and pleurectomy/decortication (P/D, or an extended form,
EPD), which removes the pleura while sparing the lung. A true microscopically negative (R0) resection
is generally not achievable in pleural mesothelioma.

- The only completed randomized trial of surgery, the MARS trial, did not show a survival benefit
  for EPP over chemotherapy alone, and outcomes were worse with EPP in that trial. This is a genuinely
  contested finding (small sample, high perioperative mortality in the surgical arm) but remains the
  only randomized evidence to date.
- Reported operative mortality is higher with EPP than with P/D or EPD, and most centers have shifted
  toward P/D/EPD as the more commonly performed operation for that reason, even without a randomized
  trial directly comparing survival between the two approaches.
- Only a highly selected subgroup, younger patients with epithelioid histology and no nodal
  metastases, appears to gain long-term survival benefit from EPP in registry data.
- Purely palliative procedures (partial pleurectomy or video-assisted thoracoscopic pleurodesis) are
  used to control recurrent pleural effusion or re-expand an entrapped lung, not for oncologic
  resection.
- A follow-up randomized trial (MARS 2) is specifically testing lung-sparing extended P/D after
  induction chemotherapy against chemotherapy alone, reflecting that this question remains open.

## Multimodality therapy for resectable/potentially resectable disease

Surgery alone is considered insufficient; systemic chemotherapy (an antifolate-platinum doublet, see
below) as an adjunct to surgery is used for clinical stage I to III disease. Sequencing (induction
chemotherapy before surgery versus adjuvant chemotherapy after surgery) is unresolved, and some
centers are now moving away from routine induction chemotherapy for early-stage patients in whom an
excellent surgical resection is anticipated. Intracavitary approaches (chemotherapy lavage after
resection, heated intraperitoneal chemotherapy for peritoneal disease, photodynamic therapy) remain
investigational, without randomized confirmation of added benefit over standard care. Combining
adjuvant hemithoracic radiation with induction chemotherapy and EPP has produced longer survival in
patients who complete the full sequence, but the regimen carries meaningful perioperative risk, and
newer approaches (e.g., short-course accelerated hypofractionated radiation immediately before
surgery) are still investigational and should be reserved for specialized centers. Neoadjuvant
checkpoint-inhibitor-based approaches before attempted resection are being studied in trials and are
not yet standard of care outside a study.

## Systemic therapy for unresectable/metastatic disease

**First line.** Histology now drives first-line choice.

- For biphasic or sarcomatoid histology, dual immunotherapy (a PD-1 inhibitor plus a CTLA-4 inhibitor)
  is the recommended frontline approach, based on a randomized trial showing a clear overall-survival
  benefit over a platinum-antifolate doublet, with the largest benefit seen specifically in
  non-epithelioid histology.
- For epithelioid histology, the choice between frontline dual immunotherapy and an antifolate-based
  platinum doublet is more individualized; because immunotherapy can take longer to produce a
  response, patients with bulky or rapidly threatening disease may still be treated with chemotherapy
  (with or without an anti-VEGF antibody) first.
- The antifolate-platinum doublet (pemetrexed-class antifolate plus a platinum agent) established
  chemotherapy as standard of care and remains a first-line option; carboplatin can substitute for
  cisplatin when cisplatin is not tolerated, and single-agent antifolate therapy is an option for
  patients unable to tolerate any platinum agent.
- Adding an anti-VEGF monoclonal antibody to the antifolate-platinum doublet improved overall survival
  in one large randomized trial and is included as a frontline option in some guidelines, though it
  never received formal regulatory approval and adoption outside a few countries has been limited.
- A tumor-treating-fields device applied to the torso alongside chemotherapy received a device-specific
  regulatory approval based on a single-arm trial, but this has not been tested in a randomized trial
  and should be regarded as a less firmly established option (general oncology standard caveat: device
  therapies of this kind are not part of core NCCN chemotherapy algorithms and availability/insurance
  coverage varies) (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Maintenance antifolate therapy after completing first-line chemotherapy did not clearly improve
  progression-free survival in the one randomized trial that addressed this question, which closed
  early for poor accrual.

**Second line and beyond (salvage).** There is no single agreed standard regimen, and choice depends
on what was used first line.

- Patients previously treated with an antifolate-platinum doublet who have not yet received
  immunotherapy should be offered a PD-1 inhibitor (alone or combined with a CTLA-4 inhibitor), or a
  PD-1 inhibitor in PD-L1-positive disease.
- Patients previously treated with frontline dual immunotherapy should be offered an antifolate-platinum
  doublet, with or without an anti-VEGF antibody, as salvage.
- Cytotoxic options such as gemcitabine or vinorelbine remain reasonable salvage choices, and a
  randomized trial found that adding an anti-VEGF-receptor antibody to gemcitabine improved survival
  compared with gemcitabine alone in the salvage setting, though this needs confirmation in larger
  studies.
- Single-agent CTLA-4 blockade alone has not shown a survival benefit in mesothelioma and should not be
  used as monotherapy.
- Because a reliable predictive biomarker for immunotherapy benefit in mesothelioma has not been
  established, essentially all mesothelioma patients without a contraindication to immunotherapy
  (e.g., active significant autoimmune disease, poor performance status, or prior severe
  immune-related toxicity) should be considered for an immune checkpoint inhibitor regimen at some
  point during their treatment course.
- Targeted therapy has a very limited role: rare case reports of ALK-fusion-positive mesothelioma have
  responded to ALK inhibition, and BAP1-deficient tumors have shown some activity with a PARP
  inhibitor in early trial data, but neither is applicable to the majority of patients and both remain
  investigational rather than standard practice.
- Cellular therapies (CAR-T cells targeting mesothelin, dendritic-cell approaches) and mesothelin-
  directed antibody constructs remain experimental, with efficacy limited by poor tumor penetration,
  short cell persistence, and in some cases treatment-related toxicity; none of these are standard of
  care.

## Role of radiotherapy

Radiotherapy for mesothelioma is rarely curative because delivering a curative dose to the large,
irregular pleural surface risks unacceptable normal-tissue toxicity. Its role is better established as
an adjunct to surgery than as sole treatment:

- Database evidence on radiotherapy's role is a mix of different comparisons rather than a single
  consistent answer: a large registry analysis of surgery with adjuvant radiotherapy versus surgery
  alone did not show an added survival benefit from the radiotherapy, while a separate large registry
  analysis of definitive radiotherapy (without surgery) versus no definitive treatment suggested a
  significant survival benefit for the group that received radiotherapy.
- Adjuvant radiotherapy following surgical resection has been associated with improved survival in
  stage I/II disease in some analyses, but this benefit was not seen in stage III/IV disease.
- Postoperative radiotherapy following EPP, delivered to the entire hemithorax, has produced favorable
  local control in phase II experience, though pulmonary toxicity (including fatal radiation
  pneumonitis in some series) is a recognized and serious risk.
- As lung-sparing P/D has become more common, techniques delivering intensity-modulated radiation to
  the pleura and chest wall while sparing the intact ipsilateral lung (sometimes called hemithoracic
  pleural IMRT) have been developed specifically to reduce this toxicity. Evidence on whether this
  improves survival compared with older RT techniques is mixed: broad database comparisons have not
  shown a clear difference, but at least one single-center retrospective series in patients treated
  with P/D reported significantly better survival with the newer IMRT-based technique than with
  conventional postoperative radiotherapy. This remains an area of active technique development rather
  than a settled comparison.
- Combining chemotherapy with definitive (non-postoperative) radiotherapy should be considered
  experimental and is not established practice for mesothelioma.

## Monitoring

DeVita's discussion of mesothelioma centers on staging, surgical patient selection, and systemic
therapy trial data rather than a structured surveillance schedule; a specific post-treatment imaging
and follow-up interval schedule was not found in the section reviewed. In general oncology practice,
patients are followed with periodic clinical assessment and cross-sectional chest imaging to detect
local recurrence or new pleural/peritoneal disease, with the response assessed by imaging during active
systemic therapy (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## When to refer

Given the controversy over surgical management and the technical demands of EPP, EPD, and
hemithoracic radiotherapy, any patient being considered for a surgical or multimodality curative-intent
approach should be referred to, or discussed with, a high-volume thoracic surgical and multidisciplinary
mesothelioma center before treatment begins. Patients with unresectable disease should have access to
medical oncology input on histology-based first-line selection (chemotherapy versus dual
immunotherapy) and to clinical trials given the still-evolving frontline and salvage treatment
landscape.

Sources: DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of Oncology, 12th ed.
