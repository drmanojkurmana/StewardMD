# Low-grade fibromyxoid sarcoma - management

## Diagnostic confirmation before treatment planning
Low-grade fibromyxoid sarcoma (LGFMS, also called low-grade fibromyxoid tumor or Evans tumor) typically
presents as a slow-growing deep soft-tissue mass of the trunk or extremities in patients in their third
and fourth decades of life. Because the tumor is cytologically bland, with rare mitoses and atypia, it is
easily mistaken for a benign lesion on routine histology. Diagnosis should be confirmed with MUC4
immunohistochemistry, which is positive in the great majority of cases as a downstream consequence of the
driver fusion, and, where available, molecular testing for the FUS-CREB3L2 fusion (present in over 90
percent of tumors) or the rarer FUS-CREB3L1 / EWSR1-CREB3L1 alternatives. Getting this right up front
matters because the deceptively bland appearance can lead to under-treatment of a tumor that is fully
malignant and has genuine metastatic potential.

## Localized disease
- Complete surgical resection with negative margins is the primary treatment (general oncology standard,
  not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Cases with positive or close margins, or where adequate re-excision is not feasible, are considered for
  adjuvant radiotherapy (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- All cases should be managed through a specialist sarcoma multidisciplinary team given the rarity and
  the risk of misdiagnosis (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Neoadjuvant treatment has no established role for this histology in the material reviewed; treatment
  planning centers on surgery, with radiotherapy reserved for margin concerns as above.

## Role of systemic therapy
LGFMS is a low-proliferation, translocation-driven sarcoma, and conventional cytotoxic chemotherapy has a
limited role in this histology; systemic therapy is reserved for unresectable or metastatic disease
(general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease, though consistent with the
tumor's low-grade, low-mitotic biology described in DeVita). There is no disease-specific chemotherapy
regimen or targeted agent for LGFMS grounded in the material reviewed; when systemic treatment is needed
for metastatic disease, a soft-tissue-sarcoma-type cytotoxic regimen chosen by the treating sarcoma
oncologist is the general approach (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this
disease). No drug names, classes, or schedules for this can be stated as disease-specific because none
were located in DeVita's discussion of LGFMS.

## Metastatic and recurrent disease
The defining clinical feature of LGFMS, and the one most explicitly grounded in DeVita, is its capacity
for late pulmonary metastasis, which can arise even decades after resection of the primary tumor. This is
why long-term, not just short- or medium-term, follow-up is required. When distant relapse occurs:
- Isolated, resectable pulmonary metastases are reasonable candidates for pulmonary metastasectomy
  (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease, but consistent with the
  indolent, metastasectomy-amenable pattern of relapse DeVita describes for this tumor).
- Widespread or unresectable metastatic disease should be escalated to systemic oncology for
  individualized management; no specific regimen for this scenario is grounded in the material reviewed.
- Local recurrence, like the primary tumor, should be re-resected with attention to margins, again through
  a sarcoma multidisciplinary team (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this
  disease).

A related but more aggressive entity, sclerosing epithelioid fibrosarcoma (SEF), shares MUC4 expression
and overlapping fusion biology with LGFMS and metastasizes in roughly half of cases, involving bone,
pleura, and brain in addition to lung; DeVita notes that a subset of tumors with hybrid histology suggest
either a related form or progression from LGFMS to SEF. This is relevant when a resected or recurrent
specimen shows areas of higher-grade or more infiltrative histology, since it changes prognosis and may
warrant more aggressive management (drawn from DeVita's discussion of the LGFMS/SEF spectrum).

## Monitoring and follow-up
- Long-term surveillance imaging of the primary site and the chest is required specifically because local
  recurrence and pulmonary metastasis in this tumor can present many years, even decades, after initial
  treatment. Surveillance should not be curtailed on the assumption that a long disease-free interval
  means cure.
- Any new or growing pulmonary nodule in a patient with a history of LGFMS, however remote in time,
  warrants prompt evaluation for metastatic disease rather than being dismissed as incidental.
- Pathologic review of any recurrence for areas of increased cellularity or higher-grade
  transformation is warranted, given the recognized LGFMS-to-SEF spectrum.

## When to refer
Refer to a specialist sarcoma multidisciplinary team at diagnosis, given the rarity of the tumor, the
risk of missing the diagnosis on routine histology, and the need for coordinated margin-focused surgery
and, where indicated, radiotherapy (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this
disease). Refer to sarcoma medical oncology for any unresectable primary, metastatic disease, or a
recurrence showing higher-grade or SEF-like transformation.

Sources: DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of Oncology, 12th ed.
