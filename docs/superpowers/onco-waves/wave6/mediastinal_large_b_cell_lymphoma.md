# Primary mediastinal large B-cell lymphoma (PMBCL / PMBL) — management narrative

## Overview relevant to treatment planning
PMBL is a distinct clinical entity within the diffuse large B-cell lymphoma category, arising in the anterior mediastinum. It has a female predominance with a median age of about 40 years, and most patients present with stage I/II bulky mediastinal disease. Pleural or pericardial effusions are seen in roughly half of patients, superior vena cava syndrome is a frequent complication, and an elevated LDH and B symptoms are common at presentation.

## Treatment approach by stage and intent
Because the great majority of patients present with limited-stage (I/II) but bulky disease confined largely to the mediastinum, treatment is delivered with curative intent using systemic chemoimmunotherapy as the backbone, with radiotherapy reserved as a consolidative option rather than a routine component. There is no surgical role in the definitive treatment of PMBL; biopsy is diagnostic only (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease, though consistent with the fibrotic, diffusely infiltrative growth pattern described there).

### First-line (frontline) systemic therapy
- The first-line therapy for PMBL consists of anthracycline-based chemoimmunotherapy combined with an anti-CD20 antibody.
- A dose-adjusted etoposide-prednisone-vincristine-cyclophosphamide-doxorubicin regimen given together with an anti-CD20 antibody (referred to as dose-adjusted EPOCH plus rituximab) has been reported, without radiotherapy, to produce outstanding progression-free and overall survival outcomes in this disease.
- Retrospective comparisons of a standard CHOP-based regimen plus radiotherapy against the dose-adjusted EPOCH-based regimen have shown broadly similar outcomes between the two approaches.
- While a prospective randomized comparison between these approaches is lacking, the dose-adjusted EPOCH-based, radiotherapy-sparing approach has become a de facto standard of care for this disease.

### Role of radiotherapy
The dose-adjusted EPOCH-based regimen was studied without radiotherapy and produced excellent outcomes. In practice, omitting radiotherapy after this regimen is generally guided by a response-adapted, PET-based assessment showing complete metabolic response, rather than treated as an automatic rule (general clinical inference, not from DeVita's section on this disease). Radiotherapy remains an option, and outcomes with a CHOP-based regimen plus radiotherapy have been reported as similar to the radiotherapy-sparing regimen. In the relapsed or refractory setting, radiation can be used to salvage incomplete responses or localized relapses.

### Relapsed or refractory disease
- Chemotherapy-refractory or relapsed PMBL generally carries a poor prognosis. Second-line chemotherapy often fails to bridge patients to autologous stem cell transplant, and relapse rates remain high even among those who do reach transplant.
- CD19-directed CAR T-cell therapy (axicabtagene ciloleucel and lisocabtagene maraleucel) is approved for relapsed or refractory PMBL, based on favorable responses seen in this subgroup within the pivotal CAR T-cell trials for aggressive B-cell lymphoma.
- The anti-PD-1 antibody pembrolizumab has been tested in this disease and is associated with a meaningful overall response rate, though complete responses are few; responses that do occur tend to be durable.
- The anti-CD30 antibody-drug conjugate brentuximab has been tested but produced a low response rate as a single agent.
- Combined PD-1 inhibition with CD30-directed therapy (nivolumab plus brentuximab) has been reported to produce higher response rates in single-arm data than those reported for either agent alone, in relapsed or refractory PMBL (cross-study observation, not a head-to-head comparison).
- Because PMBL shares biological overlap with classic Hodgkin lymphoma, including amplification of the 9p24 region driving JAK-STAT signalling and PD-L1/PD-L2 expression, checkpoint blockade has a rational and clinically demonstrated role in the relapsed setting.

## Monitoring
- PET-CT is used for staging and, importantly, for end-of-treatment response assessment, because a residual mediastinal mass is common after treatment of a bulky anterior mediastinal tumour and functional imaging is needed to distinguish residual fibrosis from active disease (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Ongoing surveillance after completion of therapy should follow standard aggressive lymphoma follow-up principles, with attention to late effects of any radiotherapy given, such as long-term cardiac and, in young women, breast cancer surveillance (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Superior vena cava syndrome and any airway or haemodynamic compromise from bulky mediastinal disease should be assessed for at presentation and monitored during induction (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## When to refer
- All patients with a newly diagnosed or suspected anterior mediastinal mass require prompt referral to haemato-oncology for tissue diagnosis and staging, given the risk of superior vena cava obstruction or airway compromise with bulky disease (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Patients with chemotherapy-refractory or relapsed disease should be referred early to a centre with access to CAR T-cell therapy and clinical trials of checkpoint blockade or CD30-directed therapy, given the poor prognosis of relapsed disease with conventional second-line chemotherapy and the specific approvals of CAR T-cell products in this setting.

## What DeVita does not address for this disease
DeVita's section on PMBL does not discuss maintenance therapy, specific systemic therapy regimen choice for older or frail patients, or granular staging criteria beyond noting the predominance of stage I/II bulky presentation; these points have deliberately been omitted rather than filled in with assumptions.
