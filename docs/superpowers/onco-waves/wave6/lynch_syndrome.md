# Lynch Syndrome - Management

Lynch syndrome (hereditary non-polyposis colorectal cancer, HNPCC) is a germline mismatch-repair
(MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2) or EPCAM deletion disorder. Management is split between risk reduction in
unaffected or previously-treated carriers and treatment of an established Lynch-spectrum cancer once
it occurs. There is no single "stage" for the syndrome itself, so the approach below is organised by
what is actually being managed: colorectal cancer risk, gynaecologic cancer risk, an established
mismatch-repair-deficient (dMMR) tumour, and lifelong surveillance.

## Colorectal cancer risk reduction

- Regular colonoscopy with polypectomy is the backbone of colorectal risk reduction in carriers; DeVita
  discusses Lynch syndrome as the setting for up to one-third of microsatellite-instability-high (MSI-hi)
  colorectal cancers, underlining why endoscopic surveillance and removal of adenomas before they
  progress is central to reducing incidence in this population.
- Aspirin chemoprevention has trial-level support that is specific to Lynch syndrome: DeVita cites the
  CAPP2 study, a randomised double-blind trial in which patients with confirmed Lynch syndrome were
  assigned to aspirin or placebo and showed a significantly decreased incidence of colorectal cancer in
  the aspirin group, with a trend toward fewer extracolonic Lynch-associated cancers and no significant
  increase in gastrointestinal bleeding, ulcers, or anaemia. This is described as providing a rationale
  for routine aspirin chemoprevention in Lynch syndrome.
- When a colorectal cancer does arise in a Lynch carrier, an extended (rather than segmental)
  colectomy is favoured because of the ongoing risk of metachronous cancer elsewhere in the colon
  (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Gynaecologic cancer risk reduction

- Women with Lynch syndrome carry a substantial lifetime risk of endometrial cancer, reported by
  DeVita as 40% to 60% for MLH1 or MSH2 carriers, 16% to 20% for MSH6 carriers, and about 15% for
  PMS2 carriers, a risk that equals or exceeds their colorectal cancer risk; endometrial cancers in
  this population present at a younger mean age than sporadic cases.
- Roughly 15% of Lynch syndrome patients carry meaningful ovarian cancer risk, and DeVita notes that
  because these same women also carry a high lifetime endometrial cancer risk, risk-reducing surgery
  in Lynch syndrome is directed at the uterus as well as the adnexa.
- The recommended risk-reducing approach is total abdominal hysterectomy with bilateral
  salpingo-oophorectomy once childbearing is complete, timed before the age at which most carriers
  would naturally reach menopause; DeVita specifically flags that Lynch syndrome carriers report
  significant sexual side effects after this risk-reducing surgery (vaginal dryness, pain with
  penetration, decreased arousal) and need dedicated counselling and support around this, separate
  from the surgery's oncologic benefit.
- Prophylactic surgical specimens should be processed carefully (serial sectioning, peritoneal
  washings) to detect occult malignancy, an approach described by DeVita for hereditary ovarian-risk
  surgery generally rather than specifically validated in a Lynch cohort.

## Treatment of an established mismatch-repair-deficient cancer

- Tumours arising in Lynch syndrome are mismatch-repair deficient and microsatellite-unstable. DeVita
  states that MSI-hi colorectal cancers resist adjuvant fluoropyrimidine-based chemotherapy and
  respond better to immunotherapy with PD-1 blockade than other colorectal cancers, a biological
  feature that is the rationale for preferring immune checkpoint inhibition over conventional adjuvant
  chemotherapy once a Lynch-associated dMMR cancer is diagnosed.
- Extending this dMMR/immunotherapy-responsiveness principle to other Lynch-spectrum cancers
  (endometrial, gastric, urothelial) beyond colorectal is standard oncology practice but was not
  independently grounded in the DeVita passages located for this disease (general oncology standard,
  not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- For upper-tract urothelial cancer specifically, DeVita notes Lynch syndrome as the third most common
  associated malignancy with a 6% lifetime risk, and recommends that patients presenting with upper
  tract urothelial cancer under age 60 to 65, or with a suggestive family or personal cancer history, be
  evaluated for Lynch syndrome, which is a diagnostic/case-finding point more than a treatment point
  but affects how such patients should be worked up.

## Extracolonic surveillance and referral

- Because risk extends beyond the colon and endometrium, gene- and family-history-directed
  surveillance of the upper gastrointestinal and urinary tracts is reasonable in carriers, with referral
  to a clinical geneticist for germline confirmation and cascade testing of relatives once a pathogenic
  variant is identified (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease; this
  reflects NCCN Genetic/Familial High-Risk Assessment guidance rather than a specific claim located in
  the DeVita text searched).
- Any Lynch carrier with a suspicious symptom in a Lynch-spectrum organ (unexplained uterine
  bleeding, haematuria, iron-deficiency anaemia, dyspepsia) should be referred promptly for
  organ-specific work-up rather than reassured on the basis of a recent negative surveillance test
  alone (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Referral to gynaecologic oncology is appropriate when a woman with Lynch syndrome is considering
  or approaching the age for risk-reducing hysterectomy/bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, given the
  procedure's oncologic benefit and the counselling needs around surgically induced menopause
  described above.

## What DeVita did not address for this disease

The DeVita passages located discuss Lynch syndrome mainly as a hereditary risk factor woven into the
colorectal, endometrial, ovarian, and urothelial cancer chapters, rather than as a single dedicated
"Lynch syndrome treatment" section. No specific systemic regimen, line-of-therapy sequencing, or
radiotherapy role for Lynch-associated cancers was found in the passages searched, so no claim about
regimen sequencing or radiotherapy indications has been made here beyond what is stated above; this
content should be treated as needing supplementation from NCCN Genetic/Familial High-Risk Assessment
guidance during clinician review.

Sources: DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of Oncology, 12th ed.
NCCN Guidelines: Genetic/Familial High-Risk Assessment - Colorectal.
