# Microcystic Adnexal Carcinoma (MAC) — Management

## Overview
Microcystic adnexal carcinoma is a rare, locally aggressive cutaneous adnexal carcinoma with a high rate of local recurrence but a low rate of distant metastasis. It favours the head and neck, particularly the central face, and is prone to perineural invasion with microscopic extension well beyond the clinically apparent margin. Because of this discrepancy between innocuous appearance and infiltrative behaviour, management is driven almost entirely by achieving complete local surgical clearance rather than by systemic therapy.

## Treatment approach by stage/intent

### Localized disease (the great majority of presentations)
- Complete surgical excision of the tumor is the standard of care whenever feasible.
- Because MAC frequently extends microscopically for centimeters beyond the visible tumor edge, wide local excision (WLE) margins reported in the literature range widely, from a few millimeters up to 3 to 5 cm, reflecting how unpredictable the subclinical extent can be.
- Mohs micrographic surgery (MMS) is favored over WLE where available, as it allows complete margin assessment and has shown substantially lower recurrence rates than WLE in the literature.
- In cases with clinical suspicion of deep infiltration or cranial nerve dysfunction, MRI can help delineate tumor extent before surgery, but imaging has low sensitivity for microscopic disease, so a negative scan does not rule out subclinical spread.

### When surgical margins are positive, surgery is not feasible, or as adjuvant treatment
- Radiotherapy has been used both as monotherapy and as adjuvant treatment for MAC, with reported success limited to case reports and small series rather than large trial data.
- When RT is given adjuvantly, it should be delivered with wide treatment margins to account for the tumor's infiltrative, perineural growth pattern.

### Metastatic / advanced disease
- Metastatic MAC is rare, and DeVita's section on this disease does not describe a systemic therapy approach for metastatic MAC; a systemic workup for occult metastatic disease is generally not required at diagnosis given how uncommon spread is.
- (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease) For the rare patient with unresectable, recurrent, or metastatic disease where locoregional options are exhausted, decisions about systemic therapy or clinical trial enrollment should be individualized at a center with head and neck / cutaneous oncology and multidisciplinary tumor board input, since no established regimen is described for this entity.

## Lines of therapy
There is no defined sequence of systemic "lines of therapy" for MAC in the source material; treatment is primarily a single, definitive local surgical intervention (WLE or MMS), with radiotherapy reserved for adjuvant use or for cases where surgery is incomplete or not possible. There is no established chemotherapy or targeted-therapy regimen for MAC grounded in this section.

## Role of surgery, radiotherapy, and systemic therapy
- Surgery (preferably MMS) is the primary and most effective treatment modality, given the marked difference in local recurrence rates between MMS and WLE reported in the literature.
- Adequately deep biopsy (not a superficial shave) is essential before treatment planning, since MAC is frequently misdiagnosed clinically and histologically and superficial sampling can miss the deeper, more diagnostic infiltrative component.
- Radiotherapy has a supportive role, either as adjuvant therapy after surgery or as monotherapy in patients who are not surgical candidates, but the evidence base for this is limited to small series and case reports.
- Systemic therapy has no defined role in MAC based on this section; metastasis is uncommon enough that routine systemic staging or treatment is not part of standard management.

## Monitoring
- Patients with MAC should undergo ongoing, lifelong examination of the skin and regional lymph nodes, because late local recurrence can occur decades after initial treatment.
- (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease) Surveillance intervals and use of imaging should be individualized based on excision margin status, perineural invasion, and anatomic site, since no specific surveillance schedule is given in this section.

## When to refer
- Refer to a Mohs surgeon or a center with Mohs micrographic surgery capability whenever feasible, given its favorable recurrence profile compared with WLE.
- Refer for radiation oncology evaluation when margins cannot be cleared surgically, when perineural invasion is extensive, or when RT is being considered as adjuvant or primary treatment.
- Refer to a multidisciplinary head and neck or cutaneous oncology team for cases with suspected deep invasion, cranial nerve involvement, or the rare case of confirmed metastatic disease, since no standardized systemic approach exists for these scenarios.
