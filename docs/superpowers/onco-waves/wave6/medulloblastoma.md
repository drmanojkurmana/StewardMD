# Medulloblastoma: Management

## Risk stratification drives everything

Management is organized around a risk split, not a stage split in the usual solid-tumor sense.
Patients are classified as average risk or high risk based on age, M stage (extent of
cerebrospinal fluid dissemination), extent of residual disease after surgery, and pathology.
Average-risk disease is M0 (no dissemination), arising in the posterior fossa, in a patient older
than 3 years, with less than 1.5 cm of residual tumor after resection. All anaplastic
medulloblastoma is treated as high risk regardless of these other features, and children under 3
years old carry a particularly poor prognosis, partly because radiotherapy is reduced or withheld
in this age group. Molecular subgroup (WNT-activated, SHH-activated, group 3, group 4) is
increasingly used alongside these clinical factors, since the four subgroups differ markedly in
prognosis and in whether they respond well to standard-dose therapy; WNT-activated tumors have
the most favorable outcome and group 3 the worst.

Staging requires an MRI of the whole neuraxis (ideally before surgery, or delayed 10 to 14 days
after surgery to avoid false-positive artifact from surgical debris) plus CSF cytology, because
20 to 30 percent of patients present with neuraxial spread that changes the treatment plan.
Because systemic (extra-CNS) metastasis is uncommon but occurs mostly to bone, a bone scan, chest
imaging, and bilateral marrow assessment are obtained in patients with more advanced M stage.

## Role of surgery

Surgery is the first step for essentially every patient. The goal is maximal safe resection:
residual tumor after surgery is a negative prognostic factor and pushes a patient toward more
intensive adjuvant treatment. Gross total or near-total resection is preferred, and even patients
left with minimal residual disease after a substantial resection do about as well as those with a
complete resection once they go on to combined chemotherapy and radiotherapy, so surgeons
reasonably favor a near-total resection over risking injury to the fourth ventricle floor, cranial
nerves, or the posterior inferior cerebellar artery. Extent of resection does not appear to affect
survival in patients who already have disseminated disease at diagnosis, so aggressive re-resection
purely to chase a complete resection is not indicated once metastatic spread is established.

Surgery also manages the obstructive hydrocephalus that is common at presentation; a
ventriculostomy can relieve pressure acutely, and tumor resection itself is usually sufficient to
resolve hydrocephalus, though a meaningful minority of patients (roughly a third) end up needing a
permanent shunt. Post-operative posterior fossa syndrome (cerebellar mutism, with diminished
speech, emotional lability, and other deficits) is a recognized surgical complication, seen in up
to a quarter of patients undergoing resection of a midline posterior fossa tumor, and can persist.

## Role of radiotherapy

Radiotherapy targets residual posterior fossa disease and any gross deposits along the
craniospinal axis, and also treats microscopic disease throughout the neuraxis given the tumor's
propensity to seed the CSF. In average-risk patients over 2 years old with a complete resection and
no dissemination, craniospinal irradiation with a boost to the posterior fossa or a smaller
involved field around the tumor bed, together with concurrent and then adjuvant chemotherapy, is
the established approach; a reduced-volume boost limited to the resection cavity and residual
disease (rather than the whole posterior fossa) has been shown not to compromise disease control
while reducing normal-tissue toxicity. Further dose reduction below this standard has produced
worse disease control in some subgroups (particularly group 4) even though it improves
neurocognitive outcomes, so subgroup-specific tailoring of radiation dose and volume is an area of
active refinement rather than settled practice, and WNT-activated average-risk disease in
particular is being studied for further de-escalation given its excellent control rates.

Radiotherapy carries substantial long-term toxicity in children, including hearing loss, cognitive
decline, brainstem injury, and endocrine dysfunction, which is the reason so much of the field's
effort goes into reducing dose, volume, or (in young children) avoiding radiation altogether.
Proton-based craniospinal irradiation reduces dose to normal tissue compared with photon
radiotherapy and has been associated with better preservation of measures such as IQ and working
memory, less endocrine and cardiopulmonary toxicity, and better preserved blood counts allowing
fuller delivery of concurrent chemotherapy, though a decline in processing speed over time is seen
with either technique.

In children younger than about 3 years, radiotherapy is delayed or omitted where possible because
of the severity of neurocognitive harm at this age, and chemotherapy-only or chemotherapy-plus-
delayed/limited-field-radiotherapy strategies are used instead, particularly in M0 disease; the
trade-off is that omitting radiation in this age group has, in some trial data, come at the cost of
worse disease control, and radiation delivery was the strongest positive prognostic factor in one
such trial. Adding limited conformal radiotherapy to the posterior fossa and primary site (rather
than radiation-free chemotherapy alone) has improved event-free survival in this age group without
an apparent decline in neurodevelopmental testing.

## Role of systemic (chemotherapy) therapy

Chemotherapy is used both to allow reduction of the radiation dose in average-risk patients while
preserving disease control, and to improve outcomes in high-risk patients. Randomized comparisons
of radiotherapy alone against radiotherapy plus chemotherapy established a survival benefit for
adding chemotherapy, and the combination is now standard for high-risk disease, and for average-
risk disease it enables a reduced craniospinal radiation dose without loss of disease control
compared with historical full-dose-radiation controls. A nitrosourea/platinum-based regimen with
vincristine following reduced-dose craniospinal irradiation has served as one such standard
reference approach in average-risk disease, and subsequent comparisons of different agents added
to reduced-dose craniospinal irradiation and vincristine have not shown one alkylating-agent-class
partner to be clearly superior to another.

For high-risk disease, current approaches emphasize more intensive, dose-intensified chemotherapy,
including regimens built around alkylating agents, platinum agents, and vincristine, and in some
protocols high-dose chemotherapy with autologous stem cell rescue. Outcomes in high-risk disease
remain inferior to average-risk disease overall, and anaplastic histology within high-risk cohorts
has an even worse outcome than other high-risk disease.

For infants and very young children, multi-agent chemotherapy regimens (including, in some
protocols, intraventricular methotrexate) have been used specifically to delay or avoid
radiotherapy, accepting a real chance of somewhat lower disease control in exchange for preserved
cognitive outcome; this trade-off is most defensible in M0, non-metastatic disease and is less
favorable in patients with disseminated disease at diagnosis.

Recurrent medulloblastoma responds to a variety of cytotoxic agents (including vincristine,
nitrosoureas, procarbazine, alkylating agents, etoposide, and platinum agents), and responses can
be seen with high-dose chemotherapy and stem cell rescue at relapse, but relapsed disease is
regarded as essentially incurable, with response durability limited even when an initial response
is achieved. This is the point in the disease course where referral to a center with access to
molecularly-stratified clinical trials is most valuable, since targeted approaches directed at the
WNT, SHH, group 3, and group 4 biology are an active area of trial development rather than
established standard therapy (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this
disease).

## Monitoring and long-term follow-up

Response and surveillance monitoring rests on serial MRI of the brain and whole spine plus CSF
cytology when clinically indicated, mirroring the staging work-up. Post-operative MRI is best
obtained within about 48 to 72 hours of surgery to assess residual disease before post-surgical
change confuses the picture.

Because most patients are children who have received craniospinal radiotherapy and multi-agent
chemotherapy, long-term follow-up must screen proactively for late effects: neurocognitive decline
(which can progress for a decade or more after treatment, with the largest IQ declines in children
treated at a younger age), neuroendocrine dysfunction (including delayed hypothyroidism and growth
hormone deficiency, which needs endocrinology follow-up), growth retardation, hearing loss, and an
increased risk of second malignancy relative to the general population. These late effects are a
core part of survivorship care and are part of why treatment intensity is tailored, not simply
maximized, once disease control is adequate.

## When to refer

Given the intensity and rarity of this disease, essentially all patients should be managed at (or
in close coordination with) a center experienced in pediatric neuro-oncology, with access to
neurosurgery capable of maximal safe posterior fossa resection, craniospinal radiotherapy including
proton therapy where feasible, pediatric neuro-oncology for risk-adapted chemotherapy, and
long-term survivorship follow-up (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this
disease, though it follows directly from the toxicity and rarity data above). Relapsed disease,
and any patient being considered for reduced-intensity or radiation-delaying strategies in early
childhood, warrants referral to a center running molecularly stratified trials given the current
absence of an established salvage standard.
