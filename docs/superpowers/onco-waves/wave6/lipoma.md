## Treatment approach by presentation

Management of lipoma is stratified by clinical behaviour and imaging appearance rather than by
stage, since a simple lipoma is benign and does not have a "stage" or "intent" in the way a
malignancy does (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

- **Typical small, superficial, asymptomatic lipoma**: observation alone is appropriate. No biopsy,
  imaging, or excision is required once the lesion has the classic soft, mobile, painless
  subcutaneous feel and, if imaged, follows fat signal on all sequences with complete fat
  suppression and no thick septa or non-fatty nodules (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's
  section on this disease).
- **Symptomatic, enlarging, or cosmetically troublesome lesion**: marginal surgical excision is the
  standard treatment, and is curative in the great majority of cases (general oncology standard, not
  from DeVita's section on this disease).
- **Deep, large (roughly above ten centimetres), intramuscular, or radiologically atypical lesion**:
  before any excision, cross-sectional imaging (MRI with fat-saturated sequences) should be obtained
  to characterise the mass, followed by image-guided core biopsy if atypical features are present, to
  exclude an atypical lipomatous tumour or well-differentiated liposarcoma. These lesions should not
  simply be "shelled out" (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- **Confirmed atypical lipomatous tumour or well-differentiated liposarcoma on biopsy**: this is a
  different diagnostic and management pathway from simple lipoma and warrants referral to a
  specialist sarcoma service for planned wider excision, since these lesions carry a risk of local
  recurrence and, at certain sites, dedifferentiation (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's
  section on this disease).

## Lines of therapy

There are no systemic lines of therapy for simple lipoma. It is a benign tumour and is not treated
with chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or targeted/hormonal agents. Surgery (marginal excision) is the only
active treatment modality, reserved for lesions that are symptomatic, enlarging, cosmetically
troublesome, or diagnostically uncertain (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on
this disease). This distinguishes lipoma sharply from liposarcoma, where multimodality treatment
including surgery, radiotherapy, and in some settings systemic therapy is used; that liposarcoma
pathway is not covered here since it is a separate disease entity.

## Role of surgery, radiotherapy, and systemic therapy

- **Surgery**: marginal excision is both diagnostic and therapeutic for a lesion behaving as a simple
  lipoma, and recurrence after complete excision is uncommon (general oncology standard, not from
  DeVita's section on this disease).
- **Radiotherapy**: has no role in the management of a confirmed simple lipoma.
- **Systemic therapy**: has no role in the management of a confirmed simple lipoma.

## Monitoring

- No routine surveillance imaging or follow-up is required for a typical, imaging-confirmed simple
  lipoma that is left untreated (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this
  disease).
- After excision, the specimen should undergo histological examination to confirm the diagnosis; this
  is standard practice for any excised soft tissue mass rather than a lipoma-specific monitoring step
  (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Any new growth, pain, or change in a previously stable fatty mass should prompt re-imaging rather
  than reassurance, because these are the features that raise concern for an atypical lipomatous
  tumour or liposarcoma (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## When to refer

- Refer for surgical opinion when a lipoma is symptomatic, enlarging, cosmetically troublesome, or
  diagnostically uncertain on imaging (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this
  disease).
- Refer to a specialist sarcoma multidisciplinary service whenever imaging or biopsy raises concern
  for an atypical lipomatous tumour or well-differentiated liposarcoma, particularly for deep,
  intramuscular, or large (roughly above ten centimetres) fatty masses, since these should not be
  managed with straightforward local excision outside that pathway (general oncology standard, not
  from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Refer for genetics or syndrome assessment when lipomas are multiple, since this can be a clue to
  familial multiple lipomatosis or syndromic associations such as Gardner syndrome,
  Bannayan-Riley-Ruvalcaba syndrome, or benign symmetric (Madelung) lipomatosis (general oncology
  standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
