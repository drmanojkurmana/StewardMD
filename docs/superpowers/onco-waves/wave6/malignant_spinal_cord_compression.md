# Malignant Spinal Cord Compression (MSCC): Management Expansion

Aliases: MSCC, metastatic spinal cord compression, malignant epidural spinal cord compression.

## Overview and treatment intent

MSCC is an oncologic emergency in which epidural tumour (most often from a vertebral body metastasis extending posteriorly) compresses the thecal sac and cord, producing vasogenic oedema that can progress to irreversible infarction if untreated. Because neurological outcome is governed largely by the deficit present when treatment starts, the overriding intent at every stage is to preserve or restore ambulatory and sphincter function rather than to pursue oncologic cure of the compression itself; overall survival is instead determined by the underlying malignancy and its treatability.

## Step 1: establish histology before committing to therapy

If the patient has a known diagnosis of cancer, especially metastatic disease, treatment can be triaged directly without a new tissue diagnosis. If there is no cancer history, or only a remote early-stage history, a confirmatory tissue diagnosis (excisional or CT-guided needle biopsy, or surgical resection if biopsy is not feasible) should be obtained expeditiously before treatment, because histology determines both radiosensitivity and the appropriateness of surgery. A recognised pitfall is starting corticosteroids before tissue is obtained when lymphoma is a possibility, because steroids can lyse tumour and obscure the histologic diagnosis.

## Step 2: corticosteroids on suspicion

Once tissue has been secured, or immediately if the patient already has known metastatic cancer, corticosteroid therapy (a glucocorticoid such as dexamethasone) should be started without waiting for pathology, because steroids reduce vasogenic cord oedema and pain and may limit further cord damage from impaired perfusion. Oral dosing is well absorbed; intravenous administration is reserved for patients unable to swallow. Very high "trauma-protocol" steroid regimens are not recommended given the lack of consistent benefit and their associated complications, and a trial comparing a low-dose bolus with a much higher-dose bolus found no difference in pain, ambulation, or bladder outcomes, so more steroid is not better once compression is being treated urgently. Gastroprotection alongside corticosteroids is standard practice (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Step 3: assess prognosis, performance status, and systemic options

Before choosing a definitive local treatment, performance status, life expectancy, disease burden, and the availability of effective systemic therapy must be assessed. In patients with poor performance status, poor prognosis, few remaining treatment options, and widely metastatic disease, a conservative approach (short-course radiotherapy or a comfort-focused/hospice pathway) is often the appropriate choice. Conversely, patients with good performance status, an anticipated survival beyond roughly two months, a limited or moderate disease burden, and/or effective systemic treatment options available are the ones most likely to benefit from radiotherapeutic or neurosurgical intervention, because the long-term functional and quality-of-life cost of unresolved MSCC generally outweighs the burden of active treatment in this group.

## Step 4: the MNOP framework for definitive treatment

Once histology, steroids, and prognosis are addressed, treatment selection follows a multidisciplinary framework (the International Spine Oncology Consortium's MNOP algorithm) built around four questions:

- **Mechanical**: is the spine stable? An unstable spine is itself an oncologic emergency and generally requires surgical stabilisation; if the spine is unstable, primary radiotherapy alone (however radiosensitive the tumour) should not be used up front, with radiotherapy instead reserved as an adjuvant after stabilisation.
- **Neurologic**: how severe and how long-standing is the deficit? Acute deficits evolving over hours to days are more likely to recover with prompt decompression, whereas deficits that have been complete for more than about 72 hours are far less likely to improve with decompressive surgery, favouring radiotherapy alone to arrest further progression rather than surgery aimed at recovery.
- **Oncologic**: how radiosensitive is the histology? Tumours vary widely in response to conventional low-dose radiotherapy: lymphoma and multiple myeloma are highly radiosensitive (myeloma and lymphoma can achieve durable local control, and lymphoma can respond even to very low radiation doses), prostate and breast cancer are intermediate/favourable, non-small-cell lung cancer and gastrointestinal primaries are intermediate/unfavourable, and melanoma, sarcoma, and renal cell carcinoma are relatively radioresistant to conventional fractionation.
- **Preferred treatment**: synthesising the above, options are conventional external beam radiotherapy alone, surgical decompression (usually circumferential) followed by adjuvant radiotherapy, or "separation surgery" (debulking epidural tumour away from the cord) followed by stereotactic body radiotherapy for radioresistant histologies where high-dose radiation could not otherwise be delivered safely near the cord.

## Role of surgery

Surgical decompression aims to restore or preserve neurologic function in high-grade compression, and secondarily to stabilise a mechanically unstable spine, to create space between tumour and cord so that high-dose stereotactic radiotherapy can be delivered safely (separation surgery), to obtain a histologic diagnosis, or to provide local control when radiotherapy cannot be safely delivered. Surgery alone does not achieve durable control, so postoperative radiotherapy is almost always added. A randomised trial found direct decompressive surgery plus adjuvant radiotherapy produced better ambulatory outcomes and survival than radiotherapy alone, though a separate matched analysis found elderly patients did not show the same benefit from adding surgery, underscoring that the surgery-versus-radiotherapy-alone decision is individualised rather than uniform. Surgery may not be appropriate for patients with a life expectancy under about three months, and perioperative risk is higher with advanced age, prior radiation, diffuse spinal disease, poor performance status, or multiple comorbidities; in such patients, less invasive stabilising procedures (vertebroplasty, kyphoplasty, percutaneous pedicle screws) can relieve mechanical pain without attempting decompression.

## Role of radiotherapy

Conventional external beam radiotherapy is delivered in a range of fractionation schedules and remains the mainstay for many patients, particularly those unfit for surgery or with radiosensitive histology. Multifraction regimens are associated with better durable local control and a lower rate of needing retreatment than single-fraction regimens, though for patients with very poor performance status and short life expectancy, shorter, lower-dose schedules provide equivalent functional outcomes without added benefit from higher, longer courses. Stereotactic body radiotherapy delivers a substantially higher biologically effective dose than conventional radiotherapy and achieves durable local control even in radioresistant histologies (renal cell carcinoma, sarcoma), but because effective stereotactic doses exceed spinal cord tolerance, a margin of separation between tumour and cord is required; consequently stereotactic radiotherapy is not used as full-dose upfront treatment for high-grade (grade 2 to 3) compression, and its main role is adjuvant/postoperative treatment after separation surgery, or upfront treatment of low-grade compression (grade 0 is the ideal candidate, with select grade 1 cases also eligible).

## Role of systemic therapy

Systemic anticancer therapy (chemotherapy for chemosensitive histologies such as non-Hodgkin lymphoma, targeted agents such as EGFR inhibitors, or hormonal therapy for breast or prostate cancer) can be an appropriate treatment approach for selected patients with low-grade, minimally symptomatic compression, used alongside or sometimes instead of radiotherapy/surgery. Systemic therapy directed at myeloma, lymphoma, prostate, and breast cancer is broadly considered a reasonable adjunct in chemosensitive or hormone-sensitive disease (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease). Radionuclide therapy (for example radium-223) can treat and help prevent painful bone metastases generally, but its very short particle path length makes it ineffective for treating established epidural disease.

## Monitoring

Local control after radiotherapy depends on tumour radiosensitivity and dose delivered: radiosensitive histologies (germinoma, lymphoma, multiple myeloma) achieve high one-year local control with conventional radiotherapy, favourable-intermediate histologies (breast, prostate) can often be managed successfully with radiotherapy alone, while unfavourable-intermediate (non-small-cell lung cancer) and radioresistant histologies (sarcoma, melanoma, renal carcinoma) have consistently poorer long-term local control with conventional fractionation, which is a reason to favour stereotactic radiotherapy or surgery-plus-radiotherapy in these groups. Ongoing supportive care (analgesia for the biologic, mechanical, radicular, and funicular pain types seen in spine metastases, venous thromboembolism prophylaxis, bladder and bowel management, and pressure-area care) and early rehabilitation should run in parallel with definitive treatment, with neutral spinal alignment and log-roll precautions maintained until spinal stability is confirmed (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease, though consistent with the mechanical-instability principles above).

## When to refer / escalate

Grade 2 to 3 compression (spinal cord compression with or without obliteration of the surrounding cerebrospinal fluid space) constitutes an oncologic emergency and warrants urgent multidisciplinary input from spine surgery, radiation oncology, and medical oncology at diagnosis, in parallel with corticosteroid initiation and whole-spine imaging. An unstable spine, regardless of neurologic grade, is itself an indication for urgent surgical referral for stabilisation. New back pain in a patient with known or suspected cancer should be treated as spine metastasis with possible cord compression until proven otherwise and should prompt urgent imaging and specialist referral rather than being managed as routine musculoskeletal pain.

## What DeVita's chapter does not specify (and is deliberately omitted here)

Specific radiotherapy dose/fractionation numbers and named prognostic scoring tools are described in the chapter but are omitted from this narrative because they are either numeric-dose content (excluded by policy) or scoring-tool detail better captured in a structured calculator rather than free-text management prose. The Bilsky grade numbers (0 to 3) are retained above only where clinically load-bearing, such as the grade 2 to 3 emergency threshold and the grade 0 to 1 low-grade / candidate-for-upfront-stereotactic-radiotherapy distinction; the full Bilsky 0-3 grading scale and the Spinal Instability Neoplastic Score are otherwise left to a structured calculator rather than being reproduced here.
