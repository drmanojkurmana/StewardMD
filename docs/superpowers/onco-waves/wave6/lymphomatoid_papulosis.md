# Lymphomatoid Papulosis (LyP): Management

## Overview and intent of treatment
Lymphomatoid papulosis is a clonal but nonmalignant member of the primary cutaneous CD30-positive
lymphoproliferative disorder spectrum. It runs a chronic, waxing-and-waning course of papular,
papulonodular, or papulonecrotic lesions at different stages of development that typically
disappear within about twelve weeks, often leaving a scar. Because the disease is indolent and
self-limited by nature, the intent of treatment is control of symptomatic or cosmetically
troublesome disease and surveillance for an associated lymphoma, not cure of an aggressive
malignancy. Clinicopathological correlation is essential, since the histology (atypical CD30+
cells, and clonal T-cell receptor rearrangement in up to 60% of cases) can look alarming and can
closely mimic mycosis fungoides or primary cutaneous anaplastic large cell lymphoma (C-ALCL),
even though the clinical behaviour remains benign and self-regressing.

## Treatment approach by extent/severity
- **Limited, asymptomatic disease:** active observation with reassurance is an accepted approach,
  reflecting the excellent prognosis and self-regressing course.
- **Extensive or symptomatic disease:** low-dose oral methotrexate or phototherapy (PUVA) are the
  established skin-directed/systemic options used to suppress the eruption.
- **Individual troublesome lesions:** topical corticosteroids or topical retinoids can be used for
  localized lesions (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- **Refractory or highly symptomatic disease:** a CD30-targeted antibody-drug conjugate
  (brentuximab vedotin) has shown activity in CD30+ lymphoproliferative disorders including LyP,
  with roughly two-thirds of patients responding and about a third achieving complete remission in
  a phase II trial that combined LyP, C-ALCL, and CD30+ mycosis fungoides. Response did not
  correlate with the level of CD30 expression. Oral retinoids (e.g. bexarotene) are a further
  option for refractory disease (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this
  disease). Aggressive multiagent chemotherapy is not appropriate for this indolent condition and
  should be avoided.

## Role of surgery and radiotherapy
DeVita's section on LyP does not describe a role for surgical excision or radiotherapy in LyP
itself (that role is described for the related but distinct entity, primary cutaneous ALCL, where
surgical excision and/or local radiotherapy are standard for solitary lesions). If a LyP-spectrum
lesion instead behaves like C-ALCL (persistent, enlarging, not self-regressing), it should be
managed and staged as that entity rather than as LyP.

## Lines of therapy, summarized
1. Observation for limited or asymptomatic disease.
2. Low-dose methotrexate or PUVA phototherapy for extensive or symptomatic eruptions.
3. Topical corticosteroids or retinoids for individual lesions (general oncology standard, not
   from DeVita's section on this disease).
4. Brentuximab vedotin, or oral retinoids (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section
   on this disease), reserved for refractory or highly symptomatic disease, explicitly avoiding
   aggressive chemotherapy.

## Monitoring
Long-term follow-up is warranted because a clonal T-cell population can, in a minority of
patients, precede or accompany another lymphoma. In DeVita's own case series (118 isolated LyP
cases), only about 4% went on to develop systemic lymphoma, and elsewhere in the same chapter up
to 20% of LyP cases may be preceded by or followed by another lymphoma such as mycosis fungoides,
anaplastic large cell lymphoma, or Hodgkin lymphoma. Practical monitoring therefore includes:
- Lifelong clinical surveillance for evolution into, or coexistence with, mycosis fungoides,
  primary cutaneous ALCL, or Hodgkin lymphoma.
- Re-biopsy of any lesion that fails to regress, progressively enlarges, or otherwise departs from
  the typical waxing-and-waning pattern, since this raises concern for C-ALCL or an evolving
  lymphoma rather than LyP.
- Photographic mapping of the recurrent, migratory eruption to track disease pattern over time
  (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Disease-specific 5-year survival for LyP is reported at 100% in the WHO-EORTC outcomes data
  reproduced in this chapter, underscoring the indolent nature of the disease while surveillance
  continues.

## When to refer
Refer to dermatology and haemato-oncology for diagnostic confirmation (including histologic
subtyping and exclusion of C-ALCL or an associated lymphoma) and for long-term follow-up planning.
Escalate promptly if a lesion stops regressing and progressively enlarges, if new lymphadenopathy
or systemic symptoms develop, or if persistent plaques or tumours typical of mycosis fungoides
appear, since these findings point toward an associated lymphoma requiring its own staging and
treatment pathway rather than continued management as LyP.

Sources: DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of Oncology, 12th ed.
