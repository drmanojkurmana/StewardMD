# Thymoma - Management

## General approach

Thymomas are considered malignant thymic epithelial neoplasms even when histologically bland or slow growing, and multidisciplinary evaluation is central to management because surgery, radiation, and systemic therapy may each play a role depending on stage. Very few well-designed prospective trials exist in thymoma, so most treatment recommendations rest on retrospective series and expert consensus rather than randomized evidence.

Staging (Masaoka-Koga stage, or the newer TNM system) is the main driver of treatment intent: early-stage (Masaoka I/II) disease is treated with curative surgery alone in most cases, while locally advanced (Masaoka III/IV) disease requires a multimodality approach combining surgery, radiation, and chemotherapy.

## Early-stage disease (Masaoka stage I/II)

- Complete (R0) surgical resection, via open sternotomy or minimally invasive (thoracoscopic/robotic) thymectomy, is the mainstay of therapy and the single most important predictor of long-term survival.
- Complete/total thymectomy remains the recommended standard rather than partial thymectomy, given limited long-term outcome data for partial resection and a meaningful risk of later myasthenia gravis requiring reoperation.
- During surgery, the distinction between noninvasive thymomas (encapsulated, easily resected) and invasive thymomas (requiring en bloc resection of involved structures) matters for surgical planning even though it is not part of formal staging systems.
- Postoperative radiation therapy (PORT) is not recommended after complete resection of stage I tumors. For stage II tumors the evidence is mixed: some large series show no benefit, while other retrospective cohorts suggest an overall survival benefit, particularly when there is regional tumor spread, close/positive margins, or high-risk histology (higher grade, pleural invasion). PORT for stage II disease is therefore an individualized decision rather than routine.
- Cryoablation may be considered for patients with high surgical risk or who decline surgery but have reasonable life expectancy, though it is not well suited to tumors closely abutting high-flow vascular structures (incomplete ablation risk); careful expectant monitoring is an alternative for such patients given the generally slow growth of these tumors.
- Pre- or postoperative chemotherapy, targeted therapy, or immunotherapy is almost never indicated for stage I/II thymoma.

## Locally advanced / advanced disease (Masaoka stage III/IV)

- The role and extent of surgery is less well defined than in early-stage disease. Extended resection (including involved pericardium, phrenic nerve, chest wall, lung, or diaphragm as needed) can achieve R0 resection in many cases, at low operative mortality in experienced centers. Even incomplete (R1/R2) resection appears to improve survival compared with biopsy alone in multiple retrospective series, and degree of resection is a strong predictor of outcome.
- In stage IVA disease with pleural or pericardial spread but no distant metastases, aggressive options such as pleurectomy/decortication or extrapleural pneumonectomy have been used at high-volume thoracic centers, though supporting data are limited and this should be reserved for highly experienced surgical teams.
- Radiation therapy has a role in locally advanced disease, particularly after incomplete resection or for unresectable tumors, and PORT appears to improve survival in resected stage III/IV disease in several retrospective analyses, though the benefit after complete resection is less consistent. Response to radiation may be more pronounced in histologic subtypes with a greater lymphocytic component.
- Systemic chemotherapy (platinum-based combination regimens, most commonly built around a cisplatin plus anthracycline backbone) is used for stage III/IV thymoma, either as neoadjuvant/induction therapy before attempted resection, as postoperative/consolidative therapy, or as treatment for unresectable, recurrent, or metastatic disease. Combination regimens generally produce higher response rates than single agents.
- A multimodality sequence (induction chemotherapy, then surgery, then adjuvant radiation, with or without further consolidative chemotherapy) has been used for stage III/IV disease in reported single-institution and cooperative-group experience, with meaningful long-term disease-free and overall survival in these series; treatment completion rates in some trials were incomplete, reflecting the intensity of this approach.
- Concurrent chemoradiotherapy is an option for unresectable disease.
- Corticosteroids can produce responses in thymoma, likely by acting on the benign lymphocytic component of the tumor rather than the malignant epithelial component; they have been used alongside chemotherapy or as monotherapy in small series.

## Recurrent, refractory, or metastatic disease

- Repeat surgical resection can be considered for recurrent disease (including pleural recurrence), as outcomes in some series do not clearly differ by whether disease is local, regional, or distant at the time of surgery.
- Second-line systemic options that have shown activity in thymoma include multikinase/angiogenesis-targeted tyrosine kinase inhibitors and mTOR inhibition, and somatostatin analogs (with or without added corticosteroid) have produced objective responses in some trials, although the biological rationale for the latter is not fully defined.
- Immune checkpoint inhibition (anti-PD-1 therapy) has shown activity in thymic epithelial tumors, but its use in thymoma specifically is limited by a substantial risk of triggering or worsening autoimmune paraneoplastic syndromes, so it warrants caution and specialist oversight, especially in patients with a history of myasthenia gravis or other autoimmune paraneoplastic disease.
- Targetable driver mutations (for example KIT mutations) are uncommon in thymoma compared with thymic carcinoma, so targeted therapy directed at a specific driver is more often relevant in thymic carcinoma than in thymoma itself.

## Paraneoplastic and associated syndromes

- Myasthenia gravis is the most common thymoma-associated paraneoplastic syndrome and should be actively screened for, since its presence and treatment can affect perioperative risk and planning; thymoma-associated MG has historically responded less well to surgery than MG without thymoma.
- Pure red cell aplasia and hypogammaglobulinemia (Good syndrome) are other recognized thymoma-associated syndromes; their presence should prompt a search for an underlying thymoma and be factored into overall management and surveillance.

## Thymic carcinoma (distinct entity, same anatomic site)

- The optimal approach to thymic carcinoma is less well defined than for thymoma, but current practice favors an aggressive multimodality approach with surgical resection plus postoperative radiation and chemotherapy when disease is resectable.
- Complete (R0) resection remains the goal and gives the best prognosis for apparently early-stage disease; PORT appears to have a trend toward benefit particularly when resection is incomplete or disease is more advanced.
- Neoadjuvant chemotherapy has been used in small numbers of patients and may help make an R0 resection achievable.
- (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease) Thymic carcinoma is more likely than thymoma to harbor targetable mutations such as KIT alterations, and multikinase inhibitors have shown more consistent activity in thymic carcinoma than in thymoma in the reviewed data.

## Monitoring and follow-up

DeVita's chapter on thymic neoplasms does not lay out an explicit surveillance imaging schedule or follow-up interval for resected thymoma. (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease) In practice, patients are followed with periodic clinical assessment and chest imaging for recurrence, since thymoma can recur locally or in the pleura even years after resection, and lifelong follow-up is generally advised because of this late-recurrence potential; the exact interval should be set by the treating multidisciplinary team rather than a fixed protocol here.

## When to refer

- Any patient with a newly identified anterior mediastinal mass suspicious for a thymic epithelial neoplasm should be referred for multidisciplinary evaluation (thoracic surgery, radiation oncology, and medical oncology) before biopsy or resection, since a needle biopsy approach is generally avoided when thymoma is suspected and R0 resection should be planned by an experienced surgical team from the outset.
- Locally advanced (stage III/IV), unresectable, recurrent, or metastatic disease should be managed at a center experienced in multimodality thymic tumor treatment, given the complexity of sequencing surgery, radiation, and chemotherapy and the low incidence of this tumor type.
- Any patient being considered for immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy should be referred to a center with experience managing thymoma-associated autoimmune paraneoplastic syndromes given the elevated risk of serious autoimmune toxicity in this population.
- Patients with thymoma-associated myasthenia gravis, red cell aplasia, or hypogammaglobulinemia should be co-managed with neurology/hematology/immunology as relevant, since these syndromes affect perioperative risk and long-term management.

Sources: DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of Oncology, 12th ed.
