# Vulvar cancer - management

## Overview

Vulvar squamous cell carcinoma (VSCC) accounts for the large majority of invasive vulvar cancers, usually arising in the setting of HPV-related vulvar intraepithelial neoplasia or, in HPV-negative cases, differentiated VIN associated with lichen sclerosus. HPV-associated tumors carry a better prognosis than HPV-negative disease, and abnormal p53 mutation status is linked with worse outcomes in HPV-negative patients. Staging is a hybrid clinical/surgical FIGO system, and inguinal lymph node status is the single most important prognostic factor: nodal involvement drops 5-year overall survival from roughly 90% to 50%.

## High-grade vulvar intraepithelial neoplasia (precursor disease)

- Low-grade lesions are not precancerous and do not require treatment unless symptomatic.
- For high-grade VIN (HSIL), excision is generally preferred over ablation or topical therapy because unsuspected invasive disease is found in a meaningful minority of excised specimens, and excision is favored when there are features worrying for invasion or a background of dVIN, lichen sclerosus, or immunosuppression.
- Wide, multifocal disease can be managed with a superficial ("skinning") vulvectomy with primary closure or a skin graft, chosen to preserve anatomy and sexual function; total vulvectomy for this indication carries a high psychosocial cost.
- CO2 laser ablation or argon beam coagulation is an option for multifocal disease or lesions involving the clitoris, urethra, anus, or vaginal introitus, after adequate biopsy sampling to exclude invasion; these methods do not give a specimen for histology.
- Topical imiquimod (a topical immune-response modifier) is often the preferred first-line medical option for recurrent HSIL; topical 5-fluorouracil is effective but used rarely because of side effects.
- Recurrence after treatment is common (roughly 20-30%, higher with positive margins), and progression to invasive cancer occurs in a meaningful minority, higher still in patients with dVIN; ongoing surveillance is warranted.

## Invasive disease: local treatment

- Surgical excision of the primary tumor with clear margins, avoiding sphincter injury, is the preferred first treatment when feasible.
- Small lesions confined to the vulva (roughly under 2 cm, without extension to urethra/vagina/anus) can be managed with radical local excision aiming for a margin of normal tissue around the tumor; lesions with minimal stromal invasion (under 1 mm depth) carry very low risk of nodal spread and can be managed with local excision alone.
- Larger (T2, up to about 4 cm) lesions are managed with wide local excision or modified radical vulvectomy, with the primary tumor and inguinal nodes addressed through separate incisions rather than en bloc resection, to reduce morbidity.
- Locally advanced disease (a meaningful proportion of squamous cell cases at presentation) has no single agreed approach; options include radical surgery (up to pelvic exenteration with permanent colostomy and/or urinary diversion when the anus, rectum, proximal urethra, or bladder is involved), concurrent chemoradiation, or a combination, chosen based on the likelihood of achieving clear margins without exenteration.
- Positive margins should prompt strong consideration of re-excision; close margins without other risk factors can be managed with close observation plus postoperative radiotherapy, discussed with the patient.
- Adjuvant radiotherapy after surgery is an important part of treatment of early-stage disease with high-risk features: lymph node involvement, close or positive margins, tumor size over roughly 4 cm, and lymphovascular invasion. Delay in completing the overall treatment course (surgery through radiotherapy) beyond about 15 weeks has been associated with worse survival, arguing for timely sequencing of adjuvant therapy.
- Local recurrence at 10 years occurs in a substantial minority of patients and is associated with worse disease-specific survival; risk factors include large tumor size, multifocal disease, deep stromal invasion, lichen sclerosus, perineural invasion, and close/positive margins.

## Invasive disease: regional (nodal) management

- Optimal management of the inguinal nodes is the single most important factor in reducing mortality from vulvar cancer; inguinal recurrence is rarely curable.
- Sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB) is now the standard of care for appropriately selected early-stage patients: clinically and radiographically node-negative, unifocal tumor under roughly 4 cm, without prior vulvar surgery that could have altered lymphatic drainage. SLNB has high sensitivity and negative predictive value, and reduces morbidity (lymphedema, wound complications) compared with full inguinofemoral lymphadenectomy (IFLND).
- If a sentinel node cannot be identified, or for tumors that cross the midline with unclear bilateral drainage, full IFLND (or bilateral SLNB) should be performed.
- Following SLNB, management is risk-stratified by the size of nodal disease: isolated tumor cells and micrometastases can often be managed with nodal radiotherapy alone (omitting IFLND), while macrometastases are generally managed with IFLND, with adjuvant radiotherapy or chemoradiation considered for larger or multiple deposits.
- Indications for adjuvant nodal radiotherapy after IFLND include two or more positive inguinal nodes, a fixed or ulcerated node, extranodal extension, or a high proportion of positive nodes; the benefit of radiotherapy for a single positive node without extranodal extension is not settled.
- The addition of chemotherapy to adjuvant radiotherapy in node-positive disease has been associated with improved overall survival in registry data, though this is not universally applied.

## Chemoradiation for locoregionally advanced disease

- Bulky T2 disease, T3/T4 tumors, or fixed/ulcerated nodes constitute locally advanced disease, for which concurrent chemoradiation (platinum-based, generally with a fluoropyrimidine or as a radiosensitizing single agent) has been used either preoperatively (to enable a smaller, more conservative resection) or as definitive treatment when surgery would otherwise require exenteration.
- Intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) is preferred over older conformal techniques because it better spares the skin, bladder, rectum, and small bowel while still covering the vulva and nodal regions.
- The decision between preoperative and definitive chemoradiation is individualized, based on comorbidities, nodal extent and location, and response to treatment; patients achieving a complete clinical response to chemoradiation frequently also have a complete pathologic response.

## Recurrent and metastatic disease

- Treatment of locoregional recurrence depends on extent of disease, prior treatment, time since initial treatment, and patient factors; options include surgery, radical radiotherapy with or without chemotherapy, palliative radiotherapy, systemic therapy, and best supportive care.
- For metastatic or unresectable recurrent disease, systemic options include chemotherapy, targeted agents, and immunotherapy. Commonly used chemotherapy combinations include a platinum agent paired with a taxane, or platinum paired with vinorelbine or gemcitabine, or single-agent taxane; response rates with chemotherapy in this setting are modest.
- Immunotherapy with a PD-1 checkpoint inhibitor has shown encouraging early results in HPV-positive vulvar carcinoma and is an area of active investigation; predictive biomarkers for PD-(L)1 inhibitors in HPV-driven disease are still emerging.
- A bevacizumab-containing regimen (extrapolated from its role in recurrent/metastatic cervical cancer) is included among systemic options for recurrent/metastatic VSCC. (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease: bevacizumab is an anti-VEGF antibody and is used with attention to bleeding, hypertension, and fistula risk.)
- EGFR inhibitors have been explored in small studies in recurrent VSCC, particularly relevant given high EGFR expression in HPV-negative disease, but this is not an established standard.
- Vulvovaginal melanoma is managed separately from squamous cell vulvar cancer: wide local excision with or without adjuvant radiotherapy is standard, morbid surgery is generally avoided given the poor prognosis of locally advanced disease, and the role of SLNB is not established in this histology. KIT-mutated melanoma may be a candidate for KIT or MAPK-pathway targeted therapy.

## Monitoring and follow-up

DeVita's chapter on vulvar cancer does not lay out a structured surveillance schedule for this disease; the following is general oncology practice rather than a DeVita-sourced protocol. (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease: routine follow-up combines periodic clinical vulvar and groin examination, with imaging reserved for symptoms or exam findings suspicious for recurrence, since isolated groin relapse carries a poor prognosis and is rarely salvageable.) Patients with a background of lichen sclerosus or VIN remain at long-term risk of a new primary vulvar cancer as well as true recurrence, and need ongoing dermatologic and gynecologic surveillance of the vulva.

## When to refer

- Any invasive vulvar lesion, or any high-grade VIN with features worrying for invasion, should be referred to gynecologic oncology for biopsy-guided staging and surgical planning.
- Locally advanced disease (large or fixed tumors, suspected pelvic bone or organ invasion, bulky/fixed/ulcerated groin nodes) should be managed at a center capable of multidisciplinary chemoradiation planning and, if needed, exenterative surgery.
- Node-positive disease, close or positive margins, or lymphovascular invasion should trigger radiation oncology involvement for adjuvant treatment planning.
- Suspected vulvovaginal melanoma should be referred promptly given its distinct biology and poor prognosis, with consideration of molecular testing (KIT) if unresectable or metastatic.

Sources: DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of Oncology, 12th ed.
