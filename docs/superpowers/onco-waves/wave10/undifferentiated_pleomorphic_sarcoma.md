# Undifferentiated Pleomorphic Sarcoma (UPS) - Management

UPS (formerly malignant fibrous histiocytoma, MFH) is a high-grade pleomorphic
soft-tissue sarcoma of exclusion, typically arising in the deep soft tissue of
the extremity (most often lower limb) or trunk in adults in their 60s to 70s.
It behaves aggressively: many patients develop metastasis within a few years
of diagnosis, and roughly 5% present with metastatic disease (usually
pulmonary) at the outset. Management follows the general framework used for
high-grade extremity/truncal soft-tissue sarcoma, since UPS is treated within
that broader soft-tissue sarcoma paradigm rather than with a UPS-specific
protocol.

## Localized disease

**Surgery** is the backbone of curative treatment. The goal is a
function-sparing complete excision with clear margins (roughly 1 mm to 1 cm
of normal tissue where feasible), sacrificing major neurovascular structures
only when unavoidable. Small (under 5 cm), superficial or single-muscle
compartment lesions can sometimes be managed with wide excision alone in
experienced hands; if margins turn out positive, radiotherapy is generally
added for high-grade tumors regardless of size. Patients who have already had
an unplanned ("whoops") excision elsewhere have a higher local recurrence
rate and should be considered for re-excision and/or adjuvant radiotherapy.

**Radiotherapy** is added to surgery for most deep, large (over 5 cm),
high-grade tumors, particularly when the margin is close, there is
extramuscular extension, or a local recurrence would require amputation or
sacrifice of a major nerve or vessel. It can be delivered before surgery
(better-defined, smaller treatment volume, but higher wound-complication
risk) or after surgery (preserves the full pathology specimen for margin
assessment, but requires a larger field and higher dose because the target
is less precisely known). Both sequences have shown similar local control,
metastasis-free survival, and overall survival in comparative trials; the
choice is individualized to wound-healing risk and functional goals.

**Systemic therapy in the localized setting** is not routinely required for
every patient and remains an individualized discussion rather than a default.
For large (over 5 cm), deep, high-grade tumors, preoperative (neoadjuvant)
chemotherapy is specifically considered for UPS with less than 5% myxoid
component, along with synovial sarcoma and round-cell/pleomorphic
liposarcoma, which are the histologies felt to be relatively more
chemo-responsive. When perioperative chemotherapy is used, it is
anthracycline-based combined with an alkylating agent (an
anthracycline-ifosfamide combination), reflecting the regimen used in the
major neoadjuvant and adjuvant trials in high-risk soft-tissue sarcoma.
Meta-analysis data show that combined anthracycline-alkylator chemotherapy
reduces local recurrence and improves overall survival, whereas single-agent
anthracycline alone has not shown a clear survival benefit; the evidence
overall is modest, so the decision to give perioperative chemotherapy should
be discussed individually with the patient, weighing tumor size, depth,
grade, and site against treatment toxicity.

## Advanced, unresectable, or metastatic disease

Systemic therapy is the mainstay once disease is unresectable or metastatic.
An anthracycline-based regimen (doxorubicin) remains the standard first-line
treatment. Combination therapy with an anthracycline plus an alkylating
agent (doxorubicin-ifosfamide) is considered when a higher chance of
objective response is needed, for example before an attempt at resecting
metastases, and is generally reserved for patients with good performance
status because of greater toxicity; single-agent anthracycline remains
standard for patients with poorer performance status. A gemcitabine-taxane
combination (gemcitabine plus docetaxel) has shown similar efficacy to
single-agent doxorubicin and is a reasonable alternative.

For second-line therapy after an anthracycline, options include a
gemcitabine-taxane combination, single-agent ifosfamide, a multi-targeted
tyrosine kinase inhibitor (pazopanib), or dacarbazine. Response rates and
progression-free survival fall with each successive line of therapy, and
given the limited number of active agents, enrollment in a clinical trial of
newer or kinase-targeted agents should be considered at any point, especially
after first-line therapy fails.

**Surgical resection of metastases**, most often pulmonary metastasectomy,
should be considered in selected patients with limited, resectable
metastatic disease, since roughly a fifth to a third of patients who undergo
metastasectomy remain alive at five years. Radiotherapy can be used for
palliation of localized, symptomatic metastatic sites. Because many patients
with metastatic sarcoma feel well for a period after metastases are found,
treatment intensity and timing should be tailored to symptoms, disease
trajectory, and patient preference rather than escalated reflexively at the
moment metastases are detected.

## Monitoring

After definitive local treatment, surveillance should include periodic
clinical examination of the primary site and imaging of the chest to detect
local recurrence or pulmonary metastases, since the lung is the dominant site
of distant spread in extremity/truncal sarcoma. A specific visit-and-imaging
interval schedule was not identified in DeVita's discussion of this disease
and is therefore not stated here; using a risk-adapted interval (more
frequent early, when recurrence risk is highest, spacing out over time)
follows general sarcoma surveillance practice (general oncology standard,
not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## When to refer

Because outcomes are sensitive to the adequacy of the first surgical
procedure and to correct diagnosis (UPS is a diagnosis of exclusion requiring
a broad immunohistochemical work-up to rule out dedifferentiated liposarcoma
and other pleomorphic sarcomas), any suspected UPS, and certainly any biopsy
of a deep soft-tissue mass suspicious for sarcoma, should be referred to a
specialist sarcoma multidisciplinary center before biopsy or excision is
attempted, so that biopsy-tract planning does not compromise a later
limb-sparing resection (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section
on this disease, though consistent with the pitfalls DeVita highlights for
this entity). Refer for consideration of perioperative chemotherapy or
clinical-trial enrollment whenever the tumor is large, deep, and high-grade,
or once the disease becomes unresectable or metastatic.

Sources: DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of
