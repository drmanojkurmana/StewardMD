# Tumour Lysis Syndrome (TLS) — Management

## Overview

Tumour lysis syndrome is an oncological emergency caused by rapid breakdown of malignant cells,
releasing intracellular potassium, phosphate, and nucleic acids (metabolised to uric acid) faster
than the kidneys can clear them. DeVita defines it by laboratory criteria (a defined rise in uric
acid or potassium or phosphorus, or a fall in calcium, within a set window around cytoreductive
therapy) which becomes clinical TLS once accompanied by a rise in creatinine, seizures, cardiac
dysrhythmia, or death. It is most closely associated with rapidly proliferating, chemosensitive,
high tumour-burden haematologic cancers (Burkitt lymphoma, acute leukaemias, high-grade
non-Hodgkin lymphoma), though solid tumours with similar biology can also trigger it.

TLS is managed by risk stratification and prevention first, then supportive correction of the
metabolic derangements it produces, not by treating a "stage" of cancer in the usual sense; the
approach is emergency/metabolic management layered onto whatever anticancer therapy is
producing (or about to produce) the tumour lysis.

## Risk Stratification (drives management intensity)

DeVita's chapter groups patients as follows, and this risk group is what determines how
aggressively TLS is managed:

- **Low risk**: low or medium tumour burden, low risk of cell lysis, normal organ function.
- **Intermediate risk**: medium or high tumour burden (bulky nodes, organomegaly, leukocytosis,
  elevated LDH), low/moderate/unknown risk of cell lysis, normal organ function.
- **High risk (not yet established)**: medium or high tumour burden with moderate/unknown risk of
  cell lysis PLUS abnormal organ function (pre-existing nephropathy, acidosis,
  hypotension/volume depletion, or prior/concurrent nephrotoxin exposure); or medium/high tumour
  burden with a high risk of cell lysis (elevated pretreatment uric acid).
- **Established clinical or laboratory TLS**: any of the above plus the laboratory or clinical
  criteria already met.

## Prevention (before and around cytoreductive therapy)

- Any rapidly growing cancer, or a cancer with high tumour burden expected to respond quickly to
  treatment, should be treated as a TLS candidate and screened before starting cytoreduction.
- Preventive measures should begin roughly a day before chemotherapy where possible; the
  cornerstone is aggressive intravenous hydration to drive a high urine output. Routine urinary
  alkalinisation is not recommended, because although it makes uric acid more soluble it makes
  calcium-phosphate complexes less soluble and can worsen renal calcium-phosphate deposition.
- Prophylactic urate-lowering therapy with a xanthine oxidase inhibitor (allopurinol, or
  febuxostat as an alternative where available) is started to blunt new uric acid formation; these
  agents do not lower an already-elevated uric acid load, since their effect is on future purine
  breakdown rather than existing urate.
- Laboratory monitoring of potassium, uric acid, phosphorus and creatinine, plus routine bloods
  and LDH (as a marker of tumour breakdown rate), should continue at least twice daily until the
  window of TLS risk has passed.

## Management by Risk Group

- **Low risk**: xanthine oxidase inhibitor prophylaxis, hydration, and daily monitoring/correction
  of electrolytes are generally sufficient.
- **Intermediate risk**: aggressive hydration and closer monitoring, with rasburicase (recombinant
  urate oxidase) considered on an individual basis depending on trajectory.
- **High risk or established TLS**: rasburicase is favoured over a xanthine oxidase inhibitor
  because it rapidly degrades the existing circulating uric acid load (converting it to a far more
  soluble metabolite) rather than only preventing new formation; use should be limited mainly to
  patients who are already at high risk of, or have established, clinical TLS, reflecting its
  higher cost relative to allopurinol. Rasburicase is avoided in G6PD deficiency because of the
  risk of haemolysis and methaemoglobinaemia (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section
  on this disease — DeVita's TLS chapter itself does not discuss the G6PD contraindication).

## Management of the Individual Derangements

- **Hyperkalaemia**: treated aggressively with cation exchange resins, dextrose with insulin, and
  calcium gluconate, with sodium bicarbonate and loop diuretics considered in addition.
  Haemodialysis is used for renal impairment or hyperkalaemia not responding to these measures.
- **Hyperphosphataemia**: managed with hydration, oral phosphate binders, and dialysis when
  needed. Oral calcium-containing binders should be used cautiously, since they can promote
  calcium-phosphate crystal precipitation.
- **Hypocalcaemia**: results from calcium-phosphate precipitation; correction is reserved for
  symptomatic hypocalcaemia rather than given routinely, because supplemental calcium can worsen
  metastatic calcium-phosphate deposition (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on
  this disease — this specific caution on calcium supplementation was not found in the excerpted
  DeVita passage, though it is consistent with the phosphate-driven pathophysiology DeVita
  describes).
- **Hyperuricaemia**: an allopurinol- or febuxostat-type xanthine oxidase inhibitor for
  prophylaxis, or rasburicase where rapid lowering is needed to avert renal failure, as above.

## Role of Renal Replacement Therapy

Haemodialysis or other renal replacement therapy has a defined rescue role: for refractory
hyperkalaemia not responding to medical measures, and for renal impairment complicating TLS. It
is not first-line but should not be delayed once medical measures fail.

## Monitoring

- Laboratory monitoring (potassium, uric acid, phosphorus, calcium, creatinine, and routine
  bloods) at least twice daily during the at-risk window, with LDH used to gauge the pace of
  tumour breakdown.
- Continuous cardiac monitoring and threshold for renal replacement therapy should be
  individualised to the severity of electrolyte derangement and renal function trend (general
  oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## When to Refer / Escalate

- Early involvement of nephrology (and critical care where relevant) is warranted once renal
  replacement therapy is being considered, or when hyperkalaemia, symptomatic hypocalcaemia, or
  renal impairment are not responding to first-line medical management, since the DeVita chapter
  frames haemodialysis as the fallback for refractory derangement.
- Any patient with a rapidly proliferating, high-burden malignancy starting cytoreductive therapy
  should have oncology/haematology coordinating TLS risk assessment and prophylaxis from the
  outset, before problems arise, rather than reacting only once laboratory abnormalities appear.

## What DeVita's TLS section does not address

The excerpted DeVita passage focuses on hydration, urate-lowering therapy, and correction of the
individual electrolyte derangements; it does not detail specific chemotherapy regimen selection,
radiotherapy, or surgery as tools for managing TLS itself, since TLS is a treatment-related
metabolic emergency rather than a disease with its own lines of anticancer therapy. Any statement
about surgery or radiotherapy is therefore omitted here rather than invented.

Sources: DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of Oncology, 12th ed.
