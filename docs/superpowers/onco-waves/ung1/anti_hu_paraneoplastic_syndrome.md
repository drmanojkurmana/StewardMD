# Anti-Hu Paraneoplastic Syndrome (ANNA-1 associated paraneoplastic encephalomyelitis / sensory neuronopathy) - Management

## Overarching principle: this is a tumour problem first, an immune problem second

Anti-Hu is a high-risk antibody (greater than 70 percent association with an underlying cancer), most often small cell lung carcinoma, and the phenotype it produces (encephalomyelitis, limbic encephalitis, sensory neuronopathy, or gastrointestinal pseudo-obstruction) is driven by a cytotoxic T-cell attack against an intracellular neuronal antigen. Because the antigen is intracellular, immunotherapy directed at antibodies is less effective here than in syndromes caused by surface-antigen antibodies. The two pillars of management are therefore (1) an intensive, sustained search for and treatment of the underlying malignancy, and (2) immunotherapy plus supportive/symptomatic care layered on top, with realistic expectations about how much of the neurological deficit will actually reverse.

## 1. Diagnostic workup and tumour search (precedes and runs alongside treatment)

- Do not wait for antibody confirmation to start treatment if clinical suspicion for a paraneoplastic neurologic syndrome is high; exclude viral and bacterial infection first, then treat.
- Test both serum and cerebrospinal fluid for anti-Hu (ANNA-1), because sensitivity and specificity differ between serum and CSF depending on the antibody.
- Because a high-risk phenotype/antibody combination carries a high probability of an underlying tumour, an initially negative malignancy screen should be repeated every four to six months for up to two years, since most paraneoplastic-associated tumours surface within that window.
- Small cell lung carcinoma is the dominant association; imaging should be directed at the chest, with repeat and/or functional imaging over time if the first study is negative (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease, though DeVita does support the repeat-screening interval above).

## 2. Treatment of the underlying tumour: the primary intervention

- Prompt identification and treatment of the underlying small cell lung carcinoma is the intervention most likely to halt neurological progression.
- Successful tumour treatment tends to help paraneoplastic syndromes driven by ectopic hormone production more reliably than immune-mediated neurological syndromes such as this one, where tumour-directed treatment is less effective at reversing deficits even though it remains the priority.
- Standard oncologic staging and treatment-intent decisions for small cell lung carcinoma (limited versus extensive stage) should proceed on their own usual timeline and are not modified by the presence of the paraneoplastic syndrome; systemic therapy, radiotherapy, and (where applicable) surgery are selected according to standard staging, not detailed further here since that is a separate disease's protocol (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## 3. Immunotherapy: first line

- Patients with high suspicion should start acute immunotherapy without waiting for antibody results (once infection is excluded).
- First-line acute treatment is corticosteroid therapy (intravenous methylprednisolone or oral prednisone-class dosing over several days), with intravenous immunoglobulin and plasma exchange as alternative or add-on first-line options.
- Expect only modest benefit from immunotherapy given that the T-cell-mediated neuronal loss driving this syndrome is largely irreversible; the realistic goal is stabilisation rather than reversal of established deficits.

## 4. Second-line immunotherapy (for non-responders)

- For patients who do not respond to first-line therapy, second-line steroid-sparing/immunosuppressive options described in the oncology literature include rituximab, azathioprine, mycophenolate mofetil, and cyclophosphamide.
- Tocilizumab (an interleukin-6-directed antibody) may be considered in autoimmune encephalitis that is refractory to rituximab.
- These agents are named here as drug classes/agents only; a decision on which second-line agent to use, and any dosing, belongs in the structured protocol/regimen template, not in this narrative.

## 5. Symptomatic and supportive management

- Neuropathic pain and disabling sensory ataxia from the sensory neuronopathy (dorsal root ganglionopathy) require symptomatic pain treatment and rehabilitation input.
- Autonomic failure, including orthostatic hypotension and chronic intestinal pseudo-obstruction, needs supportive and prokinetic management; pseudo-obstruction carries a real risk of malnutrition and bowel perforation and should be monitored proactively (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Antiseizure medication is used for encephalitic seizures, alongside supportive neuropsychiatric care for behavioural/cognitive symptoms of limbic or brainstem involvement.
- Brainstem involvement threatening bulbar or respiratory function, and dysautonomia with arrhythmia risk (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease), are red flags that warrant escalation of monitoring/care intensity.

## 6. Monitoring

- Serial clinical neurological assessment to track whether the syndrome is stabilising, progressing, or (rarely) improving after tumour treatment and immunotherapy.
- Continued surveillance imaging for an occult tumour on the four-to-six-month/two-year schedule above if no cancer has yet been found, since the neurological syndrome usually precedes the cancer diagnosis.
- Ongoing autonomic and gastrointestinal monitoring in patients with dysmotility, given the risk of malnutrition and perforation.
- Reassessment of antibody titre is not a validated way to track treatment response in this syndrome and should not replace clinical and tumour-directed follow-up (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## 7. When to refer

- Refer to neurology (ideally a centre experienced in autoimmune/paraneoplastic neurology) as soon as a high-risk phenotype or a positive anti-Hu antibody is identified, for diagnostic confirmation, immunotherapy selection, and long-term follow-up.
- Refer to oncology/pulmonology urgently for tumour staging and treatment once malignancy is found, or for a structured repeat-screening pathway if the first screen is negative.
- Multidisciplinary follow-up spanning neurology, oncology, and palliative care is appropriate given the combination of a likely irreversible neurological deficit and an underlying malignancy that determines overall survival.
- Refer for rehabilitation (physical/occupational therapy) for sensory ataxia and functional decline, and for gastroenterology input if pseudo-obstruction is present or suspected.

Sources: DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of Oncology, 12th ed.
