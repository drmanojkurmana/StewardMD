# Atypical Spitz Tumour (STUMP / Spitzoid Tumour of Uncertain Malignant Potential) - Management

## What this entity is and why management is uncertainty-driven
Atypical Spitz tumour sits on a morphologic and genetic continuum between benign Spitz naevus and Spitz melanoma, the malignant counterpart of Spitz naevi. DeVita describes this as "Pathway IV" among the non-cumulative-sun-damage melanoma pathways: Spitz naevi occur mostly in childhood, while atypical Spitz tumours and Spitz melanomas occur more often in older age groups. Lesions with intermediate genetic or histopathologic features are, by definition, called atypical Spitz tumours. Because the diagnosis itself is one of uncertainty rather than a clean binary, management is built around resolving that uncertainty (adequate tissue, ancillary testing) and then treating conservatively unless features shift the lesion toward frank melanoma.

## Diagnostic foundation that drives management
- A full-thickness biopsy of the entire lesion (not a thin shave) is required to assess overall architecture; DeVita notes this is critical specifically for differentiating melanoma from Spitz naevus, and that thin shave biopsies can lead to under-recognition of deeper/atypical lesions. This applies directly here: adequate initial tissue is what allows the intermediate "atypical" category to be assigned rather than missed.
- Cytogenetic and molecular ancillary testing (fluorescence in situ hybridisation, comparative genomic hybridisation, kinase-fusion and HRAS testing) has become an important adjunct specifically to differentiate melanoma from Spitz naevus/atypical Spitz tumour when morphology alone is equivocal (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease, though DeVita does state cytogenetic advances have aided this differentiation generally).
- Recognised driver alterations in this pathway are kinase fusions (ALK, ROS1, NTRK1, NTRK3, MET, RET, BRAF) and MAP3K8 fusions, or HRAS mutation, distinct from the driver profile of conventional melanoma.

## Treatment approach by intent
There is no staged, intent-based systemic treatment algorithm for atypical Spitz tumour analogous to melanoma staging, because the entity is managed as a surgical/pathological problem of uncertain potential rather than a confirmed malignancy.

- **Primary (local) management**: complete surgical excision of the lesion is the mainstay, aiming for clear histologic margins. If margins are involved or excision was incomplete, re-excision/wider local excision is indicated (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease; DeVita's specific text on atypical Spitz tumour management is limited to diagnosis and pathway biology rather than a detailed surgical-margin protocol).
- **Regional nodal disease**: sentinel lymph node biopsy may be considered in higher-risk lesions, but a positive node in this context does not carry the same ominous prognosis it does in conventional melanoma, so its result should be interpreted cautiously and not automatically trigger melanoma-type completion lymphadenectomy or adjuvant systemic therapy (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- **Systemic therapy**: DeVita's material on this pathway does not describe a systemic therapy algorithm for atypical Spitz tumour; systemic/targeted therapy is not part of standard management of the intermediate lesion itself. If a lesion behaves aggressively or is later reclassified as Spitz melanoma, it should be managed and staged as melanoma, at which point targeted therapy directed at an identified driver kinase fusion (ALK, ROS1, NTRK) could be considered by extrapolation from targeted-therapy principles used in other kinase-fusion-driven cancers (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- **Radiotherapy**: no role is described for atypical Spitz tumour; radiotherapy is not part of routine management of this entity.

## Lines of therapy
This is not a disease with sequential lines of systemic therapy in the way invasive melanoma is. The "escalation" pathway is really a reclassification pathway: if follow-up, re-excision pathology, or ancillary testing shift the diagnosis toward Spitz melanoma (accumulating chromosomal aberrations, high mitotic rate, deep extension, ulceration), management converts to melanoma-directed care (wide excision to melanoma margins, staging, and consideration of adjuvant/systemic therapy per melanoma pathways), rather than atypical-Spitz-tumour therapy being intensified as such.

## Monitoring
- Clinical surveillance of the excision site and regional nodal basins for local or nodal recurrence, since the intermediate category carries genuine biological uncertainty even after apparently complete excision.
- Ongoing follow-up is warranted because these lesions, grouped conceptually with other melanocytic tumours of uncertain malignant potential, tend to have benign or indolent courses but still deserve attention in follow-up per the recognised diagnostic uncertainty in this group.
- There is no described role for imaging-based surveillance, tumour marker follow-up, or fixed-interval restaging protocols specific to atypical Spitz tumour; monitoring is clinical/dermatologic rather than oncologic-imaging based, reflecting the generally indolent behaviour of this entity.

## When to refer
- Any diagnostically ambiguous lesion, or one with worrying features (large size, ulceration, deep extension, high mitotic rate, atypical mitotic figures, positive or close margins, or accumulating molecular/chromosomal abnormalities on ancillary testing) should prompt multidisciplinary dermatopathology and oncology review rather than management in isolation, given how easily this entity can be overcalled as benign or undercalled as melanoma.
- Children and young patients with a changing pigmented lesion pose a particular diagnostic dilemma: DeVita notes that excisional biopsy is often not feasible under local anaesthesia in this group, that some undergo incomplete shave biopsy, and that this can leave a genuine diagnostic dilemma between melanoma and Spitz naevus. This should prompt early referral to a centre able to obtain and interpret full-thickness tissue and ancillary testing rather than prolonged observation.
- Any lesion reclassified as, or behaving as, melanoma should be referred into standard melanoma staging and multidisciplinary oncology care rather than continued management as an atypical Spitz tumour.

Sources: DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of Oncology, 12th ed.
