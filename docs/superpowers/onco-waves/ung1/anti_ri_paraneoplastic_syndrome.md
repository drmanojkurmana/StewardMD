# Anti-Ri Paraneoplastic Syndrome (ANNA-2 syndrome) - Management

Anti-Ri (ANNA-2) is a high-risk onconeural antibody most often found in the setting of opsoclonus-myoclonus syndrome, and in adults is associated with breast cancer, gynaecological cancer, and small cell lung carcinoma. DeVita's chapter on paraneoplastic syndromes notes that opsoclonus-myoclonus syndrome is paraneoplastic in about 40% of adult cases, with small cell lung cancer, breast cancer, and ovarian teratoma the most commonly associated tumors in adults, and that Ri is the antibody most commonly found in this phenotype. There is no anti-Ri-specific treatment protocol in DeVita; management below follows the general framework DeVita gives for paraneoplastic neurologic syndromes (PNS) as a class, applied to this antibody/phenotype.

## Treatment approach by intent

There are two parallel tracks, and DeVita is explicit that both should proceed together rather than sequentially.

- **Tumor-directed therapy.** Identifying and treating the underlying malignancy is the first priority once a high-risk phenotype or antibody is found. DeVita states that patients with high suspicion for a paraneoplastic neurologic syndrome should not wait for antibody confirmation to start treatment, and should begin once infectious causes have been reasonably excluded.
- **Immunotherapy for the neurologic syndrome.** DeVita notes that PNS treatment is based mainly on immunotherapeutic approaches, drawn largely from retrospective series and expert opinion, since evidence is limited. It also notes that antibodies against cell-surface antigens tend to respond better to immunotherapy than antibodies against intracellular antigens; anti-Ri targets the intracellular RNA-binding proteins NOVA-1/NOVA-2, so a lesser or slower response to immunotherapy than surface-antigen syndromes would be expected on this general principle (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease, since DeVita does not name anti-Ri specifically when making this point).

There is no role for surgery or radiotherapy directed at the neurologic syndrome itself; surgery and/or radiotherapy and/or systemic therapy are used only as dictated by the stage of the underlying breast, gynaecological, or lung cancer that is driving the paraneoplastic process (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Lines of therapy for the neurologic syndrome

DeVita lays out a first-line/second-line structure for paraneoplastic neurologic syndromes generally:

- **First-line (acute) immunotherapy:** corticosteroids, or intravenous immunoglobulin, or plasma exchange.
- **Second-line, for non-responders:** options named by DeVita include an anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody (rituximab), azathioprine, mycophenolate mofetil, or cyclophosphamide.
- Tocilizumab (an interleukin-6 antibody) is named by DeVita for autoimmune encephalitis refractory to rituximab; this is not specific to opsoclonus-myoclonus or anti-Ri and should be read as an option for the broader PNS category rather than a directive for this syndrome.

For the movement disorder component (opsoclonus-myoclonus) itself, symptomatic control with a GABA-enhancing agent such as clonazepam is standard practice; this comes from general neurology/paraneoplastic references rather than DeVita's oncology chapter (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

The distinctive brainstem features that can accompany anti-Ri disease, jaw dystonia, jaw-opening dystonia, trismus, and laryngospasm, are not addressed in DeVita's paraneoplastic chapter. Airway vigilance and supportive management of laryngospasm follow general critical-care and neurology practice (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Monitoring

- DeVita states that when screening for the associated malignancy is initially negative in a patient with a high-risk phenotype or antibody, it should be repeated every four to six months for two years, since most PNS-associated tumors are found within this window.
- Diagnostic confidence and the case for continued tumor surveillance can be framed using DeVita's PNS-Care score, which weighs phenotype risk, antibody risk, and whether a cancer consistent with the phenotype has been found.
- Ongoing neurologic monitoring (gait, speech, eye movements, swallow/airway status) and response to immunotherapy should guide escalation between first- and second-line options; DeVita does not give a specific reassessment interval for this, so timing should follow standard neurology/oncology multidisciplinary practice (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## When to refer

- Any adult presenting with opsoclonus-myoclonus should prompt onconeural antibody testing and a coordinated search for an underlying malignancy; this is consistent with DeVita's description of the syndrome and its cancer associations.
- Refer to neurology (or neuro-oncology) for antibody confirmation, CSF studies, and immunotherapy decisions, and to oncology/gynaecologic oncology or thoracic oncology depending on which malignancy screening identifies.
- Escalate urgently for disabling opsoclonus-myoclonus affecting vision or mobility, or for airway-threatening features (laryngospasm, severe jaw dystonia/trismus); these red flags are not from DeVita's oncology chapter and reflect standard neurology practice (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## What was deliberately omitted

No specific drug, dose, or regimen numbers are given anywhere above, consistent with the dose-free requirement. Statistics on response rates specific to anti-Ri disease were not found in DeVita and are not included; only the general PNS treatment framework and the opsoclonus-myoclonus epidemiology DeVita gives are presented as DeVita-sourced.
