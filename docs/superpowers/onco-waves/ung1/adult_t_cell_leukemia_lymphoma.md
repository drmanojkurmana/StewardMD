# Adult T-cell leukemia-lymphoma (HTLV-1) - management narrative

## Overview and why subtype drives treatment

Adult T-cell leukemia-lymphoma (ATLL) is a highly aggressive CD4-positive T-cell neoplasm caused by chronic HTLV-1 infection. Treatment approach is dictated first and foremost by the clinical subtype, not by an anatomic stage system, because the four recognized variants (acute, lymphomatous, chronic, and smoldering) differ enormously in behavior and median survival. This subtype-first framework is the organizing principle for everything below.

## Treatment by subtype/intent

- **Smoldering and chronic subtypes**: these more indolent forms can be managed with antiviral-based therapy (zidovudine combined with interferon-alpha) rather than cytotoxic chemotherapy up front, based on a retrospective analysis showing improved survival with this antiviral combination in these subtypes as well as in acute disease. The chronic subtype can evolve into the acute type, so ongoing monitoring for transformation is required.
- **Lymphomatous subtype**: in the same retrospective analysis, patients with lymphomatous-type disease did better with first-line chemotherapy rather than antiviral therapy, so this subtype is generally directed toward intensive combination chemotherapy from the outset.
- **Acute subtype**: this is the most aggressive variant and is treated with intensive multiagent chemotherapy. A phase III randomized trial comparing an intensive alternating regimen against a dose-dense CHOP-based comparator regimen with intrathecal methotrexate showed a significantly higher complete response rate and a better 3-year overall survival with the intensive regimen, though overall response rates did not differ significantly between the two approaches. Even with the more intensive regimen, 3-year overall survival remained poor, underscoring how difficult-to-treat this subtype is.

## Lines of therapy and role of specific agents

- First-line treatment for acute (and generally lymphomatous) ATLL is intensive multiagent combination chemotherapy.
- The anti-CCR4 monoclonal antibody (mogamulizumab) has documented activity in relapsed aggressive ATLL, with an overall response rate around 50% in a phase II study, though median progression-free survival was short (about 5 months), and its main current interest is investigational combination with chemotherapy rather than as a reliable single-agent cure.
- Mogamulizumab given before allogeneic stem cell transplant has been associated with an increased risk of severe, treatment-refractory graft-versus-host disease, likely related to depletion of regulatory T cells; this timing risk should factor into sequencing decisions when transplant is being considered.
- Lenalidomide has shown only limited value in relapsed ATLL (an objective response in under half of patients but a short progression-free survival).
- Alemtuzumab has shown activity in relapsed disease in a small phase II study, but essentially all patients developed cytomegalovirus antigenemia during treatment (an infectious complication that would need proactive monitoring), and progression-free survival was again short.
- Given the generally poor results of chemotherapy alone in relapsed/aggressive disease, allogeneic stem cell transplantation (myeloablative or reduced-intensity) has been applied to ATLL, but with limited overall success. It is generally regarded as the only potentially curative option in eligible responders (this "only potentially curative" framing is general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Role of surgery and radiotherapy

DeVita's section on ATLL does not describe a defined role for surgery or radiotherapy in this disease; these modalities are not discussed as part of primary or salvage management here. Local radiotherapy may occasionally have a role for symptomatic, localized disease sites in individual patients (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease), but this is not something to present as a standard, protocolized component of ATLL management.

## Monitoring and supportive care

- Because ATLL frequently presents with (and can relapse with) hypercalcemia driven by parathyroid hormone-related protein, transforming growth factor beta, and RANK ligand, calcium levels warrant ongoing monitoring, particularly during active disease or at relapse.
- Patients are profoundly immunosuppressed and susceptible to opportunistic infections including pneumocystis, cryptococcal meningitis, strongyloides, and disseminated herpes zoster (this infection list is from DeVita's clinical-presentation description of this disease); monitoring for and prophylaxis against these should be part of ongoing care, and screening for these infections is relevant before and during immunosuppressive treatment.
- Given the risk of severe graft-versus-host disease when mogamulizumab precedes transplant, patients on a transplant trajectory who have received or are being considered for mogamulizumab need close monitoring for this complication.

## When to refer

Patients with ATLL should be managed by or in conjunction with a hematology-oncology service experienced in T-cell malignancies, given the need for subtype-specific therapy selection, the investigational and evolving role of targeted agents such as mogamulizumab, and the complexity of deciding on and sequencing allogeneic stem cell transplantation. Referral for transplant evaluation is appropriate for patients who achieve a response to initial therapy and are otherwise transplant-eligible, given that transplant is generally regarded as the only potentially curative option in this disease, despite its limited overall success rate (this "only potentially curative" framing is general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
