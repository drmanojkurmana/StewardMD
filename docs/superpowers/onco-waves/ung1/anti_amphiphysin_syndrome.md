# Anti-Amphiphysin Paraneoplastic Syndrome: Management

## Overview

Anti-amphiphysin antibody is a high-risk paraneoplastic antibody targeting an intracellular
presynaptic protein, most strongly linked to small cell lung carcinoma and breast carcinoma. The
best-recognised phenotype is paraneoplastic stiff-person syndrome (SPS), but it can also present as
encephalomyelitis or sensory neuronopathy. Because amphiphysin is an intracellular antigen,
antibody-mediated syndromes of this class respond less well to immunotherapy than syndromes driven
by antibodies against cell-surface targets, so tumour identification and treatment carries more of
the therapeutic weight here than in surface-antigen paraneoplastic disease.

## Treatment approach by intent

There is no "cure" for the neurological syndrome independent of the tumour; management runs two
tracks in parallel, and both start as soon as the paraneoplastic syndrome is suspected.

- **Find and treat the underlying malignancy.** This is the primary, disease-modifying
  intervention. Once a tumour (breast or small cell lung carcinoma) is found, standard
  stage-appropriate oncologic treatment for that cancer (surgery, radiotherapy, and/or systemic
  therapy as dictated by stage and histology) is pursued in its own right; successful tumour
  treatment is the best chance of neurological stabilisation, though the response of the
  neurological syndrome to tumour treatment is not guaranteed given the intracellular nature of the
  antigen.
- **Immunotherapy**, started without waiting for antibody confirmation once infection has been
  excluded and clinical suspicion is high. First-line treatment is a course of high-dose
  corticosteroids (intravenous or oral), with intravenous immunoglobulin or plasma exchange as
  alternative or added first-line options. For patients who do not respond, second-line
  options include rituximab, azathioprine, mycophenolate mofetil, or cyclophosphamide;
  tocilizumab has been used for autoimmune encephalitis refractory to rituximab. These are
  described as based mostly on retrospective series and expert opinion rather than randomised
  trial data.
- **Symptomatic control of rigidity and spasms** (the stiff-person phenotype) with
  GABA-enhancing agents such as benzodiazepines and baclofen, alongside physiotherapy and
  management of neuropathic pain (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this
  disease).

## Lines of therapy

1. First line: exclude infection, then start corticosteroids, with IVIG or plasma exchange as
   alternatives or adjuncts, concurrent with the malignancy work-up.
2. Second line (for non-responders): rituximab, azathioprine, mycophenolate mofetil, or
   cyclophosphamide.
3. Tocilizumab has been used for refractory autoimmune encephalitis after rituximab failure,
   though this evidence is not specific to the amphiphysin/stiff-person phenotype.

## Role of surgery, radiotherapy, and systemic therapy

DeVita's paraneoplastic chapter does not detail modality-specific oncologic treatment for the
breast or lung primary itself; it treats "detect and treat the tumour" as the operative principle
without prescribing a specific surgical/RT/systemic sequence for this syndrome. Surgery, RT, and
systemic anticancer therapy should therefore follow the standard stage-directed protocol for the
identified cancer type (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease), not
a paraneoplastic-syndrome-specific pathway.

## Monitoring

- If no tumour is found on initial screening despite a high-risk phenotype or antibody, repeat
  malignancy screening every four to six months for up to two years, since most paraneoplastic-
  associated tumours are detected within this window.
- Ongoing multidisciplinary follow-up between neurology and oncology, tracking both tumour status
  and neurological symptom trajectory.
- Watch for progression of rigidity/spasms into ventilatory compromise, and for evolution into
  encephalomyelitis or myelopathy, which carries a worse prognosis than isolated stiff-person
  disease.

## When to refer

- Refer to neurology (or a neuro-oncology/autoimmune neurology service where available) as soon as
  a high-risk antibody such as anti-amphiphysin is identified or strongly suspected, for antibody
  confirmation, phenotyping, and immunotherapy initiation.
- Refer for oncologic staging (breast and chest/lung imaging) in parallel, given the strong tumour
  association, rather than waiting for neurological workup to conclude.
- Escalate to a service capable of second-line immunotherapy (rituximab, cyclophosphamide, or
  similar) if there is no response to first-line corticosteroids/IVIG/plasma exchange.
- Urgent referral/admission is warranted for severe truncal or respiratory spasms, autonomic
  instability, or rapidly progressive encephalomyelitis or myelopathy.

Sources: DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of Oncology, 12th ed.
