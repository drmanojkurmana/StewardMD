# Giant cell fibroblastoma - Management

## Grounding note
Giant cell fibroblastoma (GCF) does not have its own dedicated management section in DeVita.
It appears only in the WHO soft-tissue tumour classification list, where it sits under
"intermediate (locally aggressive) tumours" alongside desmoid-type fibromatosis, and in a
citation noting that the PDGFB gene is rearranged "in dermatofibrosarcomas and giant-cell
fibroblastomas" (i.e. the same COL1A1-PDGFB fusion drives both). DeVita's substantive
management discussion is written for dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans (DFSP), which is
described in the same text (and in the KB's own reference note) as the adult counterpart of
GCF, with the two sharing genetics and sometimes recurring as, or arising combined with, one
another. The narrative below therefore draws the DFSP-specific management points from DeVita
and applies them to GCF by extension of that shared-entity relationship, flagged explicitly
where it is extrapolation rather than a GCF-specific statement in the source.

## Overview
GCF is a rare, locally aggressive fibroblastic tumour of infancy and early childhood, regarded
as the paediatric/juvenile counterpart of DFSP and driven by the same COL1A1-PDGFB fusion.
Because dedicated paediatric outcome data for GCF are not addressed in DeVita, management is
guided by the principles established for DFSP, the closely related entity with which GCF forms
a biological continuum, together with standard paediatric surgical oncology practice.

## Treatment approach by intent

**Localized, resectable disease (the great majority of cases).** Complete surgical excision is
the mainstay of treatment, mirroring the DFSP principle that "the gold standard of treatment
remains surgery." Because GCF, like DFSP, grows in an infiltrative pattern with irregular
radial extensions, standard excision without attention to margins carries a meaningfully higher
risk of local recurrence than for a well-circumscribed benign lesion. Wide local excision with
clear microscopic margins is the goal; in DFSP, planning gross margins of a few centimetres
achieves complete resection in most tumours, and complete microscopic clearance is associated
with low long-term local recurrence.

- Confirm the diagnosis (and the COL1A1-PDGFB fusion where feasible) before definitive surgery.
- Plan excision with margin assessment, favouring complete microscopic clearance over the
  narrowest possible defect, particularly in cosmetically or anatomically sensitive paediatric
  sites (trunk, groin).
- Where margins are positive or anatomically constrained (e.g. proximity to major neurovascular
  structures), re-excision to achieve clear margins is preferred over accepting a positive
  margin.

**Positive or close margins where re-excision is not feasible.** In DFSP, radiotherapy has
historically been used to reduce local recurrence after an incomplete (R1) resection in
anatomically complex sites, though DeVita notes that observation after R1 resection is
increasingly the preferred strategy rather than automatic adjuvant radiotherapy. The same
threshold applies by extension to GCF: radiotherapy is not the primary treatment and is reserved
for margin-positive disease that cannot be safely re-excised, with close observation as an
accepted alternative in many such cases, especially given the additional long-term
radiation-morbidity concerns unique to young children.

**Unresectable, recurrent, or locally advanced disease.** DFSP is sensitive to PDGFR-targeted
tyrosine kinase inhibition (imatinib), reflecting constitutive PDGFR activation from the
COL1A1-PDGFB fusion; DeVita describes imatinib as used for salvage of recurrent or residual
DFSP. Since GCF shares this identical fusion and downstream signalling driver, the same class of
PDGFR-targeted tyrosine kinase inhibitor represents the biologically rational systemic option
for unresectable or recurrent GCF, by direct extension of the DFSP data rather than dedicated
GCF trial evidence.

**Metastatic disease.** DeVita notes that metastasis in DFSP occurs infrequently and is
generally seen only in the context of fibrosarcomatous transformation to a high-grade tumour.
GCF is not described in the source as metastasizing in its conventional form; if aggressive,
high-grade transformation is identified on pathology, management intent shifts toward that of a
high-grade sarcoma and should be handled at a sarcoma-experienced centre.

## Monitoring
- Clinical follow-up of the excision site for local recurrence, given the infiltrative growth
  pattern shared with DFSP.
- Any recurrence should prompt repeat pathological review to exclude transition to, or
  recurrence as, conventional DFSP or a fibrosarcomatous (high-grade) variant, since DeVita
  describes this continuum explicitly.
- No dedicated GCF surveillance-imaging schedule is specified in the source; imaging follow-up
  should be guided by local extent, margin status, and site rather than a fixed protocol.

## When to refer
- Any suspected or confirmed GCF should be managed at, or in conjunction with, a centre
  experienced in paediatric soft-tissue tumour surgery, given the need for margin-oriented
  excision in a growing child.
- Positive or anatomically difficult margins, recurrent disease, or any suggestion of
  fibrosarcomatous (high-grade) change on histology warrants referral to a
  sarcoma/paediatric-oncology multidisciplinary team for consideration of re-excision,
  radiotherapy, or systemic PDGFR-targeted therapy.
- Cases with diagnostic uncertainty (distinguishing GCF from vascular lesions, fibrous
  hamartoma of infancy, or other paediatric spindle-cell tumours) should have fusion testing and
  expert pathology review before committing to a management plan.

## What is deliberately omitted
No statistics on GCF-specific local recurrence rates, metastasis rates, or survival are given
here, because DeVita does not report these for GCF itself (only for DFSP). No specific
drug names beyond the PDGFR-tyrosine-kinase-inhibitor class are given, and no doses, schedules,
or trial names are included, consistent with the dose-free requirement for this narrative.

Sources: DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of Oncology, 12th ed.
