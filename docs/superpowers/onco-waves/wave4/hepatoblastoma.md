# Hepatoblastoma — Management

## Overview
Hepatoblastoma is the most common primary liver malignancy of childhood, with peak incidence in the first two years of life. The tumour is characteristically chemosensitive, and this sensitivity is the central fact that shapes the whole treatment strategy: chemotherapy is used up front to convert an initially unresectable liver mass into one that can be removed.

## Treatment approach by extent of disease

**Resectable disease at diagnosis.** Surgical resection is the first line of therapy when the tumour is confined to the liver and amenable to complete excision at presentation.

**Initially unresectable, liver-confined disease.** Because hepatoblastoma is highly sensitive to chemotherapy, neoadjuvant treatment is used to shrink the tumour with the goal of rendering it resectable. Extent of disease (mapped by the PRETEXT system, grouping the liver into four sections) guides whether downstaging to resection is realistic or whether the tumour remains too central or too extensive.

**Tumours that remain unresectable after chemotherapy, without distant metastasis.** For lesions that cannot be converted to a resectable state but have not spread beyond the liver, total hepatectomy with liver transplantation is the recognised rescue option. Outcomes after transplant in this setting are excellent.

**Metastatic disease.** Pulmonary metastasis is the dominant site of spread. Metastatic and otherwise high-risk disease is managed with resection or treatment of the metastatic sites alongside intensified chemotherapy, reflecting the overall higher-risk category rather than a distinct drug class.

## Lines of therapy and role of systemic treatment
Cisplatin-based chemotherapy is the backbone of systemic treatment, used both as a single agent and in combination regimens depending on risk stratification. It is given neoadjuvantly to enable resection and is continued in the perioperative course; clinical trial data continues to refine whether single-agent platinum or a combination approach yields better event-free and overall survival in the postoperative setting. Beyond the choice of a platinum-based regimen, DeVita does not detail a codified further-line systemic approach for relapsed or refractory hepatoblastoma, so no specific second-line regimen is stated here.

## Role of surgery and transplantation
- Complete surgical resection remains the cornerstone of cure and is pursued as soon as the tumour is technically resectable, whether that is at presentation or after neoadjuvant chemotherapy has downstaged it.
- Liver transplantation is reserved for tumours that stay unresectable despite chemotherapy but remain confined to the liver (no distant metastasis); reported survival after transplantation in this group is excellent.
- Attempting primary resection of a tumour that would respond better to downstaging chemotherapy risks an incomplete excision and should be avoided.

## Radiotherapy
DeVita's discussion of hepatoblastoma centres on chemotherapy, resection, and transplantation; a defined role for radiotherapy is not described in the source, so no radiotherapy recommendation is made here.

## Monitoring
- Serum alpha-fetoprotein is tracked serially through treatment as a marker of response and of relapse; it should be interpreted against the normally elevated but declining alpha-fetoprotein levels of early infancy rather than against adult reference ranges.
- A low or normal alpha-fetoprotein at diagnosis should prompt consideration of the small cell undifferentiated subtype, which behaves more aggressively.
- Surveillance for pulmonary metastasis (the principal site of spread) is part of ongoing follow-up imaging.
- Ototoxicity and renal function should be monitored around platinum-based treatment, with otoprotective strategies used where appropriate to limit cisplatin-related hearing loss.

## When to refer
- Any child with a liver mass suspicious for hepatoblastoma should be managed by a paediatric oncology multidisciplinary team with hepatobiliary and transplant surgery input from the outset, given how central the resectability-versus-transplant decision is to outcome.
- Tumours with extensive or centrally located involvement by PRETEXT staging should be referred early to a centre capable of both complex hepatic resection and liver transplantation, since the treatment pathway bifurcates on this point.
- Children with hepatoblastoma, or a family history suggestive of Beckwith-Wiedemann syndrome or familial adenomatous polyposis, warrant genetic counselling and family screening referral given the recognised associations.
