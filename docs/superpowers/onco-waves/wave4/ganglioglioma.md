# Ganglioglioma - Management

## Overview and treatment intent

Ganglioglioma is a circumscribed glioneuronal tumor (WHO grade I or II) that behaves as a low-grade, largely indolent lesion and rarely progresses to a higher grade. Because these tumors are well demarcated and do not typically invade normal brain, treatment intent for the great majority of patients is curative, centered on surgery. A BRAF V600E mutation is present in a substantial proportion of cases and is a useful diagnostic marker; in adults this mutation occurs with somewhat higher frequency in WHO grade III tumors.

## Surgery: the primary and usually definitive treatment

- Surgical resection targeting the contrast-enhancing portion of the tumor is the mainstay of treatment for both newly diagnosed and recurrent ganglioglioma.
- Because surgery alone is often curative, most patients require no further therapy once a complete resection is achieved.
- Gross total resection provides long-term tumor control in the great majority of patients and is the preferred approach whenever feasible.
- Lesions in eloquent brain regions can still be resectable but pose added technical difficulty, since the tumor-brain boundary may be hard to define even with modern surgical adjuncts (intraoperative microscope, computer-assisted navigation, functional mapping).
- Retrospective series (no phase III trial has directly tested surgery against other approaches) associate complete resection with very favorable long-term survival, and resection can also achieve seizure control, which is a major consideration since gangliogliomas are a common cause of chronic focal epilepsy.
- Grade II tumors can recur, and the degree of anaplasia present on histology is what determines prognosis after surgery.

## Role of radiotherapy

- There is no clear consensus on adjuvant radiotherapy for ganglioglioma, and it is not required after a gross total resection given the excellent control rates from surgery alone.
- Adjuvant radiotherapy is considered after subtotal resection, particularly when the tumor sits in an eloquent area where further resection risks significant morbidity; retrospective data suggest it improves local control (and shows a trend toward improved long-term survival that has not reached statistical significance) compared with subtotal resection alone.
- For higher-grade (grade III-IV) histology, higher-dose radiotherapy is generally warranted, especially after subtotal resection, given the lower chance that surgery alone will control the tumor.
- An alternative to immediate postoperative radiotherapy after subtotal resection is close imaging surveillance, reserving further treatment for radiologic progression, particularly for lower-grade tumors where additional surgery or medical treatment remains an option later.
- Highly conformal radiotherapy techniques are preferred to limit long-term CNS toxicity, reflecting the young age of many patients and their otherwise excellent long-term prognosis.
- Stereotactic radiosurgery is a reasonable option for limited recurrence after gross or subtotal resection, or at relapse.

## Role of systemic/medical therapy

- Chemotherapy is not a first-line treatment for ganglioglioma. It is generally reserved for young children who have had a subtotal resection and go on to show disease progression, and for adults it is typically used as salvage therapy after surgery and radiotherapy have failed.
- No optimal chemotherapy regimen has been established for astroglial-variant low-grade tumors such as ganglioglioma; disease stabilization, rather than a complete response, is considered a successful outcome when chemotherapy is used.
- In adults, combined-modality radiotherapy plus chemotherapy can be considered when active antitumor treatment is indicated, but the evidence for or against its added benefit is limited.
- In selected BRAF V600E-mutant tumors that are higher-grade or unresectable, BRAF-targeted therapy (a BRAF inhibitor, with or without a MEK inhibitor) is a guideline-consistent option; DeVita's text on ganglioglioma itself does not detail this, so it is included here only as guideline-general practice for BRAF-altered glioneuronal/glial tumors and should be treated as such.

## Seizure management

Because ganglioglioma is a leading cause of chronic drug-resistant focal epilepsy, seizure control is a parallel treatment goal alongside oncologic control. Patients with drug-resistant focal seizures should be evaluated through a dedicated epilepsy-surgery pathway with video-EEG localization, and antiepileptic drugs are used to manage seizures pending and after resection. Complete resection carries a high chance of long-term seizure freedom.

## Monitoring

- Follow-up centers on surveillance brain MRI to detect residual tumor, recurrence, or transformation to a higher-grade lesion, since grade II tumors can recur and anaplastic change, though uncommon, can occur in a previously indolent lesion.
- Pathologic grade is the strongest predictor of outcome, so grading at diagnosis (and re-grading if the tumor recurs or changes on imaging) informs the intensity of surveillance.
- Ongoing seizure control should be tracked post-resection as a distinct outcome from tumor control.

## When to refer

- Any new diagnosis of ganglioglioma warrants referral to a neuro-oncology multidisciplinary team including neurosurgery and neuropathology, given the central role of surgery and the importance of accurate grading.
- Patients with drug-resistant focal seizures should be referred into a formal epilepsy-surgery pathway rather than managed on antiepileptic drugs alone.
- Subtotal resection, eloquent-area location, higher-grade histology, or recurrence should prompt discussion with radiation oncology (and, for BRAF V600E-mutant higher-grade or unresectable disease, consideration of BRAF-targeted therapy) as part of the same multidisciplinary review.
