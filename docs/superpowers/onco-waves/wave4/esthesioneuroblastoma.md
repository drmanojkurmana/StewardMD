# Esthesioneuroblastoma (Olfactory Neuroblastoma) — Management

Esthesioneuroblastoma (ENB, also called olfactory neuroblastoma) is a rare malignant tumour of the
olfactory neuroepithelium in the roof of the nasal cavity. It is usually of low to intermediate
neuroendocrine grade and staged with the Kadish system (A confined to the nasal cavity, B extending
into the paranasal sinuses, C extending beyond the sinuses, D nodal or distant disease), often
alongside Hyams histologic grade. Management is stage-driven and centres on local control of the
skull base, since the tumour characteristically erodes the cribriform plate and can extend
intracranially.

## Treatment approach by stage/intent

- **Early-stage disease confined to the nasal cavity or sinonasal cavity (roughly Kadish A/B):**
  surgical resection alone can be appropriate. Literature reviewed in DeVita notes that either
  radiotherapy or surgery alone has historically been considered sufficient for early-stage disease.
- **More advanced disease (extension beyond the nasal cavity and sinuses, involving the skull base
  or intracranial compartment; Kadish C):** the mainstay is surgical resection with negative
  skull-base margins, most often via endoscopic endonasal or open craniofacial resection, followed
  by postoperative radiotherapy. Larger modern surgical series support surgery plus postoperative
  radiotherapy, with elective nodal irradiation added for advanced Kadish C disease to reduce
  local and regional recurrence.
- **Disease with cranial nerve involvement, invasion near the optic chiasm, or cavernous/sphenoid
  sinus invasion:** these features are relative or absolute contraindications to resection, and
  such patients are generally managed with definitive radiotherapy (or chemoradiation) rather than
  surgery.
- **Nodal disease (Kadish D or clinically involved neck):** the risk of cervical lymph node
  involvement in ENB is meaningful (in the range of 20% to 25% in DeVita's review), which is why
  elective neck treatment (irradiation) is recommended even for patients who present N0, since
  omitting it has been associated with a higher rate of neck recurrence in reported series.
- **Distant metastatic disease:** systemic therapy has a role, extrapolated mainly from the broader
  sinonasal/neuroendocrine tumour literature rather than ENB-specific trial data; DeVita frames the
  role of chemotherapy in paranasal sinus tumours generally as an evolving area, reserved for
  locoregionally advanced, marginally resectable, unresectable, or metastatic disease.

## Lines of therapy and systemic treatment

- Surgery (endoscopic endonasal or open craniofacial resection) is the preferred initial modality
  whenever a gross total, margin-negative resection is anticipated.
- Postoperative radiotherapy is added when high-risk features are present on pathology (e.g.
  positive or close margins, higher Hyams grade, skull-base or intracranial extension) and is
  generally recommended regardless of margin status after resection for recurrent disease.
  Radiotherapy typically starts within about six weeks of surgery.
- For unresectable disease or patients who cannot have surgery, definitive radiotherapy or
  chemoradiation is used, with meticulous planning technique to limit injury to the cornea and
  optic pathways given proximity to the orbit.
- Systemic chemotherapy in the sinonasal/neuroendocrine group is used in two broad contexts,
  by extrapolation from the wider paranasal sinus tumour literature discussed in DeVita:
  - **Neoadjuvant/induction chemotherapy** (a platinum-based approach, sometimes combined with a
    taxane or other agent) ahead of surgery or definitive chemoradiation in marginally resectable
    or locoregionally advanced disease, with the choice of subsequent local therapy guided by
    radiologic response.
  - **Concurrent chemoradiation**, most often built around a platinum agent, used for unresectable
    disease, positive margins, or nodal disease with extranodal extension; this is an
    extrapolation from head and neck squamous cell carcinoma trial data rather than ENB-specific
    evidence.
- DeVita does not describe a specific systemic regimen validated for ENB itself (the induction and
  chemoradiation regimens it discusses are reported mainly for sinonasal undifferentiated carcinoma
  and neuroendocrine carcinoma of the paranasal sinuses, related but histologically distinct
  tumours). Any systemic regimen selection for ENB should be individualised by a multidisciplinary
  head and neck oncology team rather than taken as a fixed protocol.

## Role of surgery, radiotherapy, and multidisciplinary planning

- Surgery is the backbone of curative treatment when resectable, aiming for negative margins at the
  skull base; reconstruction of the skull base defect (e.g. with a pericranial, nasoseptal, or free
  flap) is planned as part of the same operation.
- Endoscopic endonasal approaches have largely replaced open craniofacial/bifrontal craniotomy
  approaches for many cases and, per DeVita, show equivalent disease control with better functional
  and quality-of-life outcomes, though open craniofacial resection remains necessary for some
  extensive or recurrent disease.
- Radiotherapy (conformal techniques such as IMRT, or proton-based techniques) is used
  postoperatively for high-risk pathology, or definitively when surgery is not feasible; elective
  irradiation of the neck is specifically recommended for ENB given its meaningful rate of occult
  nodal spread.
- Any case with skull-base or intracranial extension, orbital invasion, or cranial nerve
  involvement should be planned by a multidisciplinary team (head and neck surgery, neurosurgery,
  radiation oncology, and reconstructive surgery) before a treatment path is fixed.

## Monitoring

- ENB is characterised by late local recurrence and delayed cervical nodal metastasis, so
  surveillance needs to be prolonged rather than limited to the first two to three years typical of
  many other head and neck cancers.
- Follow-up should include nasal endoscopy and imaging (as used for other sinonasal cancers in
  DeVita's discussion), with attention to both the primary site and the neck, and low threshold to
  re-image if new cranial nerve symptoms, headaches, proptosis, or a neck mass develop.
- Recurrent disease after primary treatment can be difficult to distinguish from post-treatment
  change; localized recurrence after surgery alone is generally managed with chemoradiation or
  craniofacial resection plus postoperative radiotherapy, while radiotherapy failures may be
  salvaged with maxillectomy or craniofacial resection where feasible.

## When to refer

- Any suspected or confirmed ENB should be referred promptly to a tertiary head and neck oncology
  centre with dedicated skull-base surgery, neurosurgery, and radiation oncology expertise, given
  the technical demands of skull-base resection and the risk of intracranial extension.
- Refer for multidisciplinary discussion before finalising a treatment plan whenever there is
  cranial nerve involvement, invasion near the optic chiasm, cavernous or sphenoid sinus invasion,
  vision-threatening orbital disease, or nodal/distant disease, since these features change both
  resectability and the balance between surgery and definitive chemoradiation.
- Refer for consideration of proton or other highly conformal radiotherapy techniques when
  treatment volumes are close to the orbit or optic apparatus, to reduce the risk of
  radiation-related vision loss.

## What was deliberately omitted

DeVita does not report a specific validated systemic chemotherapy regimen, a defined number of
treatment lines, or outcome statistics specific to ENB beyond the historical and MD Anderson series
survival figures already summarised above in general terms; regimen-level and dosing detail was
therefore left out rather than inferred. Aside from the Kadish/Hyams staging summary carried over
from the existing sidecar, this document was grounded only in DeVita's Chapter 28 discussion of
esthesioneuroblastoma and general paranasal sinus tumour management.

Sources: DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of Oncology, 12th ed.
