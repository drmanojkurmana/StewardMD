# Gallbladder cancer (GBC) — Management

Aliases: gallbladder carcinoma, GBC, carcinoma of the gallbladder.

## Overview of approach

Treatment is driven almost entirely by stage at diagnosis and resectability, because
gallbladder cancer has a thin-walled, serosa-poor anatomy that allows early direct
extension into the adjacent liver and early lymphatic and hematogenous spread. Most
patients present at an advanced (locoregionally invasive or metastatic) stage and are
not surgical candidates, so a multidisciplinary hepatobiliary discussion (surgery,
medical oncology, radiation oncology, and palliative care) should occur before any
major treatment decision, and clinical trial enrollment should be considered whenever
available, particularly in advanced disease.

A meaningful share of GBC is diagnosed incidentally on pathology after a
cholecystectomy performed for presumed benign gallstone disease. Any incidental
finding of invasive carcinoma on a gallbladder specimen should trigger re-staging
(cross-sectional imaging, review of depth of invasion and margins) and consideration
of re-resection in a patient fit for surgery, since the original operation is rarely
adequate oncologic surgery.

## Surgery

- Surgery is the only potentially curative option for GBC.
- When GBC is suspected preoperatively, an open cholecystectomy is generally preferred
  over laparoscopic excision, to reduce the risk of tumor seeding from gallbladder
  perforation or bile spillage.
- For tumors confined to the mucosa or muscular layer with negative margins,
  cholecystectomy alone (an extraserosal cholecystectomy, removing the gallbladder
  along with the adjacent connective tissue down to liver tissue) can be curative.
- For tumors that penetrate the serosa or extend into the liver or an adjacent organ,
  more extensive resection is needed: this can include wedge resection of the
  adjacent liver segments, regional lymph node dissection, extrahepatic bile duct
  resection if the cystic duct margin is involved, and, in selected cases,
  pancreaticoduodenectomy. Direct tumor extension into the liver, duodenum, or colon
  is not by itself a contraindication to surgery; it determines how extensive the
  resection needs to be.
- Absolute contraindications to surgery include distant metastatic disease, major
  vascular (portal vein or hepatic artery) involvement, or nodal spread beyond the
  hepatoduodenal ligament.
- Achieving negative resection margins is the strongest determinant of long-term
  survival, with outcomes progressively worse from stage I through stage IV disease.
  Nodal involvement carries a worse prognosis than local hepatic invasion alone.
- Diagnostic laparoscopy and thorough staging imaging before major surgery help
  exclude peritoneal spread and avoid a non-therapeutic laparotomy.

## Adjuvant therapy after resection

- Because GBC carries a high risk of both systemic relapse and local-regional
  recurrence, adjuvant systemic therapy is recommended by most cancer centers
  following resection, most commonly a fluoropyrimidine-based regimen.
- Evidence specific to GBC is limited and drawn largely from trials of adjuvant
  chemotherapy across biliary tract cancers as a group (including gallbladder,
  intrahepatic, and extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma) rather than GBC-specific
  randomized trials, so recommendations are extrapolated from the broader biliary
  tract cancer literature.
- Adjuvant radiotherapy or chemoradiotherapy has been used after resection in some
  centers, particularly when margins are positive or residual disease is suspected,
  but the supporting evidence is retrospective and heterogeneous, so it should be
  regarded as an individualized decision made with a hepatobiliary multidisciplinary
  team rather than a uniform standard.

## Unresectable or locally advanced (intrahepatic-confined) disease

- Chemoradiation can be considered for disease that is unresectable but confined to
  the liver bed/locoregional area, as part of a multidisciplinary discussion.
- External beam radiotherapy can be used for symptom palliation; tumor control is
  rarely durable, but radiotherapy is generally well tolerated and may help with
  symptoms such as jaundice, sometimes combined with a fluoropyrimidine-based
  systemic agent.

## Metastatic or extrahepatic (unresectable) disease

- First-line systemic therapy for advanced or metastatic GBC follows the standard
  established for biliary tract cancers as a group: a gemcitabine-based, platinum-
  containing doublet (gemcitabine with a platinum agent). This regimen improves
  survival compared with gemcitabine alone in advanced biliary tract cancer and is
  the accepted backbone of first-line treatment.
- Enrollment in a clinical trial is an appropriate alternative to standard
  chemotherapy at this stage, including for genomic-profiling-directed trials.
- There is no single established standard for second-line therapy; an oxaliplatin-
  containing regimen (such as a FOLFOX-type combination) has shown a survival
  benefit over supportive care alone after progression on first-line
  gemcitabine/platinum-based therapy and can be considered.
- Targeted therapy is more established in cholangiocarcinoma generally (FGFR2
  inhibitors, IDH1 inhibitors) than specifically validated in GBC. In GBC, HER2/neu
  amplification is reported in a meaningful minority of tumors and HER2-directed
  therapy has shown early signals of activity; broader validation specific to GBC is
  still emerging, so genomic/HER2 testing to identify candidates for a clinical trial
  or targeted approach is reasonable in advanced disease rather than a settled
  standard of care.

## Palliative care

- Advanced, unresectable GBC carries a poor prognosis, and goals of care discussions
  and palliative care involvement should start early.
- The main goals of palliative management are relief of biliary obstruction (via
  endoscopic or percutaneous biliary drainage/stenting) and prevention of bowel
  obstruction, alongside symptom and pain control.
- Surgical bypass procedures and resection of metastatic or distant nodal disease are
  not generally justified given the expected survival.

## Monitoring and follow-up

- Following curative-intent resection, follow-up should include periodic clinical
  review, cross-sectional imaging, and tumor markers (CA 19-9 and CEA) to detect
  recurrence, mirroring the markers used at diagnosis and staging.
- In patients managed with chemotherapy for advanced disease, response should be
  assessed with periodic cross-sectional imaging and clinical review, with
  transition to second-line therapy or best supportive care based on progression and
  performance status.

## When to refer

- Any suspected or newly diagnosed GBC, including incidental carcinoma found after a
  routine cholecystectomy, should be referred promptly to a hepatobiliary surgical
  team and discussed in a hepatobiliary multidisciplinary tumor board before further
  treatment, since the adequacy of the index operation and the need for re-resection
  materially affect the chance of cure.
- Patients with advanced, unresectable, or metastatic disease should be referred to
  medical oncology for systemic therapy and clinical trial evaluation, with early
  concurrent referral to palliative care for symptom control and biliary drainage
  planning.

## What DeVita does not resolve for GBC specifically

The available guidance for GBC treatment is drawn largely from small case series or
studies that pool GBC together with other biliary tract cancers, so a GBC-specific,
prospectively validated standard for adjuvant chemoradiotherapy, second-line systemic
therapy, and routine post-treatment surveillance intervals is not established; this
content deliberately omits any specific surveillance schedule, second-line regimen
beyond the one noted above, or targeted-therapy recommendation that was not directly
grounded in the source.
