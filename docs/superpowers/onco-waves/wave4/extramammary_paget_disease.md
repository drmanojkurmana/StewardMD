# Extramammary Paget Disease (EMPD) - Management

Extramammary Paget disease is an intraepithelial adenocarcinoma of apocrine-gland-bearing skin (perianal, vulvar, penoscrotal, axillary and other sites). Before a treatment plan is chosen, the two central questions are whether the disease is confined to the epidermis (in situ) or has invaded the dermis, and whether it is primary cutaneous disease or secondary to an underlying visceral adenocarcinoma (anorectal, urothelial, gynaecologic). Management is overwhelmingly surgical, and every plan should be paired with a search for an associated internal malignancy.

## Treatment approach by extent/intent

- **In situ / non-invasive disease (curative intent):** Surgical excision is the mainstay. Because EMPD spreads subclinically and is frequently multifocal, margins are often positive even after apparently adequate excision, which is the main driver of local recurrence.
- **Extensive or surgically difficult in situ disease:** Non-surgical local options exist for patients who are not good surgical candidates or where excision would be very disfiguring, including topical therapy, photodynamic therapy, or radiotherapy to the affected skin.
- **Dermal invasion or nodal disease:** Once invasion is present, this behaves as an invasive adenocarcinoma of the site rather than an in-situ lesion. Surgery remains the cornerstone, with radiotherapy or systemic chemotherapy considered as adjuncts for locally advanced, node-positive, or margin-positive disease.
- **Secondary EMPD (epidermotropic spread from an underlying anorectal, urothelial, or other visceral adenocarcinoma):** The underlying primary drives management. Perianal Paget disease is managed surgically at the skin level, but a meaningful fraction of patients (roughly 5% progression to invasive disease, with invasive cancer described in up to about 40% of those left untreated in some series) and a real minority carry a synchronous internal cancer, so the underlying adenocarcinoma must be identified and treated according to its own site-specific staging and treatment pathway.

## Lines of therapy / modalities

- **Surgery** is first line for essentially all EMPD, whether in situ or invasive. Excision technique should account for the tendency toward subclinical extension beyond the visible lesion; the goal is clear margins, recognizing that recurrence after excision is common precisely because of this multifocal, subclinical spread.
- **Radiotherapy** has a role both as a non-surgical option for in-situ disease in patients unfit for or unwilling to have surgery, and as an adjunct after surgery for invasive, margin-positive, or nodal disease.
- **Topical/local therapy and photodynamic therapy** are alternatives for extensive superficial disease, generally reserved for in-situ disease where wide excision would be highly morbid.
- **Systemic therapy** is reserved for invasive, nodal, or metastatic disease, or for treating an underlying secondary adenocarcinoma; the specific systemic regimen follows the site-specific pathway for the associated cancer (e.g. anorectal or urothelial adenocarcinoma protocols) rather than a distinct "EMPD regimen."

## Monitoring

- Long-term surveillance is required after treatment because local recurrence rates are high, driven by the subclinical multifocal nature of the disease rather than true relapse from a new primary.
- Patients with perianal Paget disease should have imaging and endoscopic evaluation to exclude a synchronous underlying malignancy at diagnosis, given the described associations with tubo-ovarian adenocarcinoma and gastrointestinal cancers in this subset; the same principle of directed screening for an occult internal primary applies to other EMPD locations (e.g. urothelial evaluation for penoscrotal disease, gynaecologic evaluation for vulval disease).
- Any new nodule, induration, ulceration, or growth within a previously treated or observed area should prompt re-biopsy to reassess for dermal invasion.

## When to refer

- Any patient with biopsy-confirmed EMPD should be managed by a multidisciplinary team spanning dermato-oncology/dermatologic surgery, surgical oncology, and the relevant site specialist (colorectal surgery for perianal disease, urology for penoscrotal/periurethral disease, gynaecologic oncology for vulval disease), because the surgical approach and the search for an underlying malignancy are intertwined.
- Refer for radiation oncology and medical oncology input once dermal invasion, nodal involvement, or an associated invasive visceral adenocarcinoma is identified, since treatment then follows the invasive-cancer pathway of the relevant primary site rather than a dermatologic in-situ approach.
- Refer promptly for imaging and endoscopic/urologic workup at diagnosis in perianal, penoscrotal, or vulval EMPD to exclude a synchronous internal adenocarcinoma before finalizing a local surgical plan.

## What DeVita is silent on (deliberately omitted)

DeVita's discussion of EMPD is limited to the anal/perianal and vulval contexts and does not detail specific systemic regimens, radiotherapy fields/technique, or comparative outcomes for Mohs surgery versus wide local excision, or immunotherapy/targeted-therapy use in metastatic EMPD. Those specifics have been omitted rather than filled in from outside general knowledge.
