# Familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP) - Management

## Overview and treatment intent

FAP is a hereditary polyposis syndrome caused by a germline APC mutation, inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern (a substantial minority of cases arise de novo). Classic disease produces hundreds to thousands of colorectal adenomas beginning in the teens, and if the colon is left in place essentially all affected patients eventually progress to colorectal cancer. An attenuated form exists with a lower polyp burden, a more proximal distribution, and later onset of cancer risk, generally in the fifties or sixties. Management is therefore organized around prevention of colorectal cancer (surgery, chemoprevention, surveillance) rather than treatment of an established malignancy, with a separate surveillance track for the extracolonic manifestations of the syndrome.

## Role of surgery

Risk-reducing colectomy or restorative proctocolectomy is the definitive intervention and the central pillar of management once polyp burden becomes significant; it is planned rather than reactive, aiming to remove the colon before malignant transformation occurs. After colectomy, the retained rectum (if a subtotal colectomy is performed) or the ileal pouch requires lifelong endoscopic surveillance with removal of any significant polyps that develop. Abdominal surgery itself is recognized as a factor that can provoke desmoid tumor formation, so the decision on timing and extent of surgery has to weigh this risk. Desmoid tumors, once established, are managed with cross-sectional imaging surveillance, and are a major driver of morbidity and mortality after colectomy.

## Chemoprevention

Chemoprevention with a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory agent, such as sulindac, or a COX-2 inhibitor, can reduce adenoma burden. This is used as an adjunct to surgery and surveillance, not as a substitute for risk-reducing colectomy.

## Extracolonic surveillance and management

- Upper gastrointestinal tract: side-viewing duodenoscopy surveillance for duodenal and periampullary adenomas, with endoscopic or surgical treatment of lesions showing advanced histology (there is a recognized risk of duodenal or ampullary adenocarcinoma in this population).
- Desmoid tumors: cross-sectional imaging surveillance, particularly after abdominal surgery.
- Thyroid: ultrasound surveillance for papillary thyroid carcinoma.
- Eyes: ophthalmological examination for congenital hypertrophy of the retinal pigment epithelium, which can also serve as a clinical clue to the diagnosis.
- Brain tumors (glioblastoma or medulloblastoma) occur in the Turcot syndrome variant of FAP; neurological features in a patient with colonic polyposis should prompt evaluation for this association.

## Screening intervals for FAP and at-risk relatives

For patients known or suspected to have FAP, flexible sigmoidoscopy screening starts at ages 10 to 12 years. Once genetic or endoscopic confirmation is established, upper endoscopy with a side-viewing scope should be performed every 1 to 3 years. Genetic counseling with germline APC testing, and cascade testing of at-risk relatives, underpins this surveillance program; APC protein truncation testing is the preferred genetic test, with gene sequencing or linkage testing as less desirable alternatives when truncation testing is uninformative.

## Lines of therapy and referral

There is no staged systemic or radiotherapy pathway for FAP itself; DeVita's discussion of FAP centers on genetics, surgery, chemoprevention, and surveillance rather than any drug-based line-of-therapy sequence, so no such sequence is asserted here. If a patient with FAP develops an established colorectal cancer, that cancer is managed according to standard colorectal cancer staging and treatment pathways (surgical resection with consideration of systemic therapy as dictated by stage), which is a separate management track from the polyposis syndrome itself and is not detailed in this note.

Refer to a clinical genetics service for germline testing and cascade screening of relatives, and to a specialist polyposis registry and multidisciplinary team (colorectal surgery, gastroenterology, genetics) for coordinated lifelong surveillance. Refer promptly for evaluation of a rapidly increasing polyp burden or a discrete mass suggesting malignant transformation, a symptomatic or enlarging intra-abdominal desmoid causing bowel or ureteric compromise, a duodenal or periampullary adenoma with advanced histology, or new neurological features.

## What DeVita does not specify

DeVita's colon cancer chapter does not give a numeric age or interval for starting duodenal surveillance independent of genetic confirmation, nor does it specify a systemic therapy regimen for polyposis itself. Those points are omitted here rather than inferred.

Sources: DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of Oncology, 12th ed.
