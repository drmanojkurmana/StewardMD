# Gastrointestinal Stromal Tumour (GIST) - Management

## Overview
GISTs are mesenchymal neoplasms arising from the interstitial cells of Cajal in the myenteric plexus, most commonly driven by activating mutations in KIT or, less often, PDGFRA. A minority are "wild type" for both (including SDH-deficient GIST and NF-1-associated GIST), which behave differently and respond poorly to standard tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs). Because genotype predicts both prognosis and drug sensitivity, mutational analysis of KIT and PDGFRA (with SDH immunohistochemistry when both are negative) is considered a near-mandatory companion to pathologic diagnosis in essentially all GISTs being considered for medical therapy.

Diagnosis is usually made on a core needle biopsy (endoscopic ultrasound-guided for gastric/rectal lesions, or CT/ultrasound-guided percutaneous biopsy) obtained before any surgical planning, since GISTs sit within a broad differential of abdominal masses (epithelial tumours, neuroendocrine tumours, lymphoma, retroperitoneal sarcoma, desmoid fibromatosis, germ cell tumours) for which surgery is not always the right first step.

## Staging and risk stratification
A conventional TNM-style stage is rarely used clinically. Instead:
- Disease is first classified as localized versus metastatic.
- For localized, resectable disease, relapse risk is estimated from mitotic count (the strongest prognostic factor), tumour size, and site of origin (gastric primaries carry a better prognosis than small bowel or rectal primaries), using tools such as the Armed Forces Institute of Pathology risk criteria, the Memorial Sloan Kettering nomogram, or contour prognostic maps.
- Tumour rupture (spillage, piecemeal resection, incisional biopsy, GI perforation into the peritoneal cavity, blood-tinged ascites, or microscopic transperitoneal infiltration) is a strong independent adverse factor and should be distinguished from minor capsule defects (needle biopsy tracks, microscopically positive margins), which do not carry the same risk.
- CT of the abdomen (and chest, since rectal GIST can spread to lung) is the staging study of choice; lymph node and bone metastases are rare.

## Localized disease
Surgery is the mainstay for localized disease.
- All GISTs 2 cm or larger should generally be resected, since none can be regarded as reliably benign.
- For lesions 1 to 2 cm, either observation or resection is reasonable; a reliable mitotic index cannot be obtained from biopsy or fine-needle aspiration, so risk cannot be fully characterized before removal. Endoscopic resection of small gastric lesions is an option at experienced centres. Any small lesion that is symptomatic or growing on surveillance should be resected regardless of size.
- The surgical goal is a macroscopically complete (R0) resection; wedge or segmental resection with margins narrower than those used for adenocarcinoma is standard, escalating to more extensive resection (e.g. total gastrectomy, pancreaticoduodenectomy, abdominoperineal resection) only when required for complete removal. Routine lymphadenectomy is not needed since nodal spread is rare in adult GIST.
- Tumour rupture or capsule violation during surgery must be avoided; it carries a markedly higher recurrence risk.
- Preoperative (neoadjuvant) imatinib should be considered when it would allow a smaller, less mutilating operation (for example shrinking a large gastric, periampullary, or rectal GIST) or when the surgeon judges the mass to carry a high intraoperative rupture risk. Response to preoperative imatinib is monitored clinically and radiologically over roughly six to twelve months, the interval over which maximal shrinkage is typically seen; PET can show early response within weeks. Mutational status should be checked first, since benefit depends on the tumour being genotypically imatinib-sensitive.
- Rare syndromic GISTs (SDH-deficient, NF-1-associated) can be multifocal, behave more indolently, and are less sensitive to available TKIs; the extent of surgery in these patients should be individualized rather than following the standard algorithm.

## Adjuvant therapy
Adjuvant imatinib after complete resection meaningfully prolongs the relapse-free interval in patients with a significant risk of relapse, though the benefit tends to attenuate over the one to three years after stopping therapy, and a clear cure-fraction benefit has not been demonstrated.
- Standard adjuvant duration is three years, reserved for patients whose estimated relapse risk is high enough (commonly framed as roughly a 30 to 50 percent risk range) to justify prolonged therapy, decided together with the patient.
- Adjuvant therapy should only be offered when the tumour genotype is one known to be imatinib-sensitive (KIT-mutated, or PDGFRA-mutated excluding the imatinib-resistant D842V and related exon 18 variants). Genotypically insensitive ("wild type") GISTs are not currently selected for adjuvant treatment, in part because their natural history is also generally less aggressive.
- Decisions about adjuvant dosing in exon 9 KIT-mutated GIST are unresolved; retrospective evidence is mixed.

## Metastatic or unresectable disease
Systemic therapy with a TKI is the standard of care once disease is metastatic or locally unresectable, continued indefinitely rather than for a fixed duration. Stopping therapy, even after a prolonged response, is followed by progression within months in most patients, and while restarting a TKI usually reinduces response, that response tends to be of lower quality than the original one.

Lines of therapy (agents named generically; no specific dosing given here):
- First line: imatinib, a KIT/PDGFRA/PDGFR/ABL kinase inhibitor, for genotypes known to be imatinib-sensitive. Response probability correlates strongly with mutation type (exon 11 KIT mutations respond well; exon 9 KIT mutations respond less consistently at standard exposure). PDGFRA D842V and related resistant mutations do not respond to imatinib; avapritinib, a KIT/PDGFRA inhibitor developed specifically against D842V and related exon 18 mutations, is highly active in that genotype instead, though it carries a risk of cognitive-type neurotoxicity that needs to be watched for clinically.
- On clinical progression on standard-exposure imatinib, dose escalation is a widely used option and produces a temporary benefit in a subset of patients, though part of that benefit may reflect correction of adherence or pharmacokinetic issues rather than true added potency.
- Second line: sunitinib, a KIT/PDGFRA inhibitor with additional antiangiogenic activity, improves progression-free survival compared with placebo in imatinib-refractory or imatinib-intolerant disease and has activity against exon 9 KIT mutations and some ATP-binding-pocket secondary mutations that are less affected by imatinib.
- Third line: regorafenib, a multitargeted KIT/PDGFRA inhibitor with antiangiogenic activity, improves progression-free survival compared with placebo after failure of both imatinib and sunitinib.
- Fourth line: ripretinib, a broad-spectrum KIT/PDGFRA inhibitor active against many secondary resistance mutations, improves progression-free survival compared with placebo in this setting; dose escalation can rescue response in some patients who progress at the standard dose.
- For SDH-deficient GIST (essentially imatinib-insensitive), sunitinib or regorafenib have shown some activity and can be considered, factoring in the generally more indolent natural history of this subtype.

Progression on a TKI is often due to secondary resistance mutations in the same driver gene (in KIT-mutated disease, typically in the exons encoding the ATP-binding pocket or activation loop; in PDGFRA-mutated disease, typically D842V) and can be heterogeneous across and even within lesions, which is a major limitation for subsequent-line targeted agents. Before attributing progression to molecular resistance, adherence should be checked, since covert non-adherence is a recognized confounder.

"Limited" progression (for example a nodule appearing within an otherwise responding lesion, versus generalized progression across multiple sites) is sometimes managed by continuing the current TKI while using surgery or ablative techniques to control the progressing focus, deferring a switch to the next line, though this approach is not supported by prospective trial data.

## Role of surgery in advanced/metastatic disease
Surgical resection of residual, imatinib-responding metastatic disease is used at some institutions in an attempt to reduce tumour burden, but its prognostic benefit is unproven; retrospective series suggesting benefit are subject to selection bias, and a prospective randomized trial failed to accrue. When it is used, only patients in whom complete resection of all visible disease is feasible should be considered, and the TKI must be continued after surgery regardless of how complete the resection appears, since even patients with a complete surgical response can relapse off therapy.

## Radiation therapy
Radiotherapy is not established as a role in GIST management in this ground truth; no organ-specific curative or palliative radiotherapy indication for GIST was identified. This gap should be treated as guideline-general rather than filled in.

## Monitoring and follow-up
- After potentially curative surgery, follow-up is aimed at detecting relapse early; routine endoscopy is not needed because local relapse tends to grow outward from the GI wall rather than into the lumen.
- CT of the abdomen is the most sensitive follow-up study for peritoneal and liver metastases (the two dominant sites of spread); MRI is an acceptable alternative, while ultrasound is much less sensitive for peritoneal disease.
- The period of highest relapse risk is roughly two to three years after surgery, or after completion of adjuvant therapy if given; late relapses occur but are uncommon, more often in low mitotic rate tumours.
- On TKI therapy, response is followed with periodic cross-sectional imaging (PET can show very early metabolic response, within about 24 hours of the first dose, and can also be an early signal of resistance if uptake rises again); a rise in tracer uptake on PET while imaging otherwise looks stable can be an early warning of emerging resistance.

## When to refer
GIST is uncommon enough that management should be planned by a multidisciplinary team with sarcoma/GIST-specific expertise, particularly for: any lesion where neoadjuvant therapy is being considered to downsize before surgery, syndromic presentations (SDH-deficient, NF-1-associated, or familial germline KIT/PDGFRA GIST), rectal or periampullary GIST where the extent of surgery has a large impact on function, cases with genotypes resistant to standard TKIs (D842V and related), and any patient being considered for surgery on residual metastatic disease, given how unsettled that decision is.
