# Splenic Marginal Zone Lymphoma (SMZL) - Management Narrative

## Overview and treatment intent

SMZL is an indolent B-cell lymphoma and almost all patients present with stage IV disease because of marrow and blood involvement, so staging-driven treatment intent (as used for many solid tumours) does not really apply here. Intent is instead driven by symptom burden: many patients do not need therapy at diagnosis, and treatment is reserved for those with symptomatic splenomegaly or significant cytopenias.

## Initial approach: observation versus treatment

- Asymptomatic patients without splenomegaly or cytopenias can be observed (watchful waiting) rather than treated immediately.
- Treatment is indicated once a patient develops symptomatic splenomegaly and/or significant cytopenias.

## Hepatitis C-associated disease: treat the virus first

A distinctive, disease-specific point is that SMZL has been associated with viral infections, in particular hepatitis C virus, and treating the hepatitis C infection has been shown to induce regression of the lymphoma. For patients found to have hepatitis C, antiviral treatment of the infection is a reasonable first step, since tumor growth and survival appear to be promoted by factors elaborated in response to hepatitis C antigens.

## Role of surgery (splenectomy)

Splenectomy is a reasonable option for selected patients who require treatment and can provide excellent relief of symptoms and cytopenias. In the source data, splenectomy was associated with high response rates and favourable 5-year progression-free and overall survival.

## Role of radiotherapy

For patients who are not surgical candidates, splenic radiation has some utility. The entire spleen is irradiated, and the total dose must be kept under renal tolerance because the left kidney is almost always in the treatment field.

## Systemic therapy

- Single-agent anti-CD20 antibody therapy (rituximab) can improve splenomegaly and cytopenias in the great majority of patients, and induction followed by maintenance dosing produced high response rates with favourable 5-year survival outcomes in the data reviewed.
- Other options at relapse are similar to those used for follicular lymphoma and include retreatment with an anti-CD20 antibody, alkylating agents, and purine analogs combined with an anti-CD20 antibody.
- A Bruton tyrosine kinase inhibitor (ibrutinib) is also listed among the options used at relapse for this disease.
- More broadly for marginal zone lymphomas as a group (not specific to the splenic subtype), Bruton tyrosine kinase inhibitors have documented activity in relapsed disease, and PI3K-pathway inhibition has also shown activity in this setting; CD19-directed CAR T-cell therapy is being explored for relapsed/refractory marginal zone lymphoma in later lines. These class-level relapsed/refractory options are grounded in the broader marginal zone lymphoma discussion in the same source chapter, not in a statement specific to the splenic subtype, so they should be read as options extrapolated from marginal zone lymphoma biology rather than as splenic MZL-specific evidence.

## Lines of therapy, in practical order

1. Confirm need for treatment (observe if asymptomatic).
2. If hepatitis C-positive, treat the viral infection as an initial, potentially disease-modifying step.
3. For patients needing treatment who are surgical candidates, consider splenectomy for symptom and cytopenia relief; splenic radiotherapy is an alternative for those who are not surgical candidates.
4. Anti-CD20 antibody therapy (as a single agent, or combined with chemotherapy) for symptomatic, progressive, or HCV-negative disease.
5. At relapse: retreatment with anti-CD20 antibody-based approaches, alkylating agent or purine analog plus anti-CD20 antibody combinations, or a Bruton tyrosine kinase inhibitor.

## Monitoring

Monitor blood counts and clinical status (spleen size, cytopenias, constitutional symptoms) for disease progression. Because indolent lymphomas including marginal zone lymphomas can undergo histologic transformation, monitor for signs suggesting transformation to a more aggressive lymphoma, such as a rapid change in nodal or splenic size, new B symptoms, or a rising lactate dehydrogenase (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease, for the specific transformation red-flag triad, though transformation risk itself is discussed for related indolent lymphomas in the source).

## When to refer

Refer to haemato-oncology for confirmation of diagnosis (immunophenotyping to exclude other small B-cell lymphomas), for hepatitis C testing and coordination with hepatology/infectious disease if positive, for decisions about splenectomy versus splenic radiotherapy versus systemic therapy, and urgently if there are signs of transformation, severe cytopenias, or splenic complications such as rupture or infarction (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease, for the referral triggers listed here beyond the core treatment-option statements above).

Sources: DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of Oncology, 12th ed.
