# Sezary syndrome (SS) - Management

Aliases: SS, leukaemic cutaneous T-cell lymphoma, erythrodermic mycosis fungoides variant, Sezary-Lutzner syndrome. SS is grouped with mycosis fungoides (MF) as MF/SS in DeVita's cutaneous lymphoma chapter, and its treatment approach largely follows the shared MF/SS framework, with certain agents specifically preferred in SS.

## Overall approach and intent

Treatment is guided by clinical stage and extent of skin, blood, node, and visceral involvement rather than by a single fixed protocol. Early-stage disease confined mainly to skin can have long-term control with skin-directed therapy, but SS by definition involves a significant circulating clonal T-cell burden, so most patients are managed with systemic and immunomodulatory approaches from the outset. Advanced disease, extensive nodal or visceral involvement, and large-cell transformation are treated with intent to palliate and control rather than to cure with conventional therapy; allogeneic stem cell transplantation is the only modality reported to offer durable, potentially curative disease control in eligible patients with relapsed/refractory disease.

All confirmed or suspected cases should be evaluated at a tertiary or specialized cutaneous lymphoma center given the complexity of staging and the number of treatment options.

## Skin-directed and phototherapy-based approaches

- Topical corticosteroids and antipruritic measures are used across all stages for symptom control (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Total-skin electron beam therapy (TSEBT) is an established option for erythrodermic (T4) disease, including SS, and can also be used for symptomatic palliation. It is generally well tolerated, and repeat courses have been used for recurrence.
- Extracorporeal photopheresis (ECP), which involves leukapheresis followed by ex vivo UVA/methoxypsoralen exposure of mononuclear cells and reinfusion, has direct activity against circulating Sezary cells and is a mainstay for patients with a significant blood/leukemic component; it is commonly combined with bexarotene and/or interferon-alfa in practice, which appears synergistic and can shorten time to response.
- Combination of ECP with TSEBT has been associated with improved cause-specific survival in erythrodermic patients in retrospective series.
- Standard phototherapy (narrowband UVB, PUVA) is more relevant to patch/plaque MF than to the leukemic phenotype of SS, but may still contribute to skin disease control as part of a combined regimen.

## Systemic and immunomodulatory therapy

- Interferon-alfa has documented activity across MF/SS with a wide range of reported response rates, and is often combined with ECP or phototherapy.
- Bexarotene, an oral RXR-selective retinoid, is active both topically and systemically in MF/SS and alters T-cell skin-homing receptor expression; oral bexarotene has documented activity across stages including advanced disease. Its main toxicities are elevation of serum lipids/cholesterol and suppression of thyroid function, and it is frequently combined with ECP.
- Mogamulizumab, a humanized anti-CCR4 monoclonal antibody, showed a higher response rate specifically in SS than in skin-predominant MF in a randomized trial against vorinostat, including a high rate of clearance of circulating leukemic cells, and has become a preferred systemic agent for SS.
- Histone deacetylase inhibitors (romidepsin, vorinostat) have documented activity in CTCL/MF-SS and are FDA-approved options for refractory disease.
- Brentuximab vedotin (anti-CD30 antibody-drug conjugate) has documented activity in CD30-expressing MF/CTCL, with FDA approval in that setting; CD30 expression status should inform its use.
- Denileukin diftitox and alemtuzumab have documented activity in relapsed/refractory MF/SS but are used less commonly given toxicity and availability considerations; monoclonal-antibody approaches targeting the leukemic compartment are generally preferred over these when the disease burden is predominantly circulating, as in SS.

## Cytotoxic chemotherapy

Single-agent and combination chemotherapy can produce responses in advanced refractory disease, but responses are generally not durable, and combination regimens carry substantial myelosuppression and infection risk. Because of this, single-agent cytotoxic therapy (agents such as gemcitabine, pegylated liposomal doxorubicin, or nucleoside analogs) is generally preferred over multi-agent cytotoxic chemotherapy, which is reserved for patients who are refractory to other approaches or who present with extensive adenopathy or visceral involvement requiring more rapid disease control.

## Role of transplantation

Allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation is considered for relapsed/refractory MF/SS, particularly in younger, transplant-eligible patients with clinically aggressive disease who have exhausted other options. Reported outcomes show durable progression-free survival in a meaningful minority of patients, and it is regarded as the only potentially curative option for advanced or transplant-eligible disease. Incorporation of skin-directed radiotherapy (such as TSEBT) into the conditioning approach has been used in some series to improve disease control prior to transplant.

## Monitoring

- Serial assessment of skin disease burden (extent and type of lesion) alongside serial Sezary cell counts / circulating clonal burden is used to track disease and response to therapy.
- Imaging (CT, PET, or MRI) is recommended at initial staging of advanced disease and during follow-up to detect nodal or visceral progression, with biopsy of pathologically enlarged nodes.
- Bone marrow assessment should be performed in advanced disease, including SS, and when hematologic function is otherwise compromised.
- Watch specifically for large-cell transformation, which changes prognosis and treatment intensity, and for infection, given the profound immunosuppression and skin-barrier compromise characteristic of SS.

## When to refer

- All confirmed or suspected cases of SS/CTCL should be referred to or co-managed with a specialized cutaneous lymphoma / hemato-oncology center given the complexity of staging and the breadth of treatment options.
- Refer for transplant evaluation in relapsed/refractory disease, particularly in younger patients or those with clinically aggressive or transformed disease.
- Refer promptly for management of infection, given that immunosuppression and skin-barrier failure are major drivers of morbidity and mortality in SS.
