# Sinonasal Undifferentiated Carcinoma (SNUC) - Management

## Overall approach

Sinonasal undifferentiated carcinoma is treated with a multimodality strategy combining chemotherapy, radiotherapy and surgery, and this combined approach is described as the standard of care for the disease. Because SNUC is aggressive and usually bulky at presentation, treatment is planned upfront by a head-and-neck oncology multidisciplinary team rather than committing to a single modality (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease, though DeVita frames multimodality treatment as the standard for SNUC specifically).

## Initial evaluation before treatment

- MRI and PET-CT to define the full extent of disease, including skull base, orbital and intracranial involvement, before any treatment decision is made.
- Assessment for vision loss at baseline, since orbital/optic nerve involvement changes the initial sequence of care.
- If vision loss is present, debulking surgery and optic nerve decompression is considered before systemic therapy.

## Induction chemotherapy as the pivotal first step

For patients without an urgent surgical indication (such as vision-threatening compression), treatment begins with induction chemotherapy, and the tumor's response to this induction phase is then used to guide the definitive treatment that follows:

- An induction regimen combining a platinum agent, a taxane, and a fluoropyrimidine (described in one institutional series) has been used, though exact agent selection is an institutional choice and not a fixed rule.
- Response is reassessed with MRI (and PET-CT) after induction.
- Good radiologic responders (partial or complete response) proceed to definitive concurrent chemoradiotherapy.
- Poor responders are managed with surgical resection instead, and re-evaluation of the histopathology is considered in this group, since a poor response can prompt reconsideration of the diagnosis (for example excluding NUT carcinoma or SMARCB1-deficient carcinoma, which mimic SNUC but behave and respond differently).

## Outcome by response pattern

- In one series, patients whose tumor showed a partial or complete radiologic response to induction chemotherapy and then received definitive chemoradiotherapy had a 5-year disease-free survival rate of 81%, compared with 54% for responders who instead went to definitive surgery with postoperative radiotherapy or chemoradiotherapy.
- Among patients who did NOT respond to induction chemotherapy, the 5-year disease-specific survival was 0% for those who then received concurrent chemoradiotherapy versus 39% for those who instead underwent surgery plus radiotherapy or chemoradiotherapy.
- This response-outcome relationship is the rationale for using induction response, rather than a fixed protocol, to select the definitive local treatment (chemoradiotherapy versus surgery).

## Definitive local treatment

- Concurrent chemoradiotherapy for good responders to induction, using a platinum-based radiosensitizing regimen (an institutional protocol paired weekly platinum-based chemotherapy, with or without a taxane, with radiotherapy).
- Craniofacial or endoscopic skull-base resection with adjuvant chemoradiotherapy for poor responders or otherwise selected resectable cases, done in conjunction with neurosurgery (and ophthalmology when the orbit is involved), including reconstruction as needed.
- After definitive treatment, residual metabolically active disease on repeat MRI/PET-CT prompts further evaluation rather than automatic observation; disease-free patients move to surveillance.

## Surveillance

Follow-up combines clinical nasal endoscopy with cross-sectional imaging to detect local recurrence or distant metastasis, both of which are common in SNUC. Lysis of nasal synechiae is performed as needed during follow-up, particularly after high-dose radiotherapy to the nasal cavity, which can cause nasal narrowing and synechiae.

## Complications to anticipate and manage

- Surgical: infection, poor wound healing, midface numbness or weakness, trismus, cerebrospinal fluid leak, epiphora and hemorrhage; craniofacial resection specifically carries risk of meningitis, subdural abscess, CSF leak, diplopia and hemorrhage.
- Radiotherapy: the eye is the most common site of significant toxicity. Irradiating only the medial portion of the ipsilateral eye usually preserves vision, but extensive orbital disease requiring irradiation of the whole eye carries a near-certain risk of vision loss (these same patients would otherwise need orbital exenteration if treated surgically instead). A transient central nervous system syndrome (vertigo, headache, decreased cerebration, lethargy) can appear two to three months after treatment and resolve over one to two months. Aseptic meningitis, chronic sinusitis and serous otitis media can also occur, and septal perforation can result where tumor has destroyed the septum.
- Proton therapy is an emerging option for reducing chronic patient-reported toxicity (xerostomia, dysphagia) in sinonasal cancer generally, based on one outcomes series, though this was not reported specifically for SNUC.

## When to refer

- Refer to a head-and-neck oncology multidisciplinary team (surgery, radiation oncology, medical oncology) at diagnosis given the need for coordinated sequencing of induction chemotherapy, response assessment and definitive local therapy.
- Refer to ophthalmology and neurosurgery whenever orbital or intracranial extension is present, both at initial evaluation and if debulking/decompression is being considered before treatment.
- Consider referral to a center able to offer clinical trial enrollment, given the rarity of the disease and the absence of a single established regimen.
- Escalate urgently for new vision loss, rapidly progressive cranial neuropathy, or evidence of intracranial extension, all of which can change the initial treatment sequence.

Sources: DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of Oncology, 12th ed. NCCN Guidelines (used for the general multidisciplinary-referral framing noted above).
