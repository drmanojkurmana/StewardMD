# Radiation Proctitis - Management Narrative

Radiation proctitis (radiation proctopathy) is rectal mucosal injury following pelvic radiotherapy, most often for prostate, cervical, rectal, bladder, or other pelvic malignancies. Management differs by phase (acute versus chronic/late) and by severity, and it sits alongside management of the underlying cancer rather than replacing it.

## Acute radiation proctitis (during or within weeks of pelvic RT)

Acute injury occurs within about six weeks of starting radiotherapy and typically presents with diarrhea, cramping, rectal urgency or tenesmus, and occasionally bleeding; these symptoms usually resolve without specific therapy within two to six months of completing treatment.

- First-line management is conservative and supportive: dietary manipulation (reducing dietary fiber/residue and lactose during active symptoms) is the initial step for whole-pelvic irradiation-related bowel symptoms.
- If symptoms are more than mild and transient, antidiarrheal agents (loperamide or diphenoxylate/atropine class agents) are appropriate.
- Inflamed internal or external hemorrhoids occurring during treatment are managed with sitz baths and topical hydrocortisone.
- Radiotherapy itself is generally continued through mild-to-moderate acute proctitis; treatment breaks are reserved for more severe symptoms, decided jointly with the treating radiation oncologist (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Acute symptoms do not usually require endoscopy or biopsy; these are reserved for atypical presentations or when chronic changes/bleeding develop.

## Late (chronic) radiation proctitis / proctopathy

Late rectal toxicity typically manifests within the first three years after radiotherapy (rare after five years) and results from progressive small-vessel injury (obliterative endarteritis) and fibrosis, producing mucosal ischemia and fragile telangiectatic vessels; it can cause rectal bleeding, mucous discharge, mild incontinence, and in severe cases ulceration, stricture, or fistula.

- Milder rectal bleeding and mucous discharge are treated with topical steroid suppositories, sitz baths, and an increase in dietary fiber.
- For bleeding not responsive to these conservative measures, endoscopic argon plasma (argon-beam) coagulation is used to ablate telangiectatic vessels and reduce the frequency of bleeding episodes.
- Hyperbaric oxygen therapy has a role in refractory cases; the rationale is that it improves oxygen delivery to ischemic rectal mucosa, promoting angiogenesis and mucosal/fibroblast healing.
- Topical sucralfate is an additional option that has been used for haemorrhagic radiation proctopathy refractory to simpler measures (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Formalin application to the rectal mucosa is another described option for refractory bleeding (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Chronic bleeding should prompt evaluation for iron-deficiency anaemia, with iron replacement and transfusion as needed (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Role of biopsy, endoscopy and surgery

- Because irradiated rectal tissue is fragile and poorly healing, deep rectal biopsies and cauterization/other instrumentation should be avoided unless absolutely necessary, given the elevated risk of precipitating a fistula.
- Endoscopy is used diagnostically to document telangiectasia and mucosal change and to exclude other causes, but biopsy should be performed cautiously for the same fistula-risk reason.
- Surgical referral is reserved for stricture, fistula formation, or bleeding refractory to endoscopic and medical measures; a cautious, imaging-informed approach is preferred before intervening surgically on irradiated bowel (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease, consistent with the fistula-risk caution above).

## Distinguishing from progression and other causes

Before attributing bowel symptoms to radiation injury, recurrent or new pelvic malignancy, inflammatory bowel disease, infective proctitis, and ischemic proctitis should be excluded, particularly when bleeding is new, progressive, or associated with other red-flag features (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Prevention / technique considerations relevant when radiotherapy is being planned

Where radiation proctitis is being anticipated rather than already established, modern image-guided, conformal external-beam techniques keep severe (grade 3 or higher) late gastrointestinal toxicity low, and use of a rectal spacer at the time of prostate radiotherapy planning further reduces the incidence and severity of late rectal toxicity and preserves bowel-related quality of life compared with treatment without a spacer. Conversely, dose escalation strategies such as the addition of a brachytherapy boost to external-beam radiotherapy increase the rate of severe late gastrointestinal (and genitourinary) toxicity, so the intensity of pelvic radiotherapy is weighed against this risk during initial cancer treatment planning.

## Monitoring

- Patients with a history of pelvic radiotherapy who develop new or worsening bowel symptoms warrant assessment for both acute-phase self-limited injury and, if temporally appropriate (many months to years out), late radiation proctopathy.
- Ongoing or recurrent bleeding should be monitored with blood counts and iron studies, and endoscopic follow-up used judiciously (favoring visual assessment over biopsy) to track mucosal changes and response to ablative therapy (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## When to refer

- Severe or transfusion-dependent rectal bleeding, suspected fistula (e.g., rectovaginal or rectovesical), or stricture with obstruction warrants referral to gastroenterology/colorectal surgery and, where relevant, the treating radiation oncologist.
- Bleeding refractory to topical/medical therapy should be referred for endoscopic argon plasma coagulation or, if that fails, consideration of hyperbaric oxygen therapy.
- Any suspicion of recurrent or new pelvic malignancy as the cause of symptoms should prompt urgent oncology re-evaluation rather than assuming a purely radiation-related cause.

Sources: DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of Oncology, 12th ed.
