# Salivary gland tumour (salivary gland carcinoma / SGM) - management narrative

## Overview and treatment framework

Salivary gland malignancies (SGMs) are uncommon and histologically heterogeneous (mucoepidermoid carcinoma and adenoid cystic carcinoma, ACC, are the most common malignant subtypes; pleomorphic adenoma is the most common benign tumour). Because prospective evidence is limited, management guidance is largely built on retrospective series and expert consensus (ASCO guidelines). The standard approach across stages is definitive surgery followed by pathological risk-based use of postoperative radiotherapy. Chemotherapy was historically considered ineffective for SGMs, but molecular characterisation has identified actionable targets that inform systemic therapy in selected patients, particularly in the recurrent/metastatic setting.

Tumours are stratified into low, intermediate, and high risk groups based on histological type and grade, and this risk grouping (together with margin status and nodal involvement) drives the decision for adjuvant radiotherapy. Grade is not applied to ACC for treatment decisions: because ACC is locally aggressive with a strong propensity for perineural spread regardless of grade, all ACC is treated with surgery plus irradiation.

## Surgery

- Surgery is the gold-standard initial treatment for SGMs.
- For parotid tumours, superficial or total parotidectomy is performed with meticulous dissection to preserve the facial nerve whenever oncologically feasible; the nerve is sacrificed only where directly invaded, with facial nerve rehabilitation (grafting or reanimation) considered afterward.
- Adequate excision with a cuff of normal tissue is required for pleomorphic adenoma; enucleation is avoided because it raises the risk of recurrence and later malignant transformation.
- Benign lesions and small, early-stage cancers confined to the submandibular gland may be treated with gland excision alone; more extensive malignancies require en bloc resection incorporating the gland and adjacent level Ib nodes, with resection of adjacent muscle, nerves (lingual/hypoglossal), bone, or skin as needed depending on invasion. Sublingual gland primaries are approached similarly, balancing oncologic control against functional outcome.
- Neck dissection: clinically node-positive patients should have a therapeutic neck dissection. Elective neck dissection (for the clinically node-negative neck) is generally reserved for high-risk/high-grade tumours and locally advanced primaries, since occult nodal metastasis risk rises with higher histologic grade; elective neck dissection may improve regional control and survival in this group.
- Facial nerve weakness, marginal mandibular nerve injury (submandibular resections), and hypoglossal nerve injury are recognised surgical complications; most facial nerve weakness resolves within the first year.
- For patients who are medically unfit for surgery, or whose disease is unresectable due to invasion or location, definitive radiotherapy is an option, with concurrent platinum-based chemotherapy considered to improve locoregional control when surgery is not feasible (evidence here is limited).

## Radiotherapy

- Adjuvant (postoperative) radiotherapy is used in a risk-based way: it is indicated for high-grade tumours, positive margins, perineural invasion, or nodal disease, and is used for essentially all resected ACC given its aggressive local/perineural behaviour.
- Practice around postoperative radiotherapy varies by institution; some low-risk malignancies (certain low-grade histologies with negative margins) can be managed with surgery alone, while postoperative radiotherapy is favoured for multiple recurrences, capsular rupture, or positive margins where repeat salvage surgery would be morbid.
- Elective neck irradiation may be considered when an elective neck dissection is not performed, and is regarded as similarly effective to elective neck dissection for regional control in that setting.

## Systemic therapy

### Adjuvant/definitive setting
- The role of adjuvant concurrent chemoradiotherapy (added to postoperative radiotherapy) in SGMs remains controversial; available retrospective data have not shown a clear survival advantage over radiotherapy alone, and the largest hospital-registry analysis to date reported inferior survival with chemoradiotherapy (though details on toxicity and radiotherapy quality were incomplete). Randomised trial data (RTOG 1008, comparing adjuvant radiotherapy with chemoradiotherapy in high-risk SGM) are awaited to clarify this question.
- Until that trial reports, some clinicians favour adjuvant platinum-based chemoradiotherapy in resected high-risk SGM, reasoning by extrapolation from the benefit of chemoradiotherapy in resected mucosal squamous cell head and neck cancer with positive margins or extranodal extension.
- In high-grade subtypes with receptor overexpression (notably salivary duct carcinoma), targeted adjuvant approaches have been explored: HER2-directed therapy (a taxane-based platinum chemoradiotherapy regimen plus maintenance HER2-targeted antibody therapy) in HER2-positive resectable mucoepidermoid carcinoma, and androgen deprivation therapy (an LHRH analogue plus an antiandrogen) in androgen-receptor-positive mucoepidermoid carcinoma after resection, both based on retrospective cohort data rather than prospective confirmation; a prospective trial of HER2-targeted therapy combined with chemoradiotherapy in high-risk HER2-overexpressing salivary duct carcinoma is ongoing.

### Recurrent/metastatic disease
- Systemic therapy for recurrent or metastatic SGM should be individualised, weighing disease growth velocity, sites of involvement, symptoms, performance status, comorbidity, and threat to surrounding structures; some subtypes (notably ACC) can remain indolent for years, so observation without immediate systemic therapy is reasonable in asymptomatic, slowly progressive disease. A clinical trial should be prioritised where available, since there is no standard or approved therapy for advanced/metastatic SGM.
- Molecular testing should guide therapy selection: testing for NTRK gene fusion is important in mammary analog secretory carcinoma (a distinct non-ACC histology), and testing for HER2 overexpression/amplification and androgen receptor expression is relevant in other non-ACC histologies.
- For recurrent/metastatic ACC without an actionable molecular target or trial option, cytotoxic combination chemotherapy (regimens historically used include an anthracycline-based platinum combination, or a vinca-alkaloid-based regimen with or without a platinum agent) has shown historical response rates, though durability of response is generally short and toxicity is a concern; taxane-based chemotherapy is generally avoided in ACC given reported lack of efficacy. Antiangiogenic oral multitargeted tyrosine kinase inhibitors have shown disease-stabilising activity (partial response plus prolonged stable disease) in ACC in phase 2 trials, though not currently an approved indication, and toxicity commonly requires dose modification or discontinuation. Immune checkpoint blockade has shown only modest activity in ACC and in SGM more broadly, consistent with generally low tumour mutational burden and PD-L1 expression in this tumour class.
- For recurrent/metastatic non-ACC SGM: HER2-overexpressing disease can be treated with HER2-targeted antibody therapy combined with a taxane in the first line (extrapolating from HER2-positive breast cancer management), with continued HER2-targeted therapy using alternative HER2-directed agents considered at progression; androgen-receptor-positive disease can be treated with combined androgen blockade (an LHRH analogue plus an antiandrogen), extrapolating from prostate cancer management; when both HER2 and androgen receptor overexpression are present, the general preference is to sequence HER2-directed therapy first, followed by androgen-directed therapy at progression. NTRK fusion-positive mammary analog secretory carcinoma can be treated with an oral selective NTRK inhibitor, with high reported response rates in this molecularly selected group. In the absence of an actionable target or trial, cytotoxic chemotherapy can be considered, and non-ACC histologies may respond to a taxane-based regimen.

## Monitoring / surveillance

- Clinical follow-up with history and examination should mirror surveillance for upper aerodigestive tract squamous cell carcinoma: every 2 to 3 months for the first 2 years, every 6 months for years 3 to 5, then yearly thereafter.
- The need for surveillance imaging depends on histology, grade, and clinical presentation; surveillance MRI of the brain/neck is warranted when there is base-of-skull or named-nerve invasion, and at least an annual chest CT is recommended for high-grade/high-risk histologies (especially ACC and mucoepidermoid carcinoma) given their propensity for distant spread.
- Survivorship care should address xerostomia (dental hygiene counselling, biannual dental review, fluoride prophylaxis), and referral to lymphedema massage therapy and speech/swallow therapy where needed; trismus may require specific intervention. Thyroid-stimulating hormone should be checked annually in patients who received neck irradiation (especially bilateral), reflecting the risk of radiation-induced hypothyroidism.
- Adenoid cystic carcinoma in particular warrants prolonged follow-up because of its tendency to late recurrence and delayed distant metastasis, which can occur years after initial treatment.

## When to refer

- All patients with a suspected or confirmed malignant salivary gland tumour should be managed through a head and neck multidisciplinary team integrating surgery, radiation oncology, medical oncology, and pathology.
- Facial nerve weakness in association with a parotid mass is a strong indicator of malignant invasion and warrants urgent head and neck surgical evaluation.
- Refer for consideration of clinical trial enrolment in recurrent/metastatic disease, since this is the preferred option ahead of empiric systemic therapy where a trial is available, and refer for molecular/genomic tumour profiling (NTRK, HER2, androgen receptor, and other emerging targets) before selecting systemic therapy in the recurrent/metastatic setting.
