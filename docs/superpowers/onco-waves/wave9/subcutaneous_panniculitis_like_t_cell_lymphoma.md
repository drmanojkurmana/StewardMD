# Subcutaneous panniculitis-like T-cell lymphoma (SPTCL) — Management

## Framing the disease before choosing treatment intensity
SPTCL is defined by an alpha-beta cytotoxic T-cell infiltrate confined to subcutaneous fat, and this
alpha-beta phenotype behaves very differently from the gamma-delta cutaneous T-cell lymphomas that
were historically grouped with it. DeVita is explicit that the WHO-EORTC classification separated the
two phenotypes because of their disparate outcomes: alpha-beta SPTCL carries an excellent prognosis
(80 to 82 percent 5-year survival across the sources reviewed), while gamma-delta cutaneous T-cell
lymphoma behaves aggressively with a median survival on the order of 15 months in the reported series.
Confirming the alpha-beta phenotype (versus gamma-delta) on the diagnostic biopsy is therefore the
first branch point that determines how aggressively to treat, and this distinction should be revisited
if a patient's disease behaves atypically.

The second branch point is the presence or absence of hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH). DeVita
reports HLH in about 17 percent of SPTCL patients and notes it is associated with markedly worse
survival (46 percent versus 91 percent 5-year survival in patients without HLH). Patients with HLH are
identified by DeVita as needing combination chemotherapy and consideration for stem cell
transplantation, in contrast to the more indolent approach appropriate for uncomplicated disease.

## Treatment approach by disease behavior

**Uncomplicated alpha-beta SPTCL (no HLH), localized/indolent course**
- DeVita's cutaneous lymphoma chapter states that treatments for alpha-beta SPTCL include
  corticosteroids, single-agent chemotherapy, and radiotherapy.
- The disease can wax and wane, and DeVita notes many patients' course behaves like the cutaneous
  T-cell non-Hodgkin lymphomas generally, i.e. a relapsing but often skin-limited pattern, consistent
  with a step-up approach starting with the least aggressive option that controls symptoms.
- (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease) Localized, limited-extent
  lesions can be managed with local measures such as radiotherapy to the involved site, reserving
  systemic therapy for more extensive or symptomatic skin involvement.

**SPTCL with hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis, or otherwise aggressive/disseminated behavior**
- DeVita states plainly that patients with hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis merit combination
  chemotherapy and should be considered for stem cell transplantation (SCT).
- Both allogeneic and autologous hematopoietic stem cell transplantation have been used as a curative
  modality in this disease group; DeVita cites a series in which SPTCL and primary cutaneous
  gamma-delta T-cell lymphoma patients underwent allogeneic transplant with several long-term
  survivors, and a smaller number underwent autologous transplant with the two SPTCL/PCGD-TCL
  patients alive at intermediate follow-up. This informs transplant consideration for HLH-associated or
  relapsed disease rather than for uncomplicated presentations.
- (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease) Combination chemotherapy in
  this setting generally follows regimens used for peripheral T-cell lymphomas, with the choice and
  intensity individualized by fitness, extent of disease, and HLH severity; specific regimen selection
  and dosing sit outside this narrative and belong in structured protocol templates.

## Role of surgery, radiotherapy, and systemic therapy
- Surgery has no defined curative or debulking role in DeVita's discussion of this disease; biopsy is
  for diagnosis, not treatment, and DeVita does not describe a surgical treatment role for SPTCL.
- Radiotherapy is named by DeVita as one of the standard options for alpha-beta SPTCL, appropriate for
  localized cutaneous disease.
- Corticosteroids and single-agent chemotherapy are the other DeVita-named systemic options for the
  more indolent presentation.
- Combination (multi-agent) chemotherapy is reserved by DeVita for the HLH-associated or otherwise
  higher-risk presentation, escalating beyond the single-agent/local approach used for uncomplicated
  disease.

## Monitoring
- (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease) Because SPTCL can wax and
  wane and relapse in the skin, monitoring should track cutaneous lesion burden clinically over time
  rather than relying on a fixed imaging interval alone.
- Given DeVita's explicit statement that HLH is a major driver of mortality in this disease, patients
  should be monitored for HLH markers, cytopenias, fever, and organ dysfunction, both at diagnosis and
  at any sign of clinical worsening, so that the higher-intensity treatment pathway can be triggered
  promptly if HLH develops or recurs.
- (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease) Surveillance for systemic
  spread (nodal or marrow involvement) is reasonable at relapse or treatment failure, since DeVita
  notes systemic relapse can occur even though the disease is usually skin-limited at presentation.

## When to refer
- Confirmation of the alpha-beta versus gamma-delta phenotype requires expert hematopathology review
  (immunophenotyping for CD3, CD4, CD8, CD56, betaF1, and cytotoxic markers, plus T-cell receptor
  lineage and clonality studies), since DeVita identifies this distinction as prognostically critical;
  any diagnostic uncertainty should prompt referral to a center with lymphoma pathology expertise.
- Any patient developing features of hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis should be referred promptly to
  a hematology-oncology service experienced in HLH and lymphoma-directed combination chemotherapy,
  given the substantial survival gap DeVita reports between SPTCL with and without HLH.
- Patients being considered for stem cell transplantation, whether for HLH-associated disease or
  relapsed/refractory disease, should be referred to a transplant center, reflecting DeVita's discussion
  of allogeneic and autologous SCT as an established option in this disease group.

Sources: DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of Oncology, 12th ed.
