# Solitary Fibrous Tumour (including Haemangiopericytoma) — Management

## Overview of treatment intent

Solitary fibrous tumour (SFT) spans a spectrum from indolent lesions that are surgically cured to lesions that behave like other high-grade sarcomas, so management intent is set by resectability and by histologic risk features rather than by a fixed staging system. SFT and the older diagnosis of haemangiopericytoma are now treated as one entity, and management follows that of a fibroblastic soft tissue sarcoma.

## Localised, resectable disease

- Complete surgical resection is the treatment of choice for localised SFT, wherever it arises (deep soft tissue, retroperitoneum, meninges, or pleura).
- For pleural SFT specifically, resection strategy depends on the growth pattern: pedunculated tumours carry a better prognosis than tumours that spread over a broad pleural area, and complete resection should remove the tumour, its pedicle, and the site of origin.
- En bloc resection of adjacent lung, chest wall, or diaphragm may be required to achieve complete resection of a pleural SFT.
- Achieving complete resection is the single most important predictor of outcome for pleural SFT; reported 5-year survival after complete resection can be as high as 97%.
- If the tumour recurs locally, re-excision is again the treatment of choice, and long-term survival is possible after re-excision.
- Incomplete resection or malignant transformation is associated with a much shorter median survival, in the range of two to three years.

## Risk stratification

- Metastatic risk in SFT rises with tumour size greater than 10 cm and a mitotic rate greater than 4 per 10 high-power fields.
- Risk-stratification models that also weigh patient age and the presence of necrosis alongside size and mitotic rate are used to estimate recurrence and metastasis risk (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Retroperitoneal SFT behaves notably worse than SFT at other sites: reported cumulative incidence of distant recurrence at 10 years is 41%, with a disease-specific death rate of 34% at 10 years.
- Multidisciplinary discussion in a sarcoma tumour board to apply this risk stratification and plan surgery, radiotherapy, and follow-up intensity is standard practice for suspected or confirmed SFT (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Role of radiotherapy

Radiotherapy after resection, particularly for tumours with positive margins or high-risk histologic features, is used in soft tissue sarcoma management generally (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease). DeVita's discussion of SFT is silent on radiotherapy specifically, so its role for this tumour type in particular should be individualised by the sarcoma multidisciplinary team rather than treated as a default.

## Systemic therapy for advanced, unresectable, or metastatic disease

- SFT is highly resistant to standard anthracycline-based (doxorubicin-based) chemotherapy.
- Antiangiogenic tyrosine kinase inhibitors, specifically sunitinib and sorafenib, have shown activity in progressive or malignant SFT and are the systemic options with actual evidence in this tumour type.
- Because conventional cytotoxic chemotherapy underperforms in SFT, a trial of an antiangiogenic tyrosine kinase inhibitor is a reasonable line of therapy for unresectable or metastatic disease before or instead of anthracycline-based chemotherapy, guided by sarcoma specialist input.

## Doege-Potter syndrome (paraneoplastic hypoglycaemia)

A minority of SFTs, usually large tumours, produce an aberrant form of insulin-like growth factor 2 that causes non-islet-cell tumour hypoglycaemia (Doege-Potter syndrome). DeVita's section on SFT documents this association but does not detail its acute management. Correcting the hypoglycaemia and definitive treatment of the hypoglycaemia by resecting the tumour follow general oncology and endocrine practice (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Monitoring and follow-up

- Late local recurrence and late distant metastasis are recognised features of SFT, so surveillance needs to be prolonged rather than time-limited; the 10-year recurrence and mortality figures for retroperitoneal SFT above illustrate why extended follow-up matters even after apparently complete resection.
- Follow-up should include imaging of the primary site (and the chest, given the potential for pulmonary metastasis) for an extended period after treatment, with the exact interval and duration individualised by risk category (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## When to refer

- Any suspected or confirmed SFT with high-risk features (large size, elevated mitotic rate, retroperitoneal location, incomplete resection, or malignant/dedifferentiated histology) warrants referral to a sarcoma specialist centre or multidisciplinary team for surgical planning and consideration of adjuvant therapy (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Pleural SFT with a broad-based growth pattern, suspected chest wall, lung, or diaphragmatic involvement, or recurrence after prior resection should be referred to a thoracic surgical team experienced in en bloc resection.
- New hypoglycaemia in a patient with a known large SFT should prompt urgent endocrine and oncology input given the possibility of Doege-Potter syndrome.

Sources: DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of Oncology, 12th ed.
