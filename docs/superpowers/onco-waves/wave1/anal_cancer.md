# Anal Cancer: Management Narrative

## Overview and treatment philosophy

The great majority of anal cancers are squamous cell carcinomas, and unlike most gastrointestinal malignancies they have a relatively low propensity for distant metastatic spread, which makes locoregional control the central goal of therapy. Treatment has evolved from radical surgery (abdominoperineal resection, APR) as the historical first-line approach, with poor long-term disease-free survival and permanent colostomy, to sphincter-preserving definitive chemoradiotherapy as the standard of care for localized disease. Randomized trials have repeatedly shown that adding chemotherapy to radiation improves locoregional control and colostomy-free survival compared with radiation alone, at the cost of greater acute toxicity, so surgery is now reserved mainly for salvage after chemoradiotherapy fails or disease recurs.

## Staging and pretreatment work-up

Staging combines digital rectal examination, anoscopy or proctoscopy (with biopsy of any suspicious lesion), assessment of the inguinal nodal basins, and cross-sectional imaging. CT of the chest and abdomen plus pelvic CT or MRI is standard, and PET-CT (or PET-MRI) is recommended where available because it has been shown to upstage or downstage a meaningful proportion of patients and to change management. In women, a gynecologic examination including cervical cancer screening is recommended because of the shared HPV-driven biology of anal and cervical disease. HIV testing and, where relevant, CD4 count should be obtained because HIV status can influence toxicity and management decisions. Staging follows the AJCC TNM system for anal cancer, with T stage based on tumor size and invasion of adjacent organs, and N stage based on involvement of inguinal, mesorectal, or iliac nodal groups; tumor size and nodal status are the dominant prognostic factors.

## Treatment by stage and intent

**Very early, superficial disease.** A small, well-differentiated subset of superficially invasive squamous carcinomas confined to the perianal skin, without sphincter involvement or nodal disease, may be adequately managed with local excision alone (with attention to margin status), reserving chemoradiotherapy for cases with close or positive margins or higher-risk features. This is a narrow, carefully selected group and is not the approach for typical anal canal squamous cell carcinoma.

**Localized (nonmetastatic) squamous cell carcinoma - the general case.** Definitive chemoradiotherapy combining pelvic radiotherapy with concurrent chemotherapy (a fluoropyrimidine-based regimen combined with mitomycin) is the standard curative approach across early- and locally advanced-stage disease, and it preserves the anal sphincter in most patients. Cisplatin-based concurrent chemoradiotherapy has been studied as an alternative to a mitomycin-containing regimen; large randomized comparisons found broadly similar efficacy between the two approaches, and mitomycin-based chemoradiotherapy has remained the more widely used standard given comparable outcomes, a simpler treatment course, and a different toxicity profile. Oral fluoropyrimidine-based regimens have shown comparable efficacy to infusional fluoropyrimidine-based regimens when combined with mitomycin and radiotherapy, and are used as an alternative.

Radiation is generally delivered by intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) rather than older 2D/3D conformal techniques, since IMRT reduces high-grade skin, gastrointestinal, and genitourinary toxicity while maintaining disease control.

For select older or frailer patients with early-stage, low-risk disease who cannot tolerate concurrent chemotherapy, radiotherapy alone can be considered, though patients without significant comorbidities should generally still receive chemoradiotherapy given the general association between chemotherapy omission and lower survival.

**Persistent or recurrent disease.** Response to chemoradiotherapy can be slow, and tumor regression may continue for up to about a year; repeat biopsy of a slowly regressing lesion is generally avoided because it risks nonhealing ulceration in irradiated tissue. Only clearly progressive or convincingly non-regressing/recurrent disease should be biopsied to confirm persistence or recurrence. When chronic persistence or true recurrence is confirmed, salvage APR (with groin dissection if inguinal nodes are involved, and en bloc/multivisceral resection if adjacent organs are invaded) is the mainstay, since surgery is generally the only curative option at this point. Achieving negative surgical margins is the strongest predictor of survival after salvage surgery; outcomes are best when disease is truly recurrent (as opposed to disease that never fully responded, which behaves more aggressively). Perineal wound complications are common after salvage APR, and tissue flap reconstruction (for example a myocutaneous flap) is frequently used to reduce wound breakdown.

**Metastatic disease.** Systemic chemotherapy is the treatment of choice for metastatic squamous cell carcinoma of the anus. A platinum agent combined with a taxane has demonstrated a more favorable balance of efficacy and toxicity than a platinum-fluoropyrimidine combination in a randomized comparison and is now the preferred first-line regimen in this setting according to updated guideline recommendations; a platinum-fluoropyrimidine combination remains an alternative option. A docetaxel-platinum-fluoropyrimidine triplet has also shown activity as a first-line option in this population. For carefully selected patients with limited metastatic disease (notably isolated liver metastases) and good performance status, surgical resection of metastatic disease (metastasectomy) can be considered and has been associated with improved survival in retrospective series, though prospective evidence defining selection criteria is lacking. Immune checkpoint inhibition (PD-1-targeted monotherapy) is recommended by consensus guidelines as second-line treatment for refractory, unresectable, or metastatic disease based on durable responses seen in previously treated patients, though response rates in that setting are modest.

## Role of surgery, radiotherapy, and systemic therapy summarized

- Surgery: not first-line for typical anal canal squamous cell carcinoma; reserved for a narrow subset of superficial, low-risk lesions treated by local excision, and for salvage APR after persistent or recurrent disease following chemoradiotherapy, or for resection of selected metastatic disease.
- Radiotherapy: central to curative treatment, almost always given concurrently with chemotherapy rather than alone, delivered by IMRT to reduce toxicity; radiotherapy alone may be an option for selected frail or elderly patients with low-risk early disease.
- Systemic therapy: concurrent chemoradiotherapy (fluoropyrimidine-based plus mitomycin, or a cisplatin-based alternative) for curative treatment; platinum-taxane or platinum-fluoropyrimidine combinations for metastatic disease; checkpoint inhibitor monotherapy for refractory or metastatic disease after first-line systemic therapy.

## HIV-positive patients

Standard chemoradiotherapy should generally not be withheld in patients with HIV, since chemotherapy omission is not supported by outcome data, but patients with a low CD4 count are at increased risk of toxicity and warrant closer monitoring during treatment. Antiretroviral therapy and maintaining adequate CD4 counts appear important for tolerating combined-modality treatment.

## Monitoring and surveillance

Post-treatment surveillance after chemoradiotherapy relies on clinical examination rather than routine early biopsy, since tumor regression can take up to a year and premature biopsy of regressing tissue risks nonhealing ulceration. The first assessment is typically performed roughly 8 to 12 weeks after completing chemoradiotherapy, with digital rectal examination, anoscopy, and palpation of the inguinal nodes. For patients in complete remission, ongoing surveillance (clinical examination, with anoscopy and periodic cross-sectional imaging for those who had more advanced disease) continues on a regular schedule for several years, since most relapses occur within the first three years; only lesions that persist, enlarge, or fail to regress over time should prompt biopsy.

## When to refer

- Any persistent or atypical anal lesion, or bleeding misattributed to hemorrhoids, warrants biopsy and referral rather than empirical treatment for benign anorectal disease.
- All patients with a new diagnosis of anal squamous cell carcinoma should be managed within a multidisciplinary oncology team (radiation oncology, medical oncology, and colorectal/surgical oncology) given the central role of combined-modality therapy.
- Patients with disease that fails to regress, progresses, or clearly recurs after chemoradiotherapy should be referred for surgical evaluation for salvage APR.
- Patients with metastatic disease, particularly limited hepatic metastases, or those being considered for checkpoint inhibitor therapy, warrant referral to a center with experience in this relatively rare cancer given the limited evidence base and evolving treatment landscape.
- High-risk individuals such as people living with HIV may benefit from referral for anal dysplasia screening (anal cytology and, when indicated, high-resolution anoscopy), recognizing that screening guidelines and thresholds for intervention remain areas of ongoing study.
