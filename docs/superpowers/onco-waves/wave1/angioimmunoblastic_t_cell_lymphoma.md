# Angioimmunoblastic T-cell lymphoma (AITL) — management

## Overview and initial approach

AITL is an aggressive peripheral T-cell lymphoma arising from T-follicular helper cells, and it accounts for a small minority of all non-Hodgkin lymphomas but a substantial share of T-cell lymphomas. Most patients present with advanced-stage disease (generalised lymphadenopathy, hepatosplenomegaly, B symptoms, skin rash, and autoimmune-type phenomena such as haemolytic anaemia or polyarthritis), so staging-adapted "limited versus advanced" treatment pathways used in many B-cell lymphomas do not really apply here; nearly all patients are treated as having systemic disease from the outset.

Before starting systemic therapy, confirm the diagnosis on an excisional lymph node biopsy with a T-follicular helper immunohistochemistry panel, complete staging (cross-sectional imaging and bone marrow examination), and screen for the autoimmune and infective complications that commonly accompany this disease, since these change how aggressively supportive care needs to be layered onto chemotherapy.

## Treatment by intent

### Initial (first-line) therapy

- A meaningful minority of patients have spontaneous remissions, or remissions with corticosteroids alone, before any chemotherapy is given. Recognising this can avoid over-treating a patient who is stabilising on steroids while diagnostic and staging work-up completes.
- For patients who need systemic treatment, AITL is approached the same way as peripheral T-cell lymphoma, not otherwise specified: anthracycline-based combination chemotherapy is the backbone of first-line induction.
- Outcomes with combination chemotherapy alone are modest, with reported long-term overall survival and failure-free survival each well under half of treated patients, which is why consolidation is considered in anyone who responds and is fit enough.

### Consolidation

- Autologous stem cell transplantation in first remission is an option for younger, fit patients who respond to induction, based on phase II experience; it is not established as mandatory for every responder, and the decision should weigh age, comorbidity, and depth/durability of response.

### Relapsed or refractory disease

- Treatment options largely mirror those used in peripheral T-cell lymphoma, not otherwise specified.
- Salvage chemotherapy followed by autologous transplant has only limited benefit in this population, so it is reserved for selected chemosensitive relapses.
- Non-myeloablative allogeneic transplant has a role in selected relapsed/refractory patients and can produce durable disease control, at the cost of meaningful non-relapse mortality, so it is a decision for centres experienced in T-cell lymphoma transplant and should follow multidisciplinary discussion.
- Targeted and epigenetic-directed approaches, given the disease's recurrent epigenetic mutations, are an active area of relapsed-disease treatment; when used, this includes histone deacetylase inhibitors and hypomethylating agents, plus antifolate therapy where appropriate; responses to antifolate therapy specifically appear to be less reliable in AITL than in peripheral T-cell lymphoma more broadly. Where the tumour expresses CD30, an anti-CD30 antibody-drug conjugate is an additional option.
- Response rates to available relapsed-disease agents are generally modest and responses are often not durable, so enrolment in a clinical trial should be considered whenever one is accessible, and this should be discussed early rather than only after multiple lines have failed.

## Supportive and disease-specific care

- Corticosteroids and other immunomodulatory measures are used to control the autoimmune haemolytic anaemia, polyarthritis, and other immune-mediated phenomena that frequently accompany AITL, independent of anti-lymphoma treatment.
- Because AITL causes significant immune dysregulation and because its treatment is immunosuppressive, infection prophylaxis and vigilance for opportunistic infection are an integral part of management, not an afterthought; opportunistic infection is a recognised cause of death in this disease.
- Epstein-Barr virus-driven B-cell blasts are common in the background of AITL and can transform into a secondary EBV-positive diffuse large B-cell lymphoma; a rapidly enlarging mass or unexplained clinical deterioration should prompt re-biopsy to look for this transformation rather than being assumed to be AITL progression.

## Monitoring

- Response assessment after treatment should follow standard lymphoma restaging practice (imaging performed some weeks after completion of chemotherapy, with a longer interval if radiotherapy was part of treatment), with achievement of a complete response being the key determinant of longer-term outcome.
- Ongoing surveillance should specifically include watching for disease relapse, secondary EBV-positive large B-cell lymphoma, and opportunistic infection, since all three are recognised drivers of mortality in this disease.
- Monitor for and manage the autoimmune phenomena (haemolysis, arthropathy, thyroid dysfunction) longitudinally, as these can fluctuate independently of lymphoma activity.

## When to refer

- All patients with suspected or confirmed AITL should be managed by a haemato-oncology multidisciplinary team from diagnosis, given the diagnostic complexity, the frequency of autoimmune and infective complications, and the need to weigh transplant options early.
- Refer promptly for transplant assessment (autologous in first remission for fit responders; allogeneic for selected relapsed/refractory patients) rather than waiting until multiple relapses have occurred, since eligibility narrows with cumulative treatment and declining fitness.
- Refer to a centre with access to clinical trials whenever relapsed or refractory disease is encountered, since standard salvage options offer limited and often short-lived benefit.
- Involve palliative and supportive care early given the generally guarded prognosis and the burden of autoimmune and infective complications, rather than reserving this for end-of-life care alone.
