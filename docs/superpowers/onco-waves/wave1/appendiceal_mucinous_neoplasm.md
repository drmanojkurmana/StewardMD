# Appendiceal Mucinous Neoplasm (including LAMN, HAMN, mucinous adenocarcinoma with pseudomyxoma peritonei) — Management

## Treatment approach by extent of disease

Appendiceal mucinous neoplasms behave very differently depending on whether disease is confined to the appendix or has already breached the wall and seeded the peritoneum, so management splits along that line rather than along a conventional TNM staging ladder.

- **Disease confined to the appendix (no mural breach, negative margins):** complete surgical removal of the appendix is usually curative. Because puncture or piecemeal handling can seed the peritoneum, an intact, non-ruptured, en bloc appendicectomy is the operative goal, and a suspected mucocele should not be biopsied through the wall.
- **Higher-risk features on the appendicectomy specimen** (perforation, mucin or epithelial cells outside the appendix, involved margin, higher histologic grade) raise the risk of subsequent peritoneal spread and usually prompt further imaging and specialist surgical oncology or peritoneal-malignancy referral to decide whether completion surgery (for example right hemicolectomy or further peritoneal exploration) is warranted; a full discussion of that decision is a multidisciplinary-team question rather than a fixed rule, and DeVita does not lay out a single uniform completion-surgery algorithm.
- **Established peritoneal metastases (pseudomyxoma peritonei):** the standard of care in specialist centers is complete cytoreductive surgery (CRS), aiming to remove all visible peritoneal disease, combined with perioperative intraperitoneal chemotherapy (hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy, HIPEC, given in the operating room, or early postoperative intraperitoneal chemotherapy, EPIC, given in the early postoperative period). This combined strategy is the approach that converted pseudomyxoma peritonei from a uniformly fatal disease to one compatible with long-term survival, historically reported around 85 percent survival at 20 years in a landmark series.
- Unlike colorectal or gastric peritoneal metastases, where only truly complete (no visible residual disease) cytoreduction is considered adequate, low-grade appendiceal mucinous disease (LAMN and other low-grade mucinous tumors) is treated as adequately cytoreduced even when minute mucoid residual deposits remain, because these have not been shown to affect outcome; this is a distinguishing feature of how "complete cytoreduction" is defined for this tumor compared with other peritoneal-surface malignancies.

## Patient selection for cytoreductive surgery and HIPEC

Selection for CRS plus perioperative chemotherapy relies on a set of quantitative prognostic indicators rather than tumor stage alone:

- Histologic grade/subtype: outcomes differ substantially between LAMN, intermediate-grade tumors, and mucinous appendiceal carcinoma (MACA), with the more differentiated, low-grade tumors carrying the best prognosis after CRS.
- Peritoneal Cancer Index (PCI), a score combining lesion size and distribution across abdominal and pelvic regions, assessed at exploration; for mucinous appendiceal carcinoma a PCI ceiling is used above which CRS plus perioperative chemotherapy is unlikely to be of benefit, whereas no such PCI ceiling has been established for LAMN.
- Completeness-of-cytoreduction (CC) score at the end of surgery, with the threshold for "complete" cytoreduction being more lenient for LAMN and other low-grade mucinous tumors than for higher-grade appendiceal or other GI peritoneal malignancies (see above).
- Recognized signet-ring-cell (poorly cohesive cell) morphology is generally associated with a worse prognosis in GI peritoneal metastases, though appendiceal peritoneal metastases are noted as a possible exception to that general rule.
- Because these are complex, high-morbidity operations with a documented institutional learning curve, this treatment is concentrated in specialist peritoneal-malignancy/surgical oncology centers rather than performed broadly.

## Lines of therapy / role of systemic therapy

- Systemic chemotherapy has an established role for unresectable or diffusely disseminated disease, for disease with unfavorable prognostic features that is not a candidate for CRS/HIPEC, or as part of a neoadjuvant strategy before an attempt at CRS.
- Response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy is itself a prognostic indicator: patients with a histologically confirmed complete or near-complete response who proceed to CRS and perioperative chemotherapy within a short interval have shown markedly better survival than those with a partial or absent response; for appendiceal (and colorectal) tumors, though, a clinical/imaging response to chemotherapy can be assessed but a histologic response often cannot be directly confirmed prior to surgery.
- DeVita does not specify a distinct, appendiceal-specific systemic drug regimen name beyond describing chemotherapy used regionally (intraperitoneally) versus systemically; specific systemic drug regimen selection for mucinous appendiceal adenocarcinoma is not detailed in the source material reviewed here, so no specific regimen is asserted, and this should be treated as an area needing further guideline-specific sourcing (for example NCCN) at the point of care.
- A notable feature of appendiceal peritoneal disease, distinct from colorectal peritoneal metastases, is that meaningful salvage is possible even after recurrence following initial CRS and perioperative chemotherapy, meaning repeat cytoreductive surgery can be a legitimate option at recurrence rather than automatically signaling a shift to palliative-only systemic therapy.

## Role of surgery and radiotherapy

- Surgery is central to curative intent at every stage: initial appendicectomy for localized disease, and cytoreductive surgery (with peritonectomy of involved parietal and visceral peritoneal surfaces) for peritoneal dissemination.
- Radiotherapy is not described as a standard component of management for appendiceal mucinous neoplasm or pseudomyxoma peritonei in the source reviewed; no RT recommendation is made here for lack of grounding.
- For patients who are not candidates for curative CRS (for example bowel obstruction from bulky mucinous disease), palliative surgical procedures such as bowel bypass or debulking to relieve obstruction and preserve enteral nutrition can be appropriate, mirroring the general palliative surgical approach used for other peritoneal-surface malignancies causing obstruction.

## Monitoring

- After curative appendicectomy for confined, favorable-risk disease, follow-up is directed at detecting late peritoneal recurrence, which is the principal long-term risk for this tumor type; imaging surveillance intervals and duration are not specified in the DeVita material reviewed, so no specific surveillance schedule is asserted here.
- After CRS and perioperative chemotherapy, prognosis and expected surveillance intensity should be individualized based on the histologic subtype, PCI, and completeness-of-cytoreduction score achieved at surgery, since these are the dominant determinants of outcome and recurrence risk in this disease.
- Because recurrence after appendiceal CRS can still be salvaged surgically, ongoing surveillance for peritoneal recurrence (rather than treating any recurrence as automatically terminal) is consistent with the disease's documented natural history.

## When to refer

- Any incidental finding of a mucocele, a mucin-filled or cystic tubular appendiceal mass, or an appendicectomy specimen showing a mucinous neoplasm, perforation, or extra-appendiceal mucin/epithelial cells should prompt referral to (or discussion with) a surgical oncology or peritoneal-surface-malignancy multidisciplinary team, given the risk of occult peritoneal seeding.
- Any patient with confirmed or suspected pseudomyxoma peritonei should be referred to a specialist center experienced in cytoreductive surgery and HIPEC, given the documented institutional learning curve and the marked survival benefit of expert, complete cytoreduction over incomplete or non-specialist debulking.
- Recurrent disease after prior CRS/HIPEC should also be referred back to a specialist peritoneal-malignancy center for consideration of repeat cytoreductive surgery, rather than defaulting to palliative systemic therapy alone.
