# Aneurysmal Bone Cyst (ABC) — Management Narrative

## Overview

Aneurysmal bone cyst is a benign but locally destructive, expansile lesion of bone. Primary lesions
carry a USP6 gene rearrangement and behave as a true neoplasm rather than a purely reactive cyst.
A secondary, histologically identical process can arise within another primary bone tumour (giant
cell tumour, chondroblastoma, osteoblastoma, telangiectatic osteosarcoma) and this distinction
matters because management follows the underlying lesion in secondary cases.

## Treatment approach by presentation

- Confirm the diagnosis first. Because a malignant mimic, most importantly telangiectatic
  osteosarcoma, can look similar on imaging, histological confirmation by biopsy is expected before
  definitive treatment, unless the lesion is being treated and monitored by an experienced bone
  tumour service with characteristic imaging.
- Once a primary ABC is confirmed and a secondary process has been excluded, treatment intent is
  local control rather than systemic therapy. There is no established role for chemotherapy or
  radiotherapy as first-line treatment of an uncomplicated primary lesion.
- Asymptomatic, incidentally found small lesions in a non-critical location may occasionally be
  observed, but most symptomatic lesions with structural risk (cortical thinning, growth plate
  involvement, spinal instability, pathological fracture risk) are treated actively.

## Lines of therapy

- First line: intralesional curettage of the cystic cavity, generally combined with bone grafting
  or a bone-void adjunct (for example a synthetic graft substitute or cement) to fill the defect and
  restore mechanical stability. This remains the standard surgical approach for accessible lesions.
- Percutaneous sclerotherapy (injection of a sclerosing agent into the lesion) is used as either a
  primary alternative to surgery or an adjunct, particularly for lesions in surgically difficult
  locations such as the spine or pelvis, or in young children where extensive surgery carries higher
  morbidity.
- Selective arterial embolisation can be used pre-operatively to reduce intra-operative bleeding in
  highly vascular lesions, or as a standalone or repeated treatment strategy in some spinal and
  pelvic lesions where surgical access is difficult.
- En bloc resection is reserved for lesions where curettage is not feasible or where recurrence has
  been refractory to repeated intralesional treatment, recognising the greater functional cost of
  resection.
- Denosumab has been used off-label in refractory or surgically inaccessible ABC given the role of
  osteoclast-type giant cells in the lesion, but this is not established first-line therapy and
  should be considered only in a specialist bone tumour board setting with attention to the known
  class effects of RANKL inhibition; the specific decision to use it, and any dosing, sits outside
  the scope of this narrative and belongs in a structured protocol template.

## Role of surgery, radiotherapy and systemic therapy

- Surgery (curettage with grafting, or resection for refractory disease) is the backbone of
  treatment for accessible lesions.
- Radiotherapy has no established routine role given the benign nature of the lesion and the
  long-term risks of radiation in what is typically a paediatric or young adult population; it is
  not a standard option.
- Systemic therapy is not part of the management of an uncomplicated primary ABC. Any use of a
  targeted or anti-resorptive agent is limited to refractory, surgically inaccessible disease and
  should be managed by a specialist bone tumour service.

## Monitoring

- Post-treatment surveillance with serial plain radiographs, and MRI when needed, to assess healing
  of the curettage cavity or resolution after sclerotherapy or embolisation.
- Local recurrence is not uncommon after curettage, particularly in young children and with
  incomplete removal, so follow-up imaging is continued for a period after treatment to detect
  recurrence early.
- Growth plate involvement in skeletally immature patients warrants longer clinical and radiographic
  follow-up to monitor for growth disturbance.

## When to refer

- Any suspected ABC, especially one with atypical imaging features, spinal or pelvic location, or
  in a location where a malignant mimic (telangiectatic osteosarcoma) must be excluded, should be
  referred to an orthopaedic oncology or bone tumour service before biopsy or treatment.
- Lesions that recur after adequate curettage, or that are in a surgically difficult location, merit
  referral to a specialist centre with access to sclerotherapy, embolisation and multidisciplinary
  bone tumour board input.
- Spinal lesions with instability or neurological compromise warrant urgent referral for combined
  spinal surgical and interventional radiology input.
