# Angiosarcoma — Management

## Overview

Angiosarcoma is a malignant vascular neoplasm arising from cells that resemble endothelium; the older
term lymphangiosarcoma is now folded into the same category because the histology and outcomes
overlap. Most tumours develop in the skin or superficial soft tissue, with a minority arising in deep
soft tissue or viscera. Recognised secondary settings include chronic lymphoedema (Stewart-Treves
syndrome) and prior irradiation, most classically after breast radiotherapy. Lymphoedema-associated
disease is particularly hard to treat surgically because impaired wound healing can threaten limb
preservation, and this form carries high rates of both local and distant recurrence. Multicentric
scalp and facial angiosarcoma in elderly patients tends to progress relentlessly, causing ulceration,
infection, and eventual metastasis. Prognostic factors include patient age, tumour depth, and size;
overall survival is poor, with disease-specific death occurring within as little as a few years in
some patient groups.

## Localised disease

- Surgical resection is the mainstay for localised disease, but because angiosarcoma spreads in an
  infiltrative, often multifocal pattern, clear margins are difficult to achieve and resection alone is
  rarely curative.
- Radiotherapy is used to improve local control, particularly where margins are likely to be
  compromised by the tumour's infiltrative growth.
- Staging should include imaging of the primary site and chest imaging to exclude pulmonary
  metastases, since the lungs are the most common site of haematogenous spread.
- Even apparently localised disease has a high rate of relapse, so patients need close longitudinal
  follow-up for both local recurrence and distant metastasis after local treatment.

## Advanced, unresectable, or metastatic disease

- Angiosarcoma is relatively chemosensitive compared with many other soft tissue sarcomas: it responds,
  at least initially, to anthracycline-based therapy and to taxanes, which have limited activity in
  most other adult sarcoma subtypes.
- Beyond first-line therapy, reported options with activity in angiosarcoma include taxanes,
  gemcitabine, vinorelbine, pegylated liposomal doxorubicin, multitargeted tyrosine kinase inhibitors,
  and ifosfamide-based therapy; the choice among these is individualised based on prior treatment,
  fitness, and toxicity considerations.
- Antiangiogenic therapy has a specific rationale in this disease given its vascular biology: an
  antibody against vascular endothelial growth factor produced partial responses and disease
  stabilisation in a meaningful proportion of patients with angiosarcoma and epithelioid
  haemangioendothelioma in a phase II study, and a multitargeted tyrosine kinase inhibitor with
  antiangiogenic activity is approved for advanced soft tissue sarcoma more broadly after failure of
  standard systemic therapy. A first-generation VEGF-pathway inhibitor and a B-Raf/VEGFR-2 inhibitor
  showed only minor activity, and general first- and second-generation kinase inhibitors have been
  disappointing outside this antiangiogenic-selected use.
- Checkpoint inhibition is an emerging option: combination checkpoint blockade has shown responses
  across a range of sarcoma subtypes including angiosarcoma, and head and neck cutaneous
  angiosarcoma in particular appears to carry a higher mutation burden than other angiosarcomas,
  which may make it more likely to respond to checkpoint inhibitor combinations in trials; this
  remains an area of active investigation rather than an established standard.
- Molecular findings inform prognosis and future therapy selection: mutations affecting the VEGF
  signalling pathway occur in a subset of tumours, MYC and FLT4 amplification are enriched in
  radiation- and lymphoedema-associated disease and FLT4 co-amplification is linked to worse outcome,
  and rearrangements affecting the CIC gene, seen mostly in younger patients with primary disease, are
  associated with inferior disease-free survival. These markers are prognostic and may guide referral
  for trial enrolment, but a change in standard systemic therapy based on them is not established from
  the sources reviewed here.

## Monitoring

- Regular clinical follow-up plus imaging surveillance of the primary site and chest, since local
  recurrence and pulmonary metastasis are the dominant patterns of relapse even after apparently
  successful local treatment.
- Resection of isolated, fully resectable pulmonary metastases can be considered when the lungs are
  the only site of residual disease, as this is associated with long-term survival in selected sarcoma
  patients.

## When to refer

- Any suspected angiosarcoma should go to a specialist sarcoma multidisciplinary team given the
  difficulty of achieving clear margins, the need for combined surgery and radiotherapy planning, and
  the aggressive natural history.
- Refer for consideration of systemic therapy, antiangiogenic therapy, or clinical trial enrolment
  (including checkpoint inhibitor trials, which show particular signal in cutaneous head and neck
  disease) once disease is advanced, unresectable, or metastatic.
- Given the generally poor prognosis and high relapse rates, involve palliative care early alongside
  oncology management.
