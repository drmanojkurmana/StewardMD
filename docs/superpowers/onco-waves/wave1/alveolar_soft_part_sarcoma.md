# Alveolar Soft Part Sarcoma (ASPS) — Management Narrative

## Overview relevant to management
ASPS is a rare soft tissue sarcoma driven by an ASPSCR1-TFE3 fusion. It is typically slow growing and often painless, so patients may present with a large primary or with metastatic disease already established at diagnosis. It has a strong tendency toward early haematogenous spread, particularly to lung, bone, and brain, and this behaviour, more than local aggressiveness, dictates the overall management strategy.

A key management-defining fact is that ASPS is essentially resistant to standard cytotoxic chemotherapy. This resistance, shared with GIST and low- to intermediate-grade chondrosarcoma among sarcoma subtypes, means systemic treatment for advanced disease is built around targeted and immune-based approaches rather than conventional chemotherapy regimens.

## Localised disease
- Complete surgical resection with negative margins is the primary treatment for localised, resectable ASPS.
- Local recurrence after surgery is uncommon.
- Radiotherapy may be used as an adjunct for margin control or for tumours where clean surgical margins are difficult to achieve, following general soft tissue sarcoma principles; DeVita does not give ASPS-specific radiotherapy indications, so this should be treated as an extrapolation from general sarcoma practice rather than an ASPS-specific recommendation.
- Because ASPS can remain asymptomatic for years even with occult metastatic disease, staging to exclude metastasis (particularly pulmonary) is important before committing to a purely local treatment plan.

## Advanced, unresectable, or metastatic disease
- Cytotoxic chemotherapy is not effective in ASPS and should not be relied upon as a systemic treatment line.
- Antiangiogenic tyrosine kinase inhibitors are the current standard-of-care systemic option for advanced ASPS, reflecting the tumour biology in which the ASPSCR1-TFE3 fusion upregulates HIF1-alpha and downstream pro-angiogenic signalling such as VEGF. Reported response rates with these agents approach the range described for this class in ASPS.
- MET inhibition has also been explored, given ASPS tumours express MET kinase, with some reported disease control.
- Immune checkpoint blockade has shown durable responses in ASPS, one of the few sarcoma subtypes (along with undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma and cutaneous angiosarcoma) where checkpoint inhibition has demonstrated clear clinical benefit.
- Combining a tyrosine kinase inhibitor (axitinib) with checkpoint blockade (pembrolizumab) has been reported to further improve response rate compared with either approach alone, representing a more active combination strategy than monotherapy with either agent.
- Given the rarity of ASPS and the ongoing development of targeted and immune combination strategies, enrollment in a clinical trial should be considered at any point in the advanced-disease pathway when a suitable trial is available.

## Lines of therapy
- First line for advanced/metastatic disease: an antiangiogenic tyrosine kinase inhibitor, or a tyrosine kinase inhibitor plus checkpoint blockade combination where available, rather than cytotoxic chemotherapy.
- Subsequent lines: alternative antiangiogenic agents, MET-directed therapy, or checkpoint blockade (as monotherapy or in combination), guided by prior exposure and trial availability.
- DeVita does not specify a fixed sequencing algorithm across these classes for ASPS; treatment choice should be individualised and, where possible, discussed at a sarcoma multidisciplinary tumour board.

## Monitoring
- Because ASPS grows slowly but metastasises early, patients require periodic imaging surveillance of the primary site (if left in situ or post-resection) and of the lungs, given the propensity for pulmonary spread; bone and brain should also be considered as sites of possible spread when there is clinical suspicion.
- Long-term follow-up is warranted given the reported pattern of prolonged survival with metastatic disease in some series, meaning surveillance intervals may need to extend over many years rather than the shorter horizons used for more rapidly progressive sarcomas.
- Response assessment on systemic therapy should track with standard sarcoma response criteria used for the agent class being administered.

## When to refer
- Refer to a sarcoma specialist centre / multidisciplinary sarcoma tumour board at diagnosis, given the rarity of ASPS and the specialised nature of both surgical planning and systemic therapy selection.
- Refer for molecular confirmation (TFE3 immunohistochemistry and/or FISH or RT-PCR for the ASPSCR1-TFE3 fusion) if not already established, since this is the defining diagnostic feature and distinguishes ASPS from other soft tissue tumours with overlapping histology.
- Refer for clinical trial evaluation whenever advanced disease requires systemic therapy, given the relative paucity of established options and the pace of new targeted/immunotherapy data in this subtype.

Sources: DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of Oncology, 12th ed.
