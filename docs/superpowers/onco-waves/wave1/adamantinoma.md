# Adamantinoma - Management

## Overview
Adamantinoma is a rare, low-grade malignant bone tumour with a strong predilection for the tibial (and sometimes fibular) diaphysis, seen mainly in adolescents and young adults. DeVita's chapter on sarcomas of bone confirms this characteristic site (tibia and/or fibula) and lists adamantinoma of long bones, together with its dedifferentiated variant, as a distinct "other mesenchymal" entity in the current WHO bone sarcoma classification. The chapter does not otherwise devote disease-specific treatment discussion to adamantinoma, so the management approach below is built from that anatomic/classification grounding plus standard, uncontroversial orthopaedic oncology practice for this tumour type. Any claim that could not be grounded this way has been left out rather than assumed.

## Treatment approach by disease extent

**Localized disease (the great majority of presentations)**
- Definitive treatment is wide en bloc surgical resection with clear margins. Adamantinoma behaves as a surgical disease because it does not reliably respond to radiotherapy or chemotherapy.
- Limb-sparing resection with biological or endoprosthetic reconstruction of the diaphyseal defect is preferred when oncologically safe margins can still be achieved; amputation is reserved for cases where limb salvage cannot secure adequate margins.
- Intralesional curettage should be avoided. It is inadequate for this tumour and carries a high risk of local recurrence, largely because the epithelial component and its interface with the osteofibrous stroma are easy to under-resect.
- There is no established role for routine (neo)adjuvant radiotherapy or systemic chemotherapy in localized adamantinoma; these modalities are considered generally ineffective against this tumour and are not part of standard management.

**Locally advanced or recurrent disease**
- Recurrence after inadequate initial surgery (most often after curettage or a marginal resection) is managed with re-resection to achieve wide margins, again as the mainstay, since the tumour's resistance to radiotherapy and chemotherapy does not change with recurrence.
- Involvement of a large soft tissue or neurovascular extent may push the operative plan toward amputation if limb-sparing margins are not achievable.

**Metastatic disease**
- Metastases are chiefly pulmonary and to regional lymph nodes and tend to occur late, sometimes years after initial treatment.
- Resectable pulmonary metastases are managed with metastasectomy where feasible.
- Because systemic chemotherapy is not established as effective for adamantinoma, its use in the metastatic setting is not standard; decisions in this situation should be individualized within a specialist multidisciplinary sarcoma team rather than following a fixed systemic-therapy pathway.

## Role of surgery, radiotherapy, and systemic therapy
- Surgery is the definitive and essentially the only reliably effective modality across stages, driven by the goal of a wide margin at first operation.
- Radiotherapy has no established disease-controlling role given the tumour's resistance; it is not used as a substitute for adequate surgery.
- Systemic chemotherapy likewise has no established role; adamantinoma should not be treated on osteosarcoma- or Ewing-sarcoma-style systemic regimens, which apply to biologically distinct high-grade bone sarcomas.

## Diagnostic and staging workup that informs management
- Adequate open biopsy with cytokeratin and epithelial-marker immunohistochemistry is needed before committing to definitive surgery, since adamantinoma sits on a histologic spectrum with osteofibrous dysplasia and osteofibrous-dysplasia-like adamantinoma, and under-sampling can lead to under-treatment of a more overtly malignant lesion.
- MRI defines intramedullary and soft-tissue extent for surgical planning; CT characterizes cortical destruction and lesion architecture.
- Baseline staging with chest CT is used to exclude pulmonary metastases before definitive surgery, and regional imaging is used to assess nodal status when clinically indicated.

## Monitoring and follow-up
- Long-term surveillance is required because local recurrence and metastasis can present many years after apparently successful treatment.
- Follow-up should combine clinical review of the operated site with periodic local imaging (to detect recurrence) and periodic chest imaging (to detect late pulmonary metastasis), continued well beyond the interval typically used for higher-grade bone sarcomas.
- Any new local swelling, pain, or pulmonary/nodal findings on surveillance imaging should prompt re-biopsy or re-staging rather than being assumed benign, given the tumour's capacity for late spread.

## When to refer
- All suspected or confirmed adamantinoma should be referred to a specialist orthopaedic oncology centre with musculoskeletal pathology support before biopsy if possible, since biopsy planning affects the feasibility of a later limb-sparing resection.
- Refer for multidisciplinary sarcoma team review at diagnosis, at any local recurrence, and when pulmonary or nodal metastases are identified, so that resection planning, reconstruction options, and the (limited) role of adjunct therapies are considered jointly rather than in a single-specialty pathway.

## What was deliberately left out
DeVita's bone sarcoma chapter does not provide adamantinoma-specific data on recurrence rates, survival, or any systemic regimen, and its detailed chemotherapy discussion in that chapter applies specifically to osteosarcoma and Ewing sarcoma, not to adamantinoma. No numeric outcome statistics, regimens, or trial data have been carried over from those sections, since doing so would misattribute high-grade sarcoma evidence to this distinct, chemo-resistant low-grade tumour.

Sources: DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of Oncology, 12th ed.
