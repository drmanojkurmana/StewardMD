# Mantle cell lymphoma (CCND1/t(11;14)) - management

## Overview and initial approach

Most patients with mantle cell lymphoma (MCL) present with disseminated disease and require treatment at diagnosis. A minority, roughly one in ten, have a more indolent presentation with minimal lymphadenopathy, mild splenomegaly and a low proliferation index (Ki-67), and in this subset a period of watchful waiting does not appear to worsen the response to treatment or survival when therapy is eventually started.

A small number of patients present with truly localised (stage I to II) disease. These patients are potentially curable with combined chemotherapy plus involved-site radiotherapy, in contrast to the advanced-stage majority for whom treatment is aimed at prolonging remission rather than cure.

## Frontline systemic therapy (fit patients)

- Chemoimmunotherapy built around an anti-CD20 antibody (rituximab) is the backbone of initial treatment and has been shown to improve survival compared with chemotherapy alone.
- An anthracycline-based combination (rituximab plus a CHOP-type regimen) has been a mainstay of induction, but in older/less fit patients a bendamustine-rituximab combination has shown superior progression-free survival and less toxicity than the CHOP-type combination in a randomised comparison.
- Adding a proteasome inhibitor (bortezomib) to a CHOP-type backbone improved progression-free survival and response rates over the CHOP-type combination alone, but the extra toxicity has limited its routine adoption.
- Lenalidomide combined with rituximab has shown promising response rates in the untreated setting, though this is an evolving approach rather than an established standard.
- In younger, fit patients, more intensive approaches are used with curative intent on remission duration: a hyperfractionated cyclophosphamide/anthracycline-based regimen with rituximab, or cytarabine-containing induction (e.g. an anthracycline-rituximab backbone alternated with or followed by high-dose cytarabine) followed by consolidation with autologous stem-cell transplant (ASCT). Adding cytarabine before transplant improves remission rates, though it comes with more toxicity and has not clearly improved overall survival in randomised comparison.
- These intensive regimens carry a meaningful risk that patients cannot complete the planned course because of treatment-related toxicity, which should factor into fitness assessment before intensive induction is chosen.
- Maintenance rituximab after induction, including after consolidative ASCT, improves both progression-free and overall survival compared with observation, and is recommended for patients who respond to frontline chemoimmunotherapy.

## Relapsed or refractory disease

Most patients with MCL eventually relapse after frontline therapy, and DeVita notes the prognosis at this stage is generally poor, so referral for consideration of clinical trials should be part of the relapse discussion.

- Bruton tyrosine kinase (BTK) inhibitors are a mainstay of relapsed treatment. Ibrutinib produces meaningful response rates, but progression on ibrutinib is associated with poor subsequent survival. Second-generation, more selective BTK inhibitors (acalabrutinib, zanubrutinib) achieve higher response and complete response rates with a more favourable toxicity profile than ibrutinib.
- CD19-directed CAR T-cell therapy (brexucabtagene autoleucel) is approved for relapsed/refractory MCL after chemoimmunotherapy and a BTK inhibitor, with high overall and complete response rates seen even in high-risk disease (high Ki-67, early relapse, blastoid/pleomorphic histology, TP53 mutation). Cytokine release syndrome and neurologic toxicity occur at rates comparable to CAR-T use in large B-cell lymphoma and need a centre experienced in managing them.
- Bortezomib and lenalidomide as single agents have more modest activity in the relapsed setting.
- The BCL2 antagonist venetoclax has shown activity in relapsed disease.
- Conventional cytotoxic agents, including purine analogues and bendamustine, retain activity at relapse.
- MCL is notably radiosensitive: radiotherapy, including at modest or even low doses, can effectively palliate localised or chemo-refractory disease and should be considered for symptomatic or bulky localised relapse.
- ASCT performed specifically for relapsed disease (as opposed to first-remission consolidation) has shown limited benefit. Non-myeloablative allogeneic stem-cell transplant is an option in selected relapsed patients, though outcomes remain modest.

## Prognostic stratification and monitoring

- Overall survival in MCL is measured in years and has been improving, but outcomes are heterogeneous. The blastic/blastoid histologic variant carries a much shorter survival, and blastic transformation can occur over time in a meaningful proportion of patients, after which survival is markedly shortened.
- The MCL International Prognostic Index (MIPI), incorporating age, performance status, LDH and white cell count, stratifies patients better than the generic lymphoma IPI; adding the proliferation index (Ki-67) to the MIPI further refines risk, including in patients being considered for ASCT.
- TP53 mutation or deletion is an adverse prognostic marker associated with poor response to conventional chemoimmunotherapy and should prompt consideration of a non-chemotherapy-based or trial-based approach (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Central nervous system involvement is rare and is usually associated with a leukaemic phase of the disease, which is a trigger for closer neurologic monitoring in that subgroup.
- A minority of patients follow a more indolent leukaemic, non-nodal course with a low proliferation index (sometimes described as SOX11-negative disease), for whom active surveillance rather than immediate treatment is appropriate (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Role of surgery

MCL is a systemic haematologic malignancy; surgery has no role beyond diagnostic tissue biopsy and management of a specific mechanical complication if one arises, for example bowel obstruction from gastrointestinal lymphomatous polyposis (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## When to refer

All patients with a new diagnosis of MCL warrant referral to haemato-oncology for staging, MIPI/TP53-based risk stratification and a decision on transplant eligibility before treatment starts. Patients who relapse, particularly those with high-risk features (blastoid histology, high Ki-67, TP53 abnormality, early relapse), should be referred promptly given the generally poor prognosis at relapse and the role of specialised options such as BTK inhibitors and CD19 CAR-T therapy, which require centres experienced in delivering and monitoring them.
