# Infantile Myofibromatosis — Management

## Sourcing note

DeVita's *Cancer: Principles & Practice of Oncology* mentions myofibromatosis only once, as a single
line item ("Myopericytoma, including myofibroma and myofibromatosis") inside the WHO histologic
classification table of benign perivascular soft-tissue tumors. There is no dedicated management
section on this entity in the DeVita text searched. This content is therefore guideline-general
(general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease) and is FLAGGED as needing
manual sourcing against a dedicated pediatric soft-tissue tumor reference (for example WHO
Classification of Tumours: Soft Tissue and Bone, or Enzinger and Weiss's Soft Tissue Tumors) before
clinician sign-off.

## Treatment approach by extent of disease

Infantile myofibromatosis behaves very differently depending on whether disease is solitary,
multicentric without organ involvement, or generalized with visceral involvement, so intent of
treatment tracks disease extent rather than a single staging system (general oncology standard, not
from DeVita's section on this disease).

- **Solitary lesion**: observation is the default approach given a high rate of spontaneous
  regression; active treatment is reserved for lesions that are symptomatic, cosmetically or
  functionally compromising, or where the diagnosis remains uncertain after imaging and biopsy
  (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- **Multicentric disease without visceral involvement**: also managed with observation and serial
  imaging in most cases, since the prognosis remains favorable and many nodules regress over time
  (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- **Generalized disease with visceral involvement**: this is the subset with meaningfully higher
  morbidity and mortality, particularly in neonates, and is the group in whom systemic treatment and
  intensive supportive care are considered (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on
  this disease).

## Lines of therapy

- **First line for symptomatic or progressive disease**: low-dose combination chemotherapy (a
  vinblastine-based regimen combined with an antimetabolite such as methotrexate has been used in
  this setting) is the treatment most often reported for generalized, visceral, or otherwise
  treatment-requiring disease (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- **Targeted therapy**: because a substantial subset of cases carries activating PDGFRB mutations (or
  less commonly NOTCH3 alterations), PDGFR-targeted tyrosine kinase inhibition is a reasonable
  consideration in refractory or progressive PDGFRB-driven disease, though this is not a
  first-line standard in most published series (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section
  on this disease).
- Radiotherapy is not a standard part of management of this benign, often self-regressing entity in
  infants, and should not be used as first-line treatment (general oncology standard, not from
  DeVita's section on this disease).

## Role of surgery

Surgical resection is used selectively rather than routinely. It is appropriate for a lesion that is
symptomatic, causing functional impairment, threatening a vital structure, or when tissue is needed
to exclude a more aggressive process such as infantile fibrosarcoma or rhabdomyosarcoma. Resection of
an asymptomatic, regressing solitary lesion is generally unnecessary and represents overtreatment
(general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Role of radiotherapy and systemic therapy

Radiotherapy has no established role in this condition. Systemic therapy (low-dose chemotherapy, and
selectively a PDGFR-targeted tyrosine kinase inhibitor) is reserved for generalized visceral disease
or for progressive disease not amenable to surgery, not for solitary or multicentric non-visceral
disease (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Monitoring

- Serial clinical examination and imaging (ultrasound or MRI as appropriate to the site) to confirm
  spontaneous regression or to detect progression in lesions initially managed with observation
  (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Close monitoring for organ compromise (respiratory or gastrointestinal) in neonates with
  generalized visceral disease, since this is the group at risk of clinically significant morbidity
  and mortality (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Reassessment of the diagnosis (repeat biopsy or molecular testing) if a lesion shows rapid growth or
  atypical histologic features, given the differential with infantile fibrosarcoma (general oncology
  standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## When to refer

- Refer to pediatric oncology and soft-tissue/sarcoma services for any multicentric or generalized
  disease, any lesion with diagnostic uncertainty, and any case being considered for systemic therapy
  or surgery (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Offer genetic counselling when a germline PDGFRB or NOTCH3 variant is identified, since familial
  forms exist and may have implications for relatives (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's
  section on this disease).
- Escalate urgently for neonates with visceral or multiorgan involvement causing respiratory or
  gastrointestinal compromise, as this subgroup requires intensive supportive care alongside oncology
  input (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

Sources: DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of Oncology, 12th ed.
