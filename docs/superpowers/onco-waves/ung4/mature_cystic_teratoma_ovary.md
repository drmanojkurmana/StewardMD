# Mature cystic teratoma of the ovary (dermoid cyst) - management

## Nature of the entity and why "staging" does not apply
Mature cystic teratoma is a benign ovarian germ cell tumour. DeVita discusses it explicitly as a benign mixed cystic and solid ovarian mass (grouped with serous cystadenoma, mucinous cystadenoma, and dermoid cyst/cystic teratoma) that sits in the differential of an adnexal mass, and separately distinguishes it from the malignant germ cell tumours of the ovary (dysgerminoma, yolk sac tumour, immature teratoma, mixed germ cell tumour, embryonal carcinoma, choriocarcinoma), which are a different disease entity requiring adjuvant chemotherapy. There is no FIGO stage, no line-of-therapy ladder, and no chemotherapy or radiotherapy role for the mature (benign) lesion itself. Management is therefore surgical and expectant, not oncologic in the sense of stage-directed systemic therapy.

## Treatment approach

### Asymptomatic, small, imaging-typical lesion
- Many are incidental findings on pelvic ultrasound done for other reasons.
- A small, asymptomatic lesion with classic benign sonographic features (echogenic Rokitansky nodule, fat-fluid level, dense acoustic shadowing from hair/sebum) can be managed with surveillance imaging rather than immediate surgery (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Surgery is favoured for larger lesions, growth on serial imaging, symptoms, or any atypical/complex feature raising concern for malignant transformation or for a different adnexal pathology (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

### Symptomatic or growing lesion, or diagnostic uncertainty
- Surgical removal is the mainstay: DeVita's discussion of the analogous benign ovarian mass workup emphasises that transvaginal ultrasound (complex vs simple, septations, solid components) drives the decision for operative intervention, with CT/MRI reserved for cases with moderate-to-high suspicion of malignancy (e.g. concern for omental caking or carcinomatosis).
- The fertility-sparing approach is ovarian cystectomy with preservation of normal ovarian tissue; this is standard gynaecologic-oncology practice for a benign germ cell mass (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease, though consistent with DeVita's broader point that fertility-preserving surgery is favoured whenever the biology of a germ-cell ovarian tumour permits it).
- Oophorectomy is reserved for lesions not amenable to cystectomy, in women who have completed childbearing, or when malignancy cannot be excluded.

### Acute presentations
- Adnexal torsion is a surgical emergency; the fatty content and long pedicle of a dermoid predispose to torsion, and prompt detorsion with cystectomy (ovary-sparing where the ovary is viable) or salpingo-oophorectomy (if necrotic) is required (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Rupture with spillage of sebaceous/keratin material causes chemical peritonitis and needs surgical washout plus removal of the cyst.

### If malignant transformation or a distinct malignant germ cell tumour is found
- Malignant transformation of a mature teratoma (almost always squamous cell carcinoma arising from the ectodermal component) is rare and is managed as the corresponding malignancy, not as a benign teratoma; this is a different management pathway from the benign lesion covered here and is outside the scope of this entity's own management.
- If a lesion is instead an immature (malignant) teratoma or another malignant germ cell tumour rather than a mature one, DeVita is explicit that these are treated as malignant germ cell tumours of the ovary: therapy mirrors the testicular germ cell cancer experience, and adjuvant platinum-based combination chemotherapy (bleomycin-etoposide-cisplatin) is given to all except FIGO stage IA dysgerminoma and stage IA, grade 1-2 immature teratoma. This applies to the malignant counterpart, not to mature cystic teratoma itself, and is included here only to mark the diagnostic boundary.

## Role of surgery, radiotherapy, and systemic therapy (summary)
- Surgery: primary and essentially only modality for mature cystic teratoma - cystectomy (fertility-sparing) or oophorectomy/salpingo-oophorectomy depending on age, fertility wishes, ovarian viability, and bilaterality.
- Radiotherapy: no role, per DeVita's general statement that the benign ovarian masses in this group do not require radiotherapy or chemotherapy.
- Systemic therapy: no role for the benign lesion; systemic chemotherapy is reserved for the distinct malignant germ cell tumours or for malignant transformation, not for mature cystic teratoma.

## Monitoring
- Postoperative follow-up is by clinical exam and, where indicated, ultrasound to confirm complete excision and to monitor the contralateral ovary, since bilateral dermoids occur in a minority of patients.
- Routine tumour markers are not required for surveillance of a mature cystic teratoma because it is a benign lesion; marker testing (e.g. AFP, hCG) is instead used to help exclude a coexisting or alternative malignant germ cell tumour when the diagnosis is uncertain preoperatively (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Long-term surveillance beyond confirming complete resection is not oncologic in nature, unlike the malignant germ cell tumours, which do require marker- and imaging-based follow-up.

## When to refer
- Refer to gynaecologic oncology (rather than managing purely as general gynaecology) when imaging is atypical or complex, when malignancy or malignant transformation cannot be excluded, when the lesion is large or bilateral, or when frozen-section or final histology raises the possibility of an immature teratoma or another malignant germ cell element, since those entities require staging and consideration of adjuvant chemotherapy.
- Acute torsion or rupture warrants urgent surgical referral regardless of the benign nature of the underlying lesion.

## What DeVita is silent on for this entity
DeVita does not give a dedicated management section for mature cystic teratoma of the ovary (it is a benign lesion, so it is not covered with the depth given to malignant ovarian cancers). It mentions the entity only as one of the benign mixed cystic/solid ovarian masses in the adnexal-mass differential discussion, and it does not describe specific surgical technique, follow-up interval, or malignant-transformation management for this lesion. Points on surveillance intervals, cystectomy technique, and management of acute torsion/rupture above are marked as general oncology/gynaecology standard rather than DeVita-sourced, and no numeric statistic beyond what DeVita states elsewhere in the KB reference file (not reproduced here) has been added.

Sources: DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of Oncology, 12th ed.
