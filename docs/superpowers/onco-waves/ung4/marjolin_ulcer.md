# Marjolin Ulcer - Management

Marjolin ulcer is not a single tumour type but a malignancy, most often a well-differentiated squamous cell carcinoma, arising within chronically inflamed, scarred, or ulcerated skin (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease, and this entity is not addressed in DeVita's oncology text; it is covered in dermatology and plastic-surgery oncology references). Because DeVita does not carry a dedicated section on Marjolin ulcer, the approach below follows general cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma and wound-malignancy management principles that are uncontroversial across surgical oncology practice, and every claim is marked as such rather than attributed to DeVita.

## Diagnosis before treatment (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease)

- Any chronic wound, burn scar, venous or pressure ulcer, or osteomyelitis sinus that changes (new nodularity, everted or rolled margins, increased pain, bleeding, malodour, or failure to heal) should prompt biopsy rather than continued wound care alone.
- Diagnosis requires multiple deep biopsies from the margin and base of the lesion, because malignant transformation can be focal and a single superficial sample can miss it.
- Once malignancy is confirmed, staging includes clinical and, where indicated, radiologic examination of the regional lymph nodes, plus cross-sectional imaging (CT or MRI) when deep invasion or metastasis is suspected.

## Treatment approach by stage and intent (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease)

**Localised disease (no nodal or distant spread)**
- Wide local excision to clear histologic margins is the primary and usually curative treatment.
- Reconstruction (local flap, skin graft, or free flap depending on site and defect size) follows resection once margins are confirmed clear.
- For extensive limb involvement, or malignant transformation complicating deep chronic osteomyelitis where clear margins cannot otherwise be achieved, amputation may be required.

**Regional (nodal) disease**
- Squamous cell carcinoma arising in a burn scar or other chronic wound is recognised to behave more aggressively than typical sun-related cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma, with a higher risk of nodal metastasis, so the regional nodal basin must be assessed even when clinically silent.
- Proven or strongly suspected nodal metastasis is managed with regional lymph node dissection.
- Adjuvant radiotherapy is added for high-risk features (poor differentiation, deep invasion, perineural or lymphovascular invasion), positive or close surgical margins, or confirmed nodal disease.

**Advanced or unresectable / metastatic disease**
- Where surgery cannot achieve clearance, radiotherapy is used as a locoregional adjunct or, in unresectable disease, as the primary modality.
- Systemic therapy (in the general oncology standard sense: for advanced/unresectable or metastatic cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma, options include platinum-based chemotherapy regimens, EGFR-targeted agents, or immune checkpoint inhibitors, as used in advanced cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma more broadly) may be considered, but the reference material grounding this KB entry does not itself detail a specific systemic regimen for Marjolin ulcer, so systemic therapy should be individualised through multidisciplinary discussion rather than treated as protocolised here.

## Lines of therapy

There is no established sequential "first-line/second-line" systemic therapy ladder specific to Marjolin ulcer in the source material used for this entry. Surgery is the mainstay across essentially all resectable presentations; radiotherapy and systemic therapy are adjuncts reserved for high-risk, nodal, or unresectable/metastatic disease rather than routine first-line steps (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Monitoring

- After treatment, surveillance for local recurrence and for nodal or distant metastasis is required, particularly given the more aggressive behaviour of burn-scar-associated carcinoma.
- Any residual chronic wound or unstable scar, treated or not, should remain under long-term surveillance, with prompt re-biopsy if it changes in appearance or behaviour.
- Prevention is part of ongoing management: proactively treating chronic non-healing wounds and biopsying long-standing scars or ulcers that change reduces the risk of delayed diagnosis.

## When to refer

- Any chronic wound with red-flag change (non-healing despite optimal wound care, new nodularity, everted edges, bleeding, rapid change, or a wound arising in an old burn scar or chronic osteomyelitis sinus) should be referred for biopsy and specialist assessment rather than managed further as a simple chronic wound.
- Confirmed or strongly suspected Marjolin ulcer should be directed through a multidisciplinary oncology team spanning plastic surgery, surgical oncology, and dermatology (and radiation/medical oncology where nodal or advanced disease is present), given the need for coordinated staging, resection planning, reconstruction, and adjuvant treatment decisions.
