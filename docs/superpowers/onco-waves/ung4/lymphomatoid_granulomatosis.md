# Lymphomatoid granulomatosis (EBV) — Management

Lymphomatoid granulomatosis (LYG) is an EBV-driven, angiocentric and angiodestructive B-cell
lymphoproliferative disorder that most often involves the lungs and can also affect skin, the
central nervous system, kidney, and liver, while typically sparing lymph nodes and bone marrow.
It occurs more often in patients with an underlying immunodeficiency, whether congenital,
iatrogenic, or acquired. Management is grade-directed: histologic grading (grade one to three,
based on the density of EBV-positive large B-cells and the extent of necrosis) separates a
low-grade, more indolent process from disease that behaves as an overt EBV-positive large
B-cell lymphoma, and this grade is what determines treatment intensity (general oncology
standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease, though consistent with the WHO
classification-based grading described for this entity).

## Treatment approach by grade/intent

(General oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease, for the grade-directed
treatment approach described in this section.)

- **Low-grade disease (grade 1, and some grade 2):** the first step is to correct or reduce
  any contributing immunosuppression (for example dose-reducing iatrogenic immunosuppressants,
  treating underlying HIV). Immune-modulating therapy, particularly interferon-alfa, is used
  with the intent of restoring T-cell immune surveillance over the EBV-infected B-cell clone;
  disease can occasionally regress once immune competence is restored. Single-agent
  rituximab is an option for CD20-positive lower-grade disease that does not respond to immune
  modulation alone.
- **High-grade disease (grade 3, behaving as EBV-positive large B-cell lymphoma):** treated as
  an aggressive B-cell lymphoma, with rituximab-based combination immunochemotherapy built
  around a CHOP-like (anthracycline-containing) backbone as the standard approach. No specific
  drug doses or schedules are specified here; those live in the structured protocol templates.
- Across both grades, correcting or addressing any underlying immunodeficiency (including HIV)
  is an integral part of management, not an adjunct to it.

## Role of surgery and radiotherapy

Surgery and radiotherapy are not established primary treatment modalities for LYG. The disease
is systemic and multifocal by nature (angiocentric infiltration of small and medium vessels,
usually with bilateral pulmonary nodules), so local therapies are not the mainstay; a role for
either could not be grounded from the available material and should not be assumed (needs
manual sourcing if a local-therapy question arises, for example symptomatic control of a
dominant cavitating lesion).

## Lines of therapy

(General oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease, for the first-line
sequencing described below.)

- First line for low-grade disease: reduction of immunosuppression plus interferon-alfa, with
  single-agent rituximab as an alternative or next step for CD20-positive disease.
- First line for high-grade (large B-cell lymphoma-equivalent) disease: rituximab combined with
  a CHOP-like anthracycline-containing regimen.
- Beyond first line, the material available does not specify a defined salvage sequence for LYG
  specifically; relapsed or refractory high-grade disease would generally be approached along
  the lines used for relapsed/refractory diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (general oncology
  standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease), but this is not something to present as
  disease-specific guidance without further sourcing.

## Monitoring

- Serial cross-sectional imaging (CT chest, given the predominantly pulmonary pattern of
  nodular, sometimes cavitating disease) to track response and detect progression.
- Repeat tissue biopsy when there is a change in behavior or imaging pattern, since histologic
  grade can shift over time and detecting transformation to higher-grade disease changes
  management.
- Directed imaging (brain MRI) and clinical follow-up for extrapulmonary sites, particularly the
  central nervous system, given that CNS involvement is a marker of more aggressive disease.
- Ongoing assessment of immune status (for example HIV status, immunoglobulin levels) since the
  underlying immunodeficiency is part of what drives disease behavior and recurrence risk.

## When to refer

- All patients with suspected or confirmed LYG should be managed under haemato-oncology with
  multidisciplinary input from the outset, given the diagnostic overlap with vasculitis,
  infection, and other lymphomas, and the need for expertise in grading and immunochemotherapy.
- Refer promptly for tissue biopsy with immunohistochemistry and EBV in-situ hybridization when
  LYG is suspected clinically or radiologically, since grade cannot be determined without it and
  grade dictates treatment.
- Escalate to inpatient/urgent haemato-oncology and pulmonology input for progressive respiratory
  failure from extensive pulmonary disease, and to neurology/neuro-oncology for new neurological
  deficits suggesting CNS involvement.
- Refer for correction of the underlying immune defect (for example HIV care, adjustment of
  iatrogenic immunosuppression) in parallel with lymphoma-directed treatment, not as an
  afterthought.
