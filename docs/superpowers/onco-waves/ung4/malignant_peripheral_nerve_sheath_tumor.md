# Management: Malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumour (NF1-associated) (MPNST)

## Overview and setting
MPNST is a highly aggressive soft tissue sarcoma that arises from nerve sheath (usually Schwann cell) lineage, most often in adults between 20 and 50 years. It is associated with major nerves of the body wall and extremities, and the lower extremity and retroperitoneum are the most common sites, though MPNST can arise anywhere. It occurs sporadically, after radiotherapy, or in patients with neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1), who carry a lifetime risk of 8 to 13 percent, typically developing the tumour within a pre-existing plexiform or atypical neurofibroma. Tumour size and margin status are the factors most consistently linked to disease-specific survival across sporadic, NF1-associated and radiation-associated MPNST, and radiation-induced tumours in particular carry a significantly higher risk of local recurrence than sporadic or NF1-associated disease.

## Diagnosis and staging (bears on treatment planning)
- In a patient with NF1, rapid growth, increasing pain, or a new neurological deficit in a known neurofibroma should prompt urgent evaluation for malignant transformation.
- Increased FDG avidity on PET, or heterogeneity on contrast MRI, can help identify the site of transformation within a plexiform neurofibroma and guide biopsy of the most suspicious area.
- Diagnosis is confirmed by biopsy; most MPNSTs are high-grade. S100 staining is often only focal (general pathology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease), and weak S100 staining itself is associated with a higher risk of distant metastasis, so diagnosis should not rest on S100 alone. Loss of H3K27 trimethylation on immunohistochemistry is a sensitive additional marker, most consistently positive in sporadic and radiation-associated tumours and less consistently in NF1-related high-grade MPNST.
- Staging should assess the primary site (MRI) and screen for distant disease, particularly pulmonary metastases (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease, though consistent with the chest CT staging referenced generally for soft tissue sarcoma).

## Localized (primary, resectable) disease
- Complete surgical resection, with or without adjuvant radiotherapy, is the most important treatment for primary disease. This should be undertaken with attention to achieving negative margins, since margin status is one of the two factors most consistently tied to survival across MPNST subtypes.
- Adjuvant radiotherapy is used for local control, particularly where margins are close or the tumour is high-grade (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease, drawing on the general principle of adjuvant radiotherapy for high-grade soft tissue sarcoma; MPNST is also recognised as relatively radioresistant).
- The role of neoadjuvant chemotherapy for large primary or locally recurrent MPNST remains controversial, and outcomes with chemotherapy in this disease have generally been disappointing.
- Care should be delivered through a multidisciplinary sarcoma team spanning surgical, medical, and clinical oncology (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Locally recurrent, unresectable, or metastatic disease
- Response rates to chemotherapy in MPNST are modest, with better outcomes reported when an alkylating agent (ifosfamide) is combined with an anthracycline-based regimen than with an anthracycline alone.
- Targeted approaches have been explored based on the biology of MPNST: most tumours show loss of the NF1 tumour-suppressor gene with downstream activation of the Ras/Raf/MAPK pathway, which supported trials of a B-Raf-targeting tyrosine kinase inhibitor. In a phase II trial of this agent in metastatic MPNST, no patients had a formal response by RECIST criteria, though a minority had stable disease or regression of metastatic deposits. This reflects an area of active investigation rather than an established standard, and outcomes with systemic therapy in advanced MPNST overall remain poor.
- For limited pulmonary metastatic disease, metastasectomy can be considered in appropriately selected patients (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease, drawn from general sarcoma metastasis management).

## Monitoring and follow-up
- Long-term surveillance for local recurrence and metastasis with clinical review and cross-sectional imaging is required given the substantial recurrence and metastatic risk of this tumour (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- In patients with NF1, plexiform and atypical neurofibromas should be monitored for signs of transformation (new pain, growth, or neurological deficit), with genetic counselling and family assessment offered given the underlying heritable condition.

## When to refer
- Any suspected MPNST, including suspected malignant transformation of a neurofibroma in an NF1 patient, should be referred promptly to a specialist sarcoma centre for biopsy, staging, and multidisciplinary treatment planning, given the aggressiveness of this tumour and the technical demands of achieving adequate surgical margins near major nerves.
- Patients with NF1 and a new or enlarging plexiform neurofibroma, or new neurological symptoms, warrant referral for functional imaging and biopsy of the most suspicious region rather than continued observation alone.
