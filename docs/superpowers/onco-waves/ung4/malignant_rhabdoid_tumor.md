# Malignant rhabdoid tumour (SMARCB1) - management narrative

## Overview
Malignant rhabdoid tumour (MRT) is a highly aggressive embryonal malignancy of infancy and early childhood, defined by biallelic loss of SMARCB1 (INI1), a core subunit of the SWI/SNF chromatin-remodelling complex, with a small subset instead showing SMARCA4 loss. It arises in the kidney, central nervous system (where it is termed atypical teratoid/rhabdoid tumour, AT/RT), and soft tissue, and can occur as part of a rhabdoid tumour predisposition syndrome (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease). DeVita's text does not carry a dedicated management section for this entity; the one disease-specific management-relevant statement located is that EZH2-targeting agents are in clinical trials for SMARCB1-deficient neoplasms, explicitly including malignant rhabdoid tumour, reflecting the biological rationale that SMARCB1 loss drives dependence on the polycomb repressive complex (EZH2 upregulation). ## Treatment approach by stage and intent
- All patients are treated with curative intent multimodality therapy where feasible, given the aggressive natural history (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Localised, completely resectable disease: maximal safe surgical resection followed by multi-agent chemotherapy, with radiotherapy added for local control depending on age and site (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Metastatic or disseminated disease, including synchronous central nervous system involvement: systemic multi-agent chemotherapy is intensified and treatment is coordinated across all sites of disease, including intrathecal therapy when the central nervous system is involved; prognosis is markedly worse (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Very young infants pose a particular treatment dilemma because radiotherapy carries substantial long-term neurodevelopmental toxicity at this age, so radiotherapy timing and volume are individualised against the risk of under-treating an aggressive tumour (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Enrolment in a clinical trial should be considered whenever available, given that outcomes with conventional therapy remain poor (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Lines of therapy
- First-line treatment is multi-agent, multimodality therapy combining surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy as above (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Some protocols intensify treatment with high-dose chemotherapy supported by autologous stem-cell rescue, particularly for high-risk or relapsed disease (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- At relapse, options are limited and outcomes poor; enrolment in a clinical trial is the preferred approach where one is open (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Investigationally, EZH2-targeting agents are being studied in clinical trials for SMARCB1-deficient neoplasms, a category that explicitly includes malignant rhabdoid tumour (along with epithelioid sarcoma and poorly differentiated chordoma), based on the biological rationale that SMARCB1 loss leads to functional dependence on EZH2/polycomb repressive complex activity. This is the one point directly grounded in DeVita's text (found in the chordoma discussion, where MRT is named as sharing the same SMARCB1-deficient biology).

## Role of surgery, radiotherapy, and systemic therapy
- Surgery: maximal safe resection of the primary tumour is central to local control wherever feasible without unacceptable functional loss (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Radiotherapy: used for site-directed local control, with the treating team balancing local control benefit against the toxicity of radiation in very young children (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Systemic chemotherapy: multi-agent regimens (drug classes and combinations are protocol-specific and not detailed here) form the backbone of treatment for both localised and disseminated disease, with intrathecal chemotherapy added when there is central nervous system involvement (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Supportive care: comprehensive paediatric supportive care, coordinated by a paediatric oncology team, is required throughout treatment given the intensity of therapy and the young age of most patients (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Monitoring
- Full staging at diagnosis should include cross-sectional imaging of the primary site, chest imaging for pulmonary metastases, neuraxis imaging to detect synchronous central nervous system disease, and cerebrospinal fluid assessment where indicated (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- During and after treatment, response and surveillance imaging should continue to cover the primary site, chest, and neuraxis given the propensity for disseminated and central nervous system spread (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Because malignant rhabdoid tumour can occur as part of a germline rhabdoid tumour predisposition syndrome, germline SMARCB1 testing (and SMARCA4 testing where indicated) with genetic counselling should be arranged, with family screening considered where a predisposition syndrome is confirmed (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Long-term follow-up should also track neurodevelopmental and endocrine sequelae of intensive multimodality therapy in survivors, given the young age at treatment (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## When to refer
- All suspected or confirmed cases should be referred promptly to a paediatric oncology centre with experience managing rhabdoid tumours, given the rarity and aggressiveness of the disease (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Refer for clinical genetics assessment whenever germline SMARCB1/SMARCA4 involvement is suspected, particularly in very young children or those with multifocal disease, or where there is a family history suggestive of a predisposition syndrome (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Refer to a centre with an open clinical trial whenever one is available, given the limited efficacy of conventional therapy, including trials of EZH2-targeting agents in SMARCB1-deficient tumours (general oncology standard for trial referral pathway; the EZH2 trial fact itself is DeVita-grounded as above).
