# Extranodal marginal zone (MALT) lymphoma - management narrative

Extranodal marginal zone lymphoma (EMZL), also called MALT lymphoma, is an indolent B-cell
non-Hodgkin lymphoma most often diagnosed at an early stage confined to the tissue of origin. The
approach to treatment is driven mainly by stage and site rather than by a single uniform algorithm,
and it should be planned with a haemato-oncology multidisciplinary team.

## Overall treatment philosophy by stage

- About two thirds of patients present with stage I/II (localised) disease. In localised disease,
  treatment with radiation therapy, or with antibiotic therapy where an infectious driver such as
  H. pylori is present, is associated with high response rates and durable responses, some of which
  may represent cures. Long-term outcome in localised disease is excellent, with a reported 5-year
  overall survival around 89%.
- In advanced-stage (disseminated) disease, EMZL behaves as an indolent lymphoma with long overall
  survival, and close observation at diagnosis is appropriate until signs, symptoms, or organ
  impairment develop from the disease, mirroring the watch-and-wait approach used in other indolent
  lymphomas (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease, though DeVita
  states the general principle of observation until organ-impairing progression for advanced EMZL).
- Treatment of relapsed, refractory, or advanced-stage disease that has become symptomatic or is
  causing organ impairment is generally extrapolated from the follicular lymphoma literature, since
  there are few dedicated studies of EMZL outside of early-stage disease.

## Site- and driver-specific first-line approach

- **Gastric EMZL** is the paradigm for treating early-stage, localised disease. When gastric disease
  is associated with H. pylori infection and does NOT carry the t(11;18) translocation, eradication
  of H. pylori (an antibiotic-based regimen, agent and duration not specified here) is effective
  first-line treatment and produces good long-term disease control and overall survival.
- If the lymphoma is H. pylori-negative, carries the t(11;18) API2-MALT1 translocation (which
  predicts a poor response to H. pylori-directed therapy), or fails to regress after documented
  eradication, radiation therapy is the preferred treatment modality for localised disease.
- **Ocular adnexal EMZL** is primarily treated with radiation therapy; a low-dose approach has
  minimal toxicity and has been associated with high overall response rates and durable local
  disease control. Antibiotic therapy targeting Chlamydophila psittaci has also been studied for
  this site given a described association between that infection and ocular adnexal MALT lymphoma,
  with lower and less durable response rates than radiotherapy.
- EMZL associated with hepatitis C infection is a specific exception even in advanced-stage disease:
  antiviral therapy directed at the HCV infection may itself produce lymphoma regression and should
  be considered before other systemic treatment.
- Non-gastric, non-ocular extranodal sites (salivary gland, thyroid, lung, skin, and others) follow
  the same general principle of radiotherapy or site-appropriate local therapy for localised disease,
  with surgery having a role mainly in obtaining diagnostic tissue and managing site-specific
  complications rather than as primary curative therapy (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's
  section on this disease).

## Role of surgery

- Surgery's main role is diagnostic (endoscopic or image-guided biopsy to confirm histology and
  immunophenotype) and management of site-specific complications such as bleeding, obstruction, or
  perforation in gastrointestinal disease, rather than a primary treatment strategy for gastric or
  other EMZL. A separate DeVita table on small bowel lymphoma variants that includes marginal zone
  B-cell lymphoma of the small bowel lists "surgery and/or chemotherapy" alongside H. pylori
  eradication as treatment options in that specific location.

## Role of radiotherapy

- Radiotherapy is the preferred modality for localised disease that is H. pylori-negative, harbours
  t(11;18), or has failed to regress after eradication therapy, and it is the primary modality for
  ocular adnexal disease.
- EMZL is described as extremely sensitive to radiation, and even low-dose radiotherapy can provide
  effective and durable local disease control or palliation.

## Systemic therapy (relapsed, refractory, or advanced/symptomatic disease)

- Systemic therapy is generally reserved for disease that is relapsed or refractory to antibiotic
  therapy or radiotherapy, or for more advanced-stage or symptomatic disease, and its approach mirrors
  that used for follicular lymphoma.
- Options that have been used and reported include single-agent alkylating agents, single-agent
  anti-CD20 antibody therapy (rituximab), purine-analog-based regimens, and combination
  chemoimmunotherapy (an anti-CD20 antibody combined with a purine analog), with combination
  chemoimmunotherapy giving higher response rates at the cost of greater toxicity than single-agent
  approaches. Occasionally, multiagent anthracycline-based chemotherapy is used in younger patients
  with more aggressive presentations.
- Single-agent anti-CD20 antibody therapy has activity across gastric and non-gastric EMZL, is more
  effective in antibody- and chemotherapy-naive patients than in previously treated patients, but
  responses can be relatively short-lived in a meaningful proportion of responders.
- For the broader category of marginal zone lymphomas (including nodal and splenic MZL, which are
  managed similarly to EMZL once systemic therapy is needed), a Bruton tyrosine kinase inhibitor
  (ibrutinib) is approved for relapsed disease based on a demonstrated overall response rate, and a
  second Bruton tyrosine kinase inhibitor (zanubrutinib) is also an approved option for
  relapsed/refractory disease. A PI3K inhibitor (umbralisib) has also been approved in this relapsed
  or refractory setting. CD19-directed CAR T-cell therapy continues to be explored for
  relapsed/refractory marginal zone lymphoma in the third line and beyond. These options are
  documented for marginal zone lymphoma as a category rather than specifically restricted to EMZL in
  the source material.

## Monitoring

- After H. pylori eradication in gastric disease, endoscopic surveillance with repeat biopsy is used
  to confirm histological remission; regression can be slow, and surveillance should not be
  discontinued prematurely (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Ongoing monitoring should watch for transformation to a more aggressive lymphoma, most commonly an
  ABC-like diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, which occurs rarely in EMZL; a rapidly enlarging mass,
  ulceration, or a new organ-impairing presentation should prompt rebiopsy.
- Staging and monitoring for EMZL includes cross-sectional imaging of chest, abdomen, and pelvis,
  imaging of the neck (including parotid and salivary glands) and orbits, and bone marrow biopsy
  consideration for patients with multifocal disease; evaluation of the gastric mucosa is reasonable
  for all patients with non-gastric EMZL given the documented high rate of concurrent gastric
  involvement.

## When to refer

- All patients with EMZL should be managed through a haemato-oncology multidisciplinary team, with
  site-specific specialist input (for example gastroenterology for gastric disease, ophthalmology for
  ocular adnexal disease, or infectious disease/hepatology when hepatitis C is implicated) (general
  oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease, though the need for MDT-level
  management fits the complexity of stage- and site-driven decisions described in DeVita).
- Refer promptly for rebiopsy and re-staging if there is failure of documented H. pylori eradication
  to produce regression, a new t(11;18)-positive or otherwise poorly responding pattern, or any
  clinical or laboratory feature suggesting transformation to an aggressive lymphoma.

Sources: DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of Oncology, 12th ed.
