# Laryngeal cancer — management (wave 5 expansion)

Synonyms: larynx cancer, laryngeal carcinoma, cancer of the voice box.

## Framing the decision

Nearly all laryngeal cancers are squamous cell carcinomas, and the treatment plan is built
around two things: the anatomical subsite (supraglottis, glottis, or subglottis) and the T/N
stage, because these determine how much of the larynx can be preserved without giving up
cure. A head and neck multidisciplinary team should make the primary treatment decision,
since surgery, radiotherapy, and systemic therapy are often interchangeable options for the
same lesion with different functional trade-offs.

## Carcinoma in situ and very early disease

Carcinoma in situ and microinvasive vocal cord disease are treated the same way: either
transoral laser microsurgery (endoscopic resection) or radiotherapy, with broadly equivalent
control rates. Radiotherapy is generally favoured for patients who go on to have multiple
recurrences after laser resection, since it is not limited by prior local tissue removal.

## Early glottic (vocal cord) cancer, T1 to T2

- Radiotherapy and transoral laser microsurgery achieve comparable cure rates; radiotherapy
  is more often chosen because it tends to give better voice quality, while laser surgery is a
  single treatment episode and is cheaper, and can be repeated if a second head and neck
  cancer later develops in a previously irradiated field.
- An open partial laryngectomy (for example hemilaryngectomy) is an alternative for selected
  limited cord lesions when voice preservation is still the goal, but is used less often now
  that transoral techniques are available.
- Verrucous carcinoma is managed with transoral laser resection or open partial laryngectomy;
  radiotherapy is reserved for cases where the alternative would otherwise be a total
  laryngectomy.
- If radiotherapy fails, salvage with cordectomy, partial laryngectomy, or total laryngectomy
  is usually still possible, since early recurrences after radiotherapy respond well to
  surgical salvage.

## Early supraglottic cancer, T1 to T2 and low-volume T3

- T1, T2, and low-volume T3 (roughly under 6 mL) supraglottic lesions are considered
  favourable and can be treated with either definitive radiotherapy or a supraglottic
  laryngectomy (open, transoral laser, or transoral robotic surgery); combining radiotherapy
  and surgery for the primary site is usually unnecessary unless neck disease needs it.
- Candidates for supraglottic laryngectomy need anatomically suitable disease, resectable
  neck nodes, and lungs able to tolerate the temporary aspiration risk that follows partial
  laryngectomy.
- If the primary is early-stage but the neck disease is bulky (N2b to N3), a combined
  approach is preferred: treat the primary with chemoradiotherapy and add neck dissection to
  the involved side(s) if a repeat PET-CT roughly three months after chemoradiotherapy still
  shows nodal disease.

## Advanced disease, T3 to T4

- Advanced laryngeal cancer needs multimodality treatment. Low-volume T3/T4 glottic or
  supraglottic lesions that are mainly exophytic can still be treated with organ-preserving
  concurrent chemoradiotherapy; a platinum agent given alongside radiotherapy is the standard
  radiosensitising approach (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this
  disease, drawn from DeVita's broader head-and-neck chemoradiation chapter).
- Higher-volume, endophytic tumours, and those with vocal cord fixation or a poorly
  functioning larynx, are generally better served by total laryngectomy with neck dissection
  and postoperative radiotherapy or chemoradiotherapy, since these lesions respond less
  reliably to organ-preserving radiation.
- For T4a disease specifically, primary total laryngectomy plus adjuvant radiotherapy or
  chemoradiotherapy is the consensus-preferred approach, because several studies suggested
  better survival with upfront surgery than with nonsurgical organ preservation in this group.
- For T3 disease the choice between organ preservation and laryngectomy is less clear-cut and
  depends heavily on tumour volume and local expertise; some centres use a tumour-volume
  cut-off to decide, offering organ preservation below the threshold and laryngectomy above
  it.
- Postoperative radiotherapy or chemoradiotherapy after total laryngectomy is preferred when
  the disease was resectable; concurrent chemotherapy is added mainly when there are positive
  margins or extranodal extension.
- Subglottic carcinoma: early lesions are treated with radiotherapy; advanced subglottic
  lesions are usually managed with total laryngectomy and postoperative radiotherapy or
  chemoradiotherapy.

## Recurrent or metastatic disease

DeVita's laryngeal cancer chapter itself is focused on locoregional treatment and does not
detail systemic therapy for distant metastatic disease. For recurrent or metastatic head and
neck squamous cell carcinoma more broadly, platinum-based chemotherapy combined with an
EGFR-targeted antibody, and immune checkpoint inhibition (anti-PD-1 agents), have shown
activity and are used (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease,
drawn from DeVita's broader recurrent/metastatic head-and-neck chapter). Selection between
these options depends on PD-L1 status, prior treatment, and performance status assessed by
a head and neck oncology team; the KB deliberately omits any specific regimen sequencing
here because that was not grounded in the laryngeal cancer chapter itself.

## Supportive care and rehabilitation

- Voice rehabilitation after total laryngectomy has several options: a tracheoesophageal
  voice prosthesis, an electronic ("electric") larynx, or esophageal speech; a meaningful
  proportion of patients end up using an electronic device rather than tracheoesophageal
  speech in long-term follow-up.
- Airway obstruction from the tumour itself, or laryngeal oedema during or after
  radiotherapy, can require urgent airway intervention or a tracheostomy; this should be
  anticipated and not missed in bulky or endophytic disease.
- After chemoradiotherapy, a delayed reassessment (imaging around three months out) is used
  before deciding whether residual neck disease needs a neck dissection, because earlier
  imaging can overcall persistent disease that is actually treatment effect.

## Monitoring and second primaries

Patients with laryngeal cancer carry an increased risk of a second head and neck cancer,
attributed to the field effect of tobacco exposure across the aerodigestive tract; ongoing
surveillance should watch for this, alongside locoregional recurrence, and worsening
laryngeal oedema or new vocal cord fixation should prompt evaluation for recurrence.

## When to refer

Any new diagnosis of laryngeal cancer should go to a head and neck multidisciplinary team
(head and neck surgery, radiation oncology, medical oncology, speech and language therapy)
before a treatment modality is chosen, because the surgery/radiotherapy/chemoradiotherapy
trade-off is subsite- and stage-specific and best made jointly. Stridor or rapidly worsening
airway obstruction warrants urgent referral for airway management ahead of definitive
oncologic treatment.
