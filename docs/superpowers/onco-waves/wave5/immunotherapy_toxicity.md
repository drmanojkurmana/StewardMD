# Immunotherapy toxicity (immune-related adverse events, irAE) - Management

## Overview and mechanism

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (anti-CTLA-4, anti-PD-1, and anti-PD-L1 antibodies) work by releasing normal brakes on T-cell activation. DeVita's chapter on checkpoint modulators describes these toxicities as mechanism-related and dose/schedule related, representing autoinflammatory phenomena rather than the direct cytotoxic side effects seen with chemotherapy. The most common manifestations recorded with ipilimumab and tremelimumab in early development were rash, colitis, hepatitis, and endocrinopathies (especially hypothyroidism); the described spectrum has since expanded to include myocarditis, pneumonitis, neurologic syndromes (including Guillain-Barre syndrome and myasthenia gravis), nephritis, and haemolytic anaemia. Because this pattern of injury mimics autoimmune disease and can involve almost any organ, distinguishing it from infection or disease progression is essential before attributing new symptoms to an immune reaction (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease, though consistent with the reference note's pitfalls).

Toxicity can present at any time on treatment, and DeVita's discussion of monotherapy versus combination checkpoint blockade documents that combination therapy produces materially higher rates of grade 3 to 4 irAEs than single-agent therapy (an adjuvant ipilimumab trial reported grade 3-4-5 treatment-related irAEs in 41% of patients, with a third of the treated arm stopping therapy because of toxicity, versus the roughly 10% to 15% grade 3/4 rate historically reported with single-agent ipilimumab). This dose/intensity relationship is the single strongest lever clinicians have in preventing severe toxicity: combination regimens should be reserved for settings where the added efficacy justifies the added risk of toxicity, and the choice between monotherapy and combination checkpoint blockade is itself a toxicity-management decision, not only an efficacy one.

## Treatment approach by severity (grading-driven, not stage-driven)

Unlike a solid tumour, irAE management is organized around severity grade and organ involved rather than cancer stage. DeVita states the basic principles of management are periodic holding of therapy and use of corticosteroids, with diagnostic procedures (for example colonoscopy for suspected autoimmune colitis) considered but not mandatory. Drawing on this and on the KB's existing reference notes for this disease:

- **Mild (grade 1) events**: continue the checkpoint inhibitor with close monitoring and supportive care; treat symptomatically (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- **Moderate (grade 2) events**: withhold the checkpoint inhibitor and start corticosteroids; resume therapy once the event resolves to grade 1 or less, per the reference note's management principles.
- **Severe (grade 3-4) events**: withhold or permanently discontinue the checkpoint inhibitor depending on the organ and reversibility, and treat with corticosteroids as the cornerstone of therapy.
- **Steroid-refractory events**: escalate to additional immunosuppression. DeVita specifically notes that steroids, or in some cases TNF-alpha blocking antibodies, rapidly and permanently reverse almost all manifestations of ipilimumab-associated colitis or hypophysitis without recurrence of symptoms once given. The reference note names infliximab or vedolizumab for steroid-refractory colitis and mycophenolate mofetil for steroid-refractory hepatitis as organ-specific escalation options.

## Organ-specific approach

- **Colitis**: hold the checkpoint inhibitor, start corticosteroids, and escalate to infliximab or vedolizumab if steroid-refractory. DeVita notes colonoscopy may be considered to confirm the diagnosis but is not mandatory before starting treatment; infectious causes of diarrhoea should be excluded first.
- **Hepatitis**: monitor liver function at baseline and at intervals; escalate to mycophenolate mofetil for steroid-refractory disease.
- **Endocrinopathy (thyroid dysfunction, hypophysitis, adrenal insufficiency)**: these are described in DeVita as among the earliest-recognised irAEs, alongside colitis and hepatitis. Once diagnosed, they typically need long-term hormone replacement (levothyroxine for hypothyroidism, corticosteroid replacement for adrenal insufficiency or hypophysitis) that continues even after the acute immune reaction has settled; the therapy interruption itself does not usually reverse an established endocrine deficit. Unrecognised adrenal insufficiency or hypophysitis is a recognised trap because it can precipitate a life-threatening adrenal crisis.
- **Pneumonitis**: DeVita's own case discussion of anti-PD-1-associated pneumonitis in melanoma patients illustrates the range of severity, from ARDS-pattern pneumonitis needing intensive care, intravenous glucocorticoids, and infliximab (one fatality despite treatment), to milder interstitial pneumonitis that resolved on outpatient oral glucocorticoids with successful resumption of therapy afterward. This underlines that pneumonitis severity is highly variable and that oxygenation status and imaging pattern should guide whether inpatient escalation is needed.
- **Myocarditis**: the reference note flags this as a red flag requiring urgent high-dose corticosteroids and cardiology input because of its high mortality; this is consistent with DeVita's broader point that severe irAEs across organs are the main limitation to safe use of checkpoint inhibitors.

## Rechallenge and reintroduction

DeVita's discussion of anti-PD-1 therapy describes that in patients whose disease recurs or progresses after stopping treatment for reasons including toxicity, reintroduction of the same class of therapy can produce objective responses and clinical benefit in a subset of patients. This supports a practice of considering rechallenge once an irAE has resolved and corticosteroids have been weaned, balancing that potential benefit against the risk of recurrent or new toxicity on rechallenge, with the decision individualised to the severity and reversibility of the prior event (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease, for the risk-balancing framing itself).

## Role of surgery and radiotherapy

Surgery and radiotherapy do not have a primary role in treating the immune toxicity itself. DeVita notes that in patients whose disease relapses in a single or limited number of sites after checkpoint inhibitor therapy, local treatment of that recurrence with surgery or radiation can produce prolonged progression-free survival in a subset, but this pertains to managing oligoprogressive cancer alongside continued or resumed immunotherapy, not to treating an irAE.

## Response assessment nuance relevant to management decisions

Checkpoint inhibitor-treated patients can show atypical response patterns, including apparent early progression that later regresses ("pseudoprogression"), which DeVita's chapter frames as a reason that immune-related response criteria were developed alongside standard RECIST. This matters operationally because a scan suggesting progression during or shortly after treatment of an irAE should not automatically be assumed to represent true cancer progression, and continuing therapy (once any irAE is controlled) may still be appropriate in carefully selected patients who are clinically stable, per the corridor of overall survival data DeVita cites for this pattern.

## Monitoring

Baseline and interval monitoring of liver function, renal function, thyroid function, and glucose is recommended to detect toxicity early, per the reference note, consistent with DeVita's description of endocrinopathy and hepatitis as recurrent, sometimes clinically silent, complications of checkpoint blockade. Assessment of the adrenal and pituitary axis (cortisol and relevant pituitary hormones) should be added when endocrinopathy is suspected clinically.

## When to refer

- Any suspected myocarditis, severe colitis with risk of perforation, or hypoxic pneumonitis warrants urgent same-day escalation to the relevant organ specialist (cardiology, gastroenterology, respiratory medicine) alongside oncology, given the reference note's description of these as potentially fatal red flags.
- Suspected new endocrinopathy should prompt endocrinology input, particularly when adrenal insufficiency or hypophysitis is suspected, because of the risk of adrenal crisis if unrecognised.
- Steroid-refractory toxicity of any organ should be escalated for specialist-guided second-line immunosuppression rather than escalating steroid dose empirically.
- Overall, DeVita frames irAE management as requiring multidisciplinary input; the reference note likewise calls for management through a multidisciplinary team with organ-specialist involvement and clear safety-netting of patients for the possibility of delayed toxicity, since irAEs can arise weeks to months after the last dose of therapy.

Sources: DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of Oncology, 12th ed.
