# Hereditary leiomyomatosis and renal cell cancer (HLRCC / Reed syndrome) - management

## Overview relevant to treatment planning

HLRCC is caused by a germline fumarate hydratase (FH) mutation. The renal tumours that arise in this
syndrome are typically unilateral and solitary, are usually type 2 papillary or collecting-duct
histology, and behave aggressively: DeVita's kidney-cancer chapter notes that HLRCC-associated renal
tumours can develop with early age of onset and that they can metastasise and cause death within five
years of diagnosis, carrying a generally poor prognosis. This aggressiveness is the reason management
of the renal component of HLRCC differs from management of an equivalent-sized sporadic small renal
mass.

## Treatment approach by stage and intent

### Localised renal disease
- DeVita's chapter on cancer of the kidney explicitly flags HLRCC-associated RCC as an exception to
  the usual size-based active-surveillance pathway ("the 3 cm rule") used for small sporadic renal
  masses, stating that early treatment is recommended for these tumours given their generally poor
  prognosis. In practice this means a renal mass identified in a known or suspected FH-mutation carrier
  should prompt early surgical treatment rather than a watch-and-rescan approach, even at a size that
  would otherwise be considered low risk.
- Surgical resection (radical nephrectomy, or a nephron-sparing procedure only when margins can be kept
  wide) is the mainstay of treatment for localised disease (general oncology standard, not from
  DeVita's section on this disease). Because these tumours are often solitary and can metastasise while
  still small, the surgical margin and the decision to proceed to surgery without a surveillance
  interval take priority over nephron preservation.

### Metastatic or advanced renal disease
- DeVita's HLRCC section does not describe a systemic regimen for metastatic FH-deficient RCC; the
  chapter's discussion of bevacizumab-based combinations in that book appears elsewhere, in the
  hepatocellular carcinoma systemic-therapy section, and cannot be carried across as HLRCC-specific
  evidence.
- Combined antiangiogenic (VEGF-pathway) and EGFR-pathway blockade has been used for FH-deficient/HLRCC
  metastatic renal cancer in prospective study, reflecting the pseudohypoxic, HIF-driven biology of
  these tumours (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease). Enrolment in a
  clinical trial should be actively sought for metastatic disease given how limited the dedicated
  evidence base is.
- Standard clear-cell RCC systemic regimens (tyrosine-kinase inhibitors, PD-1/PD-L1-directed therapy)
  are not established as the preferred approach for this molecularly distinct tumour type and should
  not be assumed to apply by default (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this
  disease).

### Extrarenal manifestations (cutaneous and uterine leiomyomas)
DeVita's HLRCC section, as located, is focused on the molecular genetics and renal oncology of the
syndrome and does not detail management of the cutaneous or uterine leiomyomas. The following reflects
general, guideline-consistent practice rather than DeVita sourcing, and is marked accordingly (general
oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease):
- Painful cutaneous piloleiomyomas can be managed medically (agents that reduce smooth-muscle spasm and
  neuropathic pain) with excision or ablation reserved for troublesome individual lesions.
- Symptomatic uterine leiomyomas are managed as in non-syndromic fibroid disease, escalating from
  hormonal therapy to myomectomy or hysterectomy for bulk symptoms or severe menorrhagia, with earlier
  recourse to surgery than in the general population because HLRCC fibroids tend to be numerous and
  symptomatic at a young age.

## Lines of therapy

There is no defined multi-line systemic therapy sequence for HLRCC-associated RCC grounded in the
DeVita text located for this disease. For localised disease, surgery is first-line and, per DeVita,
should not be deferred behind a surveillance interval. For metastatic disease, DeVita is silent on
sequencing; management should be individualised, trial enrolment prioritised, and decisions made in
conjunction with genitourinary oncology given the absence of a robust dedicated evidence base (general
oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Role of surgery, radiotherapy, and systemic therapy

- Surgery is central and time-sensitive for the renal component, as set out above.
- Radiotherapy has no established role in HLRCC-associated RCC in the material grounded here; DeVita's
  HLRCC section does not describe one, and it should not be assumed to have a role in this histology
  without a specific indication such as symptomatic metastasis.
- Systemic therapy is reserved for advanced or metastatic disease, as above, with the caveat that the
  supporting evidence is thin and largely extrapolated from small studies and the shared HIF-driven
  biology of the tumour rather than from large randomised data.

## Monitoring

Because the associated renal cancer can metastasise while still small and carries a poor prognosis if
missed, carriers require ongoing renal imaging surveillance rather than a one-off assessment (general
oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease, but consistent with DeVita's statement
that early detection and treatment, rather than watchful waiting, is the goal for this tumour type).
Periodic dermatological and gynaecological review for leiomyoma symptoms should run alongside renal
surveillance.

## When to refer

- Any new or growing renal mass in a known or suspected FH-mutation carrier warrants prompt referral to
  urological oncology for consideration of early surgery, rather than referral into an active
  surveillance pathway.
- Clinical genetics referral is appropriate for germline FH testing and cascade testing of at-risk
  relatives once the diagnosis is suspected from the cutaneous, uterine, or renal phenotype.
- Metastatic disease should be referred to a centre able to offer trial enrolment or specialist
  genitourinary oncology input given the limited dedicated evidence base for this histology.

Sources: DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of Oncology, 12th ed.
