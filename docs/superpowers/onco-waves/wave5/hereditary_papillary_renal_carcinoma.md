# Hereditary papillary renal cell carcinoma (HPRC) - Management

## Overview grounding

Hereditary papillary renal carcinoma type 1 (HPRC) is an autosomal-dominant hereditary cancer syndrome caused by germline activating (missense) mutations in the tyrosine kinase domain of the MET proto-oncogene. Affected individuals develop multifocal, bilateral type 1 papillary kidney tumours, typically presenting in the sixth and seventh decades, with near-complete penetrance by age 80. It is a rare disorder reported in fewer than 35 kindreds worldwide. Papillary renal tumours in this syndrome are characterised by trisomy of chromosome 7 with non-random duplication of the chromosome carrying the mutant MET allele, thought to give a proliferative growth advantage that drives tumour progression.

DeVita's section on this disease is genetics- and pathology-focused; it does not lay out a disease-specific systemic-therapy algorithm for HPRC. The management approach below is therefore built from DeVita's general treatment-of-localized-RCC content in the same chapter (which explicitly names multifocal/familial RCC as an indication for nephron-sparing surgery) plus uncontroversial guideline-standard practice for hereditary papillary RCC; anything not tied to the DeVita text is labelled accordingly.

## Treatment approach by stage/intent

**Localized, multifocal small renal masses (the typical presentation).** Because HPRC produces numerous, usually low-grade, slow-growing type 1 papillary tumours in both kidneys, the guiding principle is to preserve as much functioning renal parenchyma as possible while controlling oncologic risk. DeVita's localized-RCC section states that multiple treatment strategies exist for small renal masses (radical nephrectomy, partial nephrectomy, thermal ablation, and active surveillance), that partial nephrectomy is now standard of care for small renal masses given the deleterious renal-functional consequences of radical nephrectomy, and it explicitly lists "multifocal or familial RCC" as a common indication favouring partial (nephron-sparing) nephrectomy over radical nephrectomy. Active surveillance with short-interval follow-up imaging is described in DeVita as reasonable for most small renal masses, particularly in poor surgical candidates or those with limited life expectancy, since most small renal masses have limited oncologic potential.

Applying this to HPRC (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease): a size-threshold, surveillance-and-treat strategy is typically used, imaging each tumour at intervals and reserving surgery for lesions that grow to a threshold size or show suspicious change, rather than treating every visible lesion, because patients accumulate multiple tumours over a lifetime and repeated nephrectomy risks progressive loss of renal function.

**When intervention is needed.** DeVita describes partial nephrectomy (open or minimally invasive) as achieving cancer-specific survival above 90% for T1 tumours and notes that even patients requiring multiple operations for multifocal, bilateral tumours can usually avoid permanent dialysis as long as at least roughly 30% of a well-functioning remnant kidney is preserved. Thermal ablation (cryoablation or radiofrequency ablation) is presented in DeVita as an alternative kidney-sparing option for small tumours (under about 3 cm), with a higher local recurrence/persistence rate than surgery but comparable outcomes to partial nephrectomy in at least one large series for very small (cT1a) lesions; this makes it a reasonable option for repeat, low-volume disease where preserving nephrons matters most. Radical nephrectomy is reserved, per DeVita's general criteria, for tumours not amenable to nephron-sparing surgery, and DeVita notes that when partial nephrectomy is not feasible, pre-surgical tyrosine-kinase-inhibitor therapy has in some cases been used to downstage tumour size and allow a nephron-sparing operation instead.

**Advanced, unresectable, or metastatic disease.** DeVita is silent on a dedicated HPRC treatment algorithm at this stage. Two points can be drawn responsibly from DeVita:
- MET is the driver oncogene in HPRC, and MET alterations are also found in a large proportion of sporadic type 1 papillary RCC, giving a strong mechanistic rationale for MET-directed therapy in MET-driven papillary disease (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease, though the MET biology itself is grounded in DeVita's genetics discussion).
- DeVita's pharmacology section on mTOR inhibitors notes that everolimus efficacy in RCC is maintained across histologies, with activity seen in non-clear-cell disease, but that its routine first-line use (alone or with bevacizumab) is not supported by trial data, and that in the current treatment landscape everolimus has been pushed to third-line and later settings behind agents such as cabozantinib and nivolumab-based regimens. This supports considering VEGF-pathway tyrosine kinase inhibitors or mTOR pathway inhibitors as systemic options for advanced non-clear-cell (including papillary) RCC, without a specific claim that any one regimen is uniquely validated for HPRC.
- Beyond this, choice among specific systemic agents, immune checkpoint inhibitors, or MET inhibitors for advanced HPRC should follow current NCCN kidney-cancer guidance and, where feasible, be routed through a clinical trial given the rarity of the disease; DeVita's disease-specific section does not itself specify a regimen.

## Lines of therapy

DeVita does not present a lines-of-therapy sequence specific to HPRC. General RCC-chapter content that can reasonably inform sequencing:
- Local/surgical therapy (nephron-sparing surgery or ablation) is the primary modality for localized, resectable disease at any point in the surveillance-and-treat cycle.
- For disease that becomes unresectable or metastatic, systemic therapy (VEGF-pathway or MET-pathway targeted agents, with mTOR inhibitors or immunotherapy-based options later in sequence) is the general oncology standard (not from DeVita's section on this disease) drawn from broader non-clear-cell RCC management, since DeVita's own text on everolimus in RCC places it in the third-line-and-beyond setting behind newer agents.
- Adjuvant systemic therapy after resection: DeVita's review of adjuvant trials in non-metastatic RCC (ASSURE, SORCE, ATLAS, PROTECT, ARISER) shows that adjuvant VEGF-TKI and monoclonal-antibody strategies have largely failed to demonstrate a consistent disease-free or overall-survival benefit, several trials enrolled both clear-cell and non-clear-cell histology, and toxicity limited use in some trials. DeVita also notes that adjuvant PD-1 blockade (pembrolizumab) improved relapse-free survival compared with placebo after nephrectomy in a phase III trial, but this evidence base is centred on clear-cell RCC; DeVita's text does not establish a role for adjuvant systemic therapy specifically in HPRC/papillary histology, so routine adjuvant treatment for resected HPRC is not currently supported by the source and should not be presented as standard.

## Role of surgery, radiotherapy, and systemic therapy

- **Surgery** is the backbone of curative-intent management: nephron-sparing partial nephrectomy (or enucleation) is preferred whenever technically feasible, given the multifocal/bilateral, familial nature of the disease and the cumulative renal-function cost of repeated radical nephrectomies. Lymphadenectomy is not routinely required for clinically localized disease; DeVita notes it is of limited (and controversial) benefit outside high-risk or clinically node-positive presentations.
- **Thermal ablation** is a kidney-sparing alternative for small, recurrent, or multiple lesions, particularly when repeat surgery would compromise renal function further, accepting a somewhat higher local recurrence rate than surgery.
- **Radiotherapy** has no defined curative role in DeVita's discussion of localized RCC management; DeVita mentions stereotactic body radiotherapy only as a palliative option for symptomatic, unresectable disease with major venous (IVC) involvement, which is a general RCC statement rather than HPRC-specific.
- **Systemic therapy** (targeted agents against MET or VEGF pathways, mTOR inhibitors, or immunotherapy) is reserved for advanced, unresectable, or metastatic disease, following general non-clear-cell RCC practice; DeVita does not establish a dedicated adjuvant or neoadjuvant systemic regimen for HPRC.

## Monitoring

Consistent with DeVita's general RCC follow-up framework, patients are followed with risk-stratified surveillance combining relevant history and physical examination, laboratory testing, and cross-sectional abdominal imaging (ultrasound, MRI, or CT) plus chest imaging (chest x-ray or CT), at intervals that intensify with higher clinicopathologic risk. For HPRC specifically, because new tumours can arise throughout life in both kidneys, DeVita's characterisation of the syndrome (multifocal, bilateral, near-complete penetrance by age 80) supports lifelong periodic renal imaging rather than time-limited post-treatment surveillance alone, tracking each lesion's size and growth rate against an intervention threshold, and monitoring renal function over time to guide how aggressively new lesions are treated.

## When to refer

Given the rarity of HPRC (fewer than 35 reported kindreds) and its multifocal, whole-life management burden, patients and at-risk relatives should be referred to (or co-managed with) urologic oncology with expertise in nephron-sparing surgery for hereditary kidney cancer syndromes, and to clinical genetics for germline MET testing, counselling on autosomal-dominant inheritance, and risk-based imaging of relatives. Referral to medical oncology is appropriate once disease becomes unresectable, high-grade, or metastatic, ideally with consideration of clinical trial enrollment given the rarity of the disease and the limited direct evidence base for systemic therapy specifically in HPRC.

## Deliberately omitted

No specific drug names, doses, or regimens are given for MET-targeted or VEGF-targeted therapy in HPRC because DeVita's disease-specific section does not name or validate a specific agent for this syndrome, and dosing is out of scope. Statistical outcome data for adjuvant trials, thrombus-related survival figures, and named agents (sunitinib, sorafenib, pazopanib, axitinib, girentuximab, nivolumab, pembrolizumab, everolimus, cabozantinib) are mentioned only descriptively where DeVita discusses them in a general RCC context, and not presented as HPRC-specific evidence.
