# Immature Teratoma of the Ovary — Management

## Overview and staging context

Immature teratoma is a malignant ovarian germ cell tumor, one of several subtypes
grouped together with dysgerminoma, yolk sac tumor, mixed germ cell tumor, embryonal
carcinoma, nongestational choriocarcinoma, and the rare polyembryoma. These tumors
account for roughly 5% of ovarian malignancies, present at a median age in the late
teens to early twenties, are almost always unilateral, and are described as
exquisitely chemosensitive. Because of this chemosensitivity and the young age of most
patients, fertility-preserving surgery is described as an option for this disease
group even when metastatic disease is present. Grading of the immature teratoma is
based on the amount of immature neuroepithelial tissue (the tissue forms
neuroepithelial rosettes), using a grading scheme (Norris) that determines how
aggressively the tumor is treated. The tumor can also produce alpha-fetoprotein and
human chorionic gonadotropin, so both markers are relevant to diagnosis and follow-up.

## Surgical approach

Initial management is surgical: unilateral removal of the involved ovary and tube
with surgical staging, preserving the uterus and the contralateral ovary whenever
technically feasible, since fertility preservation is a stated priority in this
young population and the disease is almost always confined to one ovary. This
fertility-sparing approach is presented as appropriate even in the presence of
metastatic spread, reflecting the chemosensitivity of the tumor class as a whole.

## Adjuvant systemic therapy by stage and grade

- Surgical stage IA, grade 1 or 2 immature teratoma can be observed after surgery
  without adjuvant chemotherapy; this stage/grade combination is explicitly carved
  out as an exception to routine adjuvant treatment.
- All other stages and grades of malignant ovarian germ cell tumor, including higher
  grade or more advanced immature teratoma, should receive adjuvant treatment with a
  bleomycin-etoposide-cisplatin combination (a platinum-based regimen), a treatment
  approach that mirrors and was extrapolated from the testicular germ cell tumor
  experience.
- (General oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease) Peritoneal
  or nodal implants should be assessed histologically for grade, since low-grade
  (mature) implants behave differently from immature ones and this can influence the
  decision to treat.
- (General oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease) Growing
  teratoma syndrome, enlargement of a residual mass during or after chemotherapy with
  normalizing tumor markers, is managed with surgical excision of the residual mass
  rather than escalation or continuation of chemotherapy; this specific syndrome is
  discussed elsewhere in the source in the context of testicular germ cell tumors and
  is not spelled out in the ovarian section, but the underlying biology (residual
  teratomatous tissue maturing rather than responding to cytotoxic therapy) is the same
  process.

## Monitoring

Follow-up relies on the same tumor markers used at diagnosis, alpha-fetoprotein and
human chorionic gonadotropin, tracked serially to detect recurrence or persistent
disease. Prognosis for malignant ovarian germ cell tumors as a class is described as
excellent, with return of normal menstrual function typical and numerous successful
pregnancies documented among survivors, supporting the fertility-sparing strategy.

## When to refer

Because of the rarity of this tumor, the need for accurate histologic grading of both
the primary tumor and any peritoneal or nodal implants, and the importance of getting
the fertility-sparing staging operation right the first time, care should be
concentrated with a gynecologic oncology team experienced in germ cell tumors, with
fertility counseling built into the initial treatment discussion. (General oncology
standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease.)
