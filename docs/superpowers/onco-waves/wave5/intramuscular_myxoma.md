# Intramuscular Myxoma — Management

## What DeVita's section on this tumor actually covers
DeVita's discussion of intramuscular myxoma (in the "Tumors of Uncertain Differentiation" chapter, alongside deep angiomyxoma) is diagnostic rather than therapeutic. It describes the tumor as a rare lesion of adults occurring in the large extremity muscles, composed of spindle cells without nuclear atypia in an abundant myxoid stroma, not highly vascular and not enhancing on MRI, with a cellular myxoma variant that can mimic myxofibrosarcoma on biopsy. It notes that GNAS1 mutations, common in spontaneous myxoma but not in myxofibrosarcoma, are a useful diagnostic aid, and that multiple intramuscular myxomas occur in association with fibrous dysplasia as Mazabraud syndrome. DeVita's text does not state a specific treatment approach, line of therapy, or monitoring schedule for intramuscular myxoma itself.

Because DeVita is silent on management for this specific tumor, everything below on treatment, monitoring, and referral is marked as general oncology standard rather than DeVita-sourced, and should be treated as needing manual sourcing confirmation against NCCN soft tissue sarcoma guidance or a dedicated soft tissue pathology text.

## Diagnostic workup before any treatment decision
- MRI characterization of the mass; DeVita notes it is typically non-enhancing and not highly vascular, with myxoid signal similar to other myxoid neoplasms.
- Image-guided core biopsy when imaging is not clearly typical, specifically to exclude a myxofibrosarcoma or other myxoid sarcoma, since the cellular myxoma variant can resemble these on biopsy (DeVita).
- GNAS1 mutation testing as a diagnostic aid favoring a benign myxoma over myxofibrosarcoma when histology is ambiguous (DeVita).
- Assessment for coexisting fibrous dysplasia (Mazabraud syndrome) when multiple myxomas are found (DeVita).

## Treatment approach
Intramuscular myxoma is a benign, non-metastasizing soft tissue tumor. (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease)

- **Definitive treatment intent (the norm):** marginal surgical excision is the standard approach and is generally curative, with local recurrence being uncommon. (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease)
- **Observation:** for a lesion with imaging and, where obtained, biopsy findings that are unequivocally consistent with a benign myxoma and that is asymptomatic, active surveillance rather than immediate excision can be a reasonable alternative, particularly if the mass is small and not causing functional impairment. (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease)
- **Lines of therapy:** there is no role for systemic therapy, chemotherapy, or radiotherapy in intramuscular myxoma; these are treatments for its malignant mimics (myxofibrosarcoma, myxoid liposarcoma), not for the benign myxoma itself. (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease)
- **Cellular myxoma variant:** because this variant is more cellular and can be histologically difficult to distinguish from myxofibrosarcoma, management should follow confirmation of a benign diagnosis (imaging pattern, histology, and GNAS1 status as DeVita describes); if the distinction cannot be made confidently, the lesion should be handled with the same excision and margin discipline used for a low-grade soft tissue sarcoma until pathology is definitive. (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease)
- **Mazabraud syndrome:** when intramuscular myxoma coexists with fibrous dysplasia, the myxoma itself is still managed as above; the associated bony fibrous dysplasia is managed on its own merits (symptom control, monitoring for fracture risk or deformity) rather than as an oncologic problem. (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease)

## Role of surgery, radiotherapy, and systemic therapy
- Surgery (marginal excision) is the only treatment modality with an established role; it is both diagnostic (confirms benignity on final pathology) and therapeutic. (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease)
- Radiotherapy has no established role in intramuscular myxoma and would only be considered if the final pathology instead demonstrates a myxoid sarcoma, at which point the case is managed under soft tissue sarcoma pathways rather than as a myxoma. (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease)
- Systemic therapy has no role in intramuscular myxoma. (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease)

## Monitoring
- After excision, periodic clinical follow-up (with imaging if a new mass or symptom develops) is reasonable to confirm resolution and detect the rare local recurrence; there is no evidence basis for intensive surveillance imaging given the benign, non-metastasizing nature of the tumor. (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease)
- If observation rather than excision is chosen, interval clinical and imaging follow-up should confirm stability and watch for any change in size, vascularity, or cellularity that would raise concern for a myxoid sarcoma and prompt biopsy or excision. (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease)
- In Mazabraud syndrome, ongoing surveillance of the fibrous dysplasia component (for fracture risk, deformity, or, rarely, malignant transformation of fibrous dysplasia) is separate from myxoma follow-up. (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease)

## When to refer
- Refer to an orthopaedic oncology or soft tissue tumor service for any deep intramuscular mass being considered for biopsy or excision, given the histologic overlap between myxoma and myxofibrosarcoma that DeVita highlights.
- Refer to pathology with soft tissue tumor expertise (and consider GNAS1 testing) when biopsy shows a cellular, myxoid lesion that cannot be confidently distinguished from myxofibrosarcoma on routine assessment (DeVita).
- Refer for genetics or endocrinology input when Mazabraud syndrome is suspected, to characterize and manage the fibrous dysplasia component. (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease)

Sources: DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of Oncology, 12th ed.
