# Leptomeningeal metastasis — management

Leptomeningeal metastasis (also called leptomeningeal carcinomatosis, neoplastic meningitis, or
carcinomatous/leukemic meningitis) is tumor spread within the leptomeninges and subarachnoid
space, seen with advanced solid tumors and with leukemia or lymphoma. Treatment intent is mostly
palliative, aimed at neurologic function and symptom control, since the disease is almost always
a marker of advanced, progressive systemic cancer.

## Overall approach and intent

Because leptomeningeal disease occurs in the setting of advanced disease and survival is
typically limited to a few months (with somewhat longer survival reported for breast and lung
cancer patients than for melanoma), the goal of therapy is disease control and symptom
palliation rather than cure. A confirmed diagnosis (positive CSF cytology or histology) carries a
better reported outcome than a "possible" diagnosis made on clinical or radiologic grounds alone,
which is itself a marker of more advanced disease at presentation. Decisions on how aggressively
to treat should factor in the patient's neurologic function, the availability of active systemic
therapy for the underlying primary, and overall performance status; early involvement of
palliative care is appropriate given the prognosis.

## Diagnosis drives treatment selection

- Diagnosis rests on a combination of neurologic examination, contrast-enhanced MRI of the brain
  (and spine if there are spinal signs or symptoms), and CSF examination.
- CSF cytology is the sole diagnostic criterion in roughly half of confirmed cases, but a single
  sample is insensitive (40 to 50 percent false-negative), so up to two repeat CSF examinations
  are recommended if the first is negative.
- MRI findings are classified as linear, nodular, both, or neither (no visible disease other than
  hydrocephalus), and this pattern (together with the CSF result) feeds into whether disease is
  confirmed, probable, or possible.
- If hydrocephalus develops from obstruction of CSF pathways, an external ventricular drain or
  ventriculoperitoneal shunt can relieve symptoms and should be considered before other CNS-directed
  treatment.

## Radiation therapy

- Radiation therapy is used for palliation of symptomatic or bulky sites, including cranial
  neuropathies and cauda equina syndrome.
- Whole-brain radiotherapy should be considered for extensive nodular disease, linear disease, or
  when brain metastases coexist.
- Involved-field radiotherapy is often combined with intrathecal chemotherapy, as the two are
  considered complementary; radiotherapy is generally given before intra-CSF therapy because of
  older data suggesting a neurotoxicity concern with the reverse sequence.
- Craniospinal irradiation was historically avoided because of bone-marrow suppression and other
  toxicity, but this is being reconsidered, and reports (including with proton craniospinal
  irradiation) suggest meaningful CNS disease control can be achieved with acceptable toxicity in
  selected patients.

## Intra-CSF (intrathecal) chemotherapy

- Because of the blood-brain barrier, chemotherapy delivered directly into the CSF space is the
  standard way to treat leptomeningeal disease that lacks bulky tumor.
- Delivery can be via an implanted intraventricular reservoir and catheter (an Ommaya-type device)
  or via lumbar puncture; an intraventricular device is preferred where CSF flow is not
  obstructed, since bulky meningeal disease impairing CSF flow limits this approach.
- Agent classes used intrathecally include antifolate chemotherapy (methotrexate), antimetabolite
  chemotherapy (cytarabine, including a liposomal formulation), and alkylating chemotherapy
  (thiotepa).
- For specific molecular subsets, intrathecal delivery of an antibody targeting HER2 (in
  HER2-positive breast cancer leptomeningeal disease) and of an antifolate agent for
  EGFR-mutant disease have been studied and shown activity, though the evidence for these is
  limited to early-phase trials.
- Intrathecal chemotherapy following cranial irradiation carries a risk of treatment-related
  leukoencephalopathy, which should be watched for.

## Systemic therapy

- No randomized trial has established a preferred systemic regimen for leptomeningeal disease, so
  the choice of systemic therapy is guided by the primary tumor type, prior treatment, and the
  tumor's molecular characteristics.
- In EGFR-mutant non-small-cell lung cancer, CNS-penetrant EGFR-targeted tyrosine kinase inhibitor
  therapy (including agents active against resistance mutations) has shown encouraging activity in
  this setting.
- Targeted tyrosine kinase inhibitors more broadly can have useful activity against
  leptomeningeal disease when a driver mutation relevant to the primary cancer is present and a
  CNS-penetrant agent in that class is available (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's
  section on this disease).
- Investigational drug-conjugate approaches for breast cancer leptomeningeal disease have shown
  intracranial disease control in early studies, but these remain investigational rather than
  standard of care.
- Immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy (anti-CTLA-4 and anti-PD-1 antibodies, alone or combined) has
  shown intracranial activity in metastatic melanoma and non-small-cell lung cancer with brain
  involvement, with better responses in patients who are not on corticosteroids, though this
  evidence base is drawn from parenchymal brain metastases rather than leptomeningeal disease
  specifically (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Surgery

DeVita's section on leptomeningeal metastasis does not describe a role for surgical resection of
leptomeningeal disease itself; surgical intervention in this setting is limited to CSF-diversion
procedures (external ventricular drain or ventriculoperitoneal shunt) for symptomatic
hydrocephalus, and to placement of an intraventricular access device for intrathecal therapy.

## Supportive and symptomatic care

Corticosteroids and management of raised intracranial pressure, seizure control, and standard
symptom palliation (analgesia, antiemetics) remain part of routine supportive care for patients
with CNS metastatic disease and should be applied here as clinically indicated (general oncology
standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Monitoring

- Because the value of repeat CSF cytology for monitoring is controversial, response is mainly
  followed by MRI, performed roughly every two months for the first six months and then roughly
  every three months if disease is stable.
- A standardized RANO (Response Assessment in Neuro-Oncology) framework for leptomeningeal disease
  combines a structured neurologic examination, CSF cytology or flow cytometry, and radiographic
  grading (stable, progressive, or improved), since most leptomeningeal lesions are not
  measurable in the way parenchymal tumors are. Interobserver agreement on this grading is
  imperfect, particularly for linear leptomeningeal and cranial nerve enhancement, so criteria
  continue to be revised.

## When to refer

Given the complexity of diagnosis (repeat CSF sampling, whole-neuroaxis imaging), the range of
CNS-directed treatment options (radiotherapy, intraventricular device placement, intrathecal
chemotherapy), and the need to coordinate with ongoing systemic therapy for the primary cancer,
patients with suspected or confirmed leptomeningeal metastasis should be referred early to a
neuro-oncology multidisciplinary team, with neurosurgical input if hydrocephalus or an
intraventricular device is being considered, and with early palliative care involvement given the
generally limited prognosis.

Sources: DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of Oncology, 12th ed.
