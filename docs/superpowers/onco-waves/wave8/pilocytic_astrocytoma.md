# Pilocytic astrocytoma (juvenile pilocytic astrocytoma, JPA) - Management

## Overview and treatment intent
Pilocytic astrocytoma is a circumscribed, low-grade glioma that occurs mainly in the first two decades of life, most often in the cerebellum, with a smaller share involving the optic pathway, hypothalamus, or brain stem. Cerebellar pilocytic astrocytomas made up roughly a quarter of posterior fossa tumours in a large paediatric series, and the great majority of these were low grade. The disease is driven in most cases by a KIAA1549-BRAF fusion that activates the BRAF oncogene. Overall intent is curative for accessible, resectable disease; for unresectable or midline (optic pathway, hypothalamic, brain stem) disease, the intent shifts to durable disease control while protecting vision, endocrine function, and the developing brain.

## Surgery: the mainstay for accessible tumours
- Gross total resection (GTR) is described as tantamount to a cure for cerebellar pilocytic astrocytoma.
- When resection is incomplete, conservative monitoring of the residual tumour is a reasonable strategy, because most remnants do not grow, remain low grade, and can still be removed later if they progress.
- Surgery plays a much smaller role for tumours in less accessible locations. For optic pathway gliomas, surgery is only rarely indicated; unilateral anterior tumours not involving the chiasm may be resectable, but resection of the chiasm itself is avoided because it causes bilateral blindness. Posterior optic pathway or hypothalamic lesions may be biopsied or subtotally resected if symptomatic from mass effect.
- For brain stem disease, dorsally exophytic or cervicomedullary lesions are often partially resectable with good long-term results, whereas diffuse intrinsic lesions are not amenable to complete resection.
- Obstructive hydrocephalus, which can accompany posterior fossa or midline tumours, is managed with cerebrospinal fluid diversion (endoscopic third ventriculostomy or shunting) and may be needed even after the tumour itself has been treated.

## Role of radiotherapy
- Nearly all completely resected cerebellar pilocytic astrocytomas do not require radiotherapy, and even with progression, repeat resection is often preferred over radiotherapy if further debulking is feasible.
- Radiotherapy is reserved for children with multiple recurrences, or for residual disease in a location where further progression would carry serious clinical consequences.
- For optic pathway/hypothalamic gliomas, definitive radiotherapy is typically deferred in younger children because chemotherapy is preferred as first-line treatment, partly to avoid radiation-associated vasculopathy (radiation-induced moyamoya syndrome) and secondary malignancy, risks that are of particular concern in neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1). Radiotherapy remains an important definitive option for local control and vision preservation in well-selected older or sporadic cases.
- Adult pilocytic astrocytoma is rare; outcomes after resection are generally excellent, and radiotherapy is reasonably reserved for unresectable disease or salvage treatment rather than used routinely.

## Systemic and targeted therapy
- For cerebellar pilocytic astrocytoma specifically, chemotherapy is generally not indicated once the tumour is resected or under surveillance.
- Chemotherapy has an established role for progressive or unresectable disease along the optic pathway/hypothalamic axis, particularly in young children, where it is used as initial therapy to delay or avoid irradiation. A platinum agent combined with a vinca alkaloid is described as the most common first-line approach in this setting; alkylator-based regimens are generally avoided in NF1 patients because of second-malignancy risk.
- Some pilocytic tumours harbour targetable driver mutations (BRAF fusion or BRAF V600E), which raises the possibility of a targeted agent as a first-line option in selected cases (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease, for the specific class of agent: MAPK-pathway-targeted drugs such as MEK inhibitors and BRAF inhibitors are used in this context, though DeVita's cerebellar/optic pathway discussion of pilocytic astrocytoma itself only notes the presence of a targetable fusion without naming a drug class).

## Monitoring and long-term follow-up
- After GTR, routine surveillance imaging is appropriate given the possibility, if rare, of an incompletely excised or recurrent tumour; incomplete resections are followed conservatively with serial imaging since most remnants remain indolent.
- Adult patients in a reported cohort had 5-year overall survival and progression-free survival around 85% and 70% respectively, with older age and higher body mass index associated with worse progression-free and overall survival, findings that support continued long-term follow-up rather than a fixed short surveillance window.
- Optic pathway tumours require ongoing formal ophthalmological and visual field assessment, and hypothalamic/chiasmatic tumours warrant endocrine follow-up (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease, for the specific ophthalmology/endocrine surveillance recommendation, though consistent with the pathway involvement DeVita describes).
- Patients with NF1 and an optic pathway tumour should remain under a combined neuro-oncology/genetics surveillance plan given the different natural history (more indolent course, higher spontaneous regression rate) compared with sporadic tumours.

## When to refer
- Any new diagnosis of pilocytic astrocytoma, at any site, warrants referral to a neuro-oncology or paediatric neuro-oncology multidisciplinary team for surgical planning and long-term management, since extent of resection is the single strongest determinant of outcome.
- Refer promptly for evidence of raised intracranial pressure or hydrocephalus, progressive visual loss in an optic pathway lesion, or new focal neurological deficit, all of which need urgent neurosurgical assessment.
- Refer for genetics/NF1 evaluation when an optic pathway tumour is identified, since a substantial proportion arise in the context of NF1 and this changes both treatment threshold and long-term surveillance strategy.
- Adult patients, in whom this tumour is rare, should still be managed through a neuro-oncology service experienced with low-grade glioma, since published data in adults are comparatively limited.

Sources: DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of Oncology, 12th ed.
