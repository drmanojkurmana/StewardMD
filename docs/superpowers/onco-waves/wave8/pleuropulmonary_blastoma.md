# Pleuropulmonary blastoma (PPB) — management

Note on sourcing: the DeVita text made available for this task lists "Pleuropulmonary blastoma" only
as a single line item in the WHO thoracic tumour classification table, and its only other nearby
hit ("pulmonary blastoma") describes a different, unrelated adult biphasic lung tumour with
TP53/DICER1/CTNNB1 mutations, not pediatric PPB. DeVita's Cancer: Principles and Practice of
Oncology, in the section actually located for this disease, contains no substantive management
narrative for pleuropulmonary blastoma. Every clinical claim below is therefore marked as general
oncology/pediatric-oncology standard rather than DeVita-sourced, and this file should be flagged
for manual sourcing against a pediatric oncology text (for example Nelson Textbook of Pediatrics or
a COG/International PPB Registry protocol) before it is treated as fully grounded.

## Overview

Pleuropulmonary blastoma is a rare dysontogenetic tumour of the lung and pleura occurring almost
exclusively in young children, most often linked to a germline DICER1 pathogenic variant (general
oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease). It is staged pathologically by type
rather than by a formal TNM system, and treatment intent and intensity are driven primarily by that
type.

## Treatment approach by type

- **Type I (purely cystic):** complete surgical resection is the primary and often sole treatment;
  because a Type I lesion can harbour an occult malignant cambium layer and can progress to Type II
  or III if left in place, resection is advised even when imaging looks benign (general oncology
  standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- **Type Ir (regressed cystic):** a recognised subtype in the PPB classification in which cystic
  disease has spontaneously regressed to a fibrous remnant; surveillance imaging is used in this
  setting rather than further resection once the diagnosis is secure (general oncology standard, not
  from DeVita's section on this disease).
- **Type II (mixed cystic and solid) and Type III (purely solid, high-grade sarcomatous):** treated
  as high-grade pediatric sarcomas, combining surgery with systemic chemotherapy, and radiotherapy in
  selected cases (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Lines of therapy and role of surgery, radiotherapy and systemic therapy

- **Surgery** is the cornerstone of treatment at every stage: complete resection of the primary
  tumour is pursued whenever feasible, including re-resection of residual or recurrent disease and
  resection of accessible metastatic deposits (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section
  on this disease).
- **Systemic therapy** for Type II and III disease follows a pediatric soft-tissue sarcoma type
  chemotherapy backbone, typically combining an alkylating agent, an anthracycline-based component,
  and a vinca alkaloid/actinomycin-based component, given as adjuvant treatment after resection or as
  neoadjuvant treatment to downsize unresectable disease (general oncology standard, not from
  DeVita's section on this disease). Doses and schedules are not detailed here and belong in a
  separate structured protocol reference.
- **Radiotherapy** has a selective role, considered for unresectable primary disease, gross residual
  disease after surgery, or symptomatic metastatic sites, balanced against the long-term risks of
  radiation in a young child (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- **Recurrent or refractory disease** is managed on a case-by-case basis with further surgery,
  alternative or salvage chemotherapy regimens, and consideration of clinical trial enrolment, given
  the rarity of the disease and the reliance on registry-based rather than large randomised evidence
  (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## DICER1 testing and family surveillance

- Germline DICER1 testing is recommended for all patients with PPB, given the high proportion of
  cases arising in DICER1 syndrome, with genetic counselling and cascade testing offered to
  first-degree relatives when a pathogenic variant is confirmed (general oncology standard, not from
  DeVita's section on this disease).
- Patients and DICER1 carrier relatives should enter a surveillance programme for the other
  DICER1-associated tumours, including cystic nephroma, Sertoli-Leydig cell tumour, multinodular
  goitre and thyroid carcinoma, ciliary body medulloepithelioma, and nasal chondromesenchymal
  hamartoma (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Monitoring

- Surveillance chest imaging is used to detect local recurrence after resection, at a frequency and
  duration that should be individualised to tumour type and resection margins (general oncology
  standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Because solid-type disease metastasises preferentially to brain and bone, periodic brain MRI and
  skeletal imaging are used both at diagnosis and during follow-up for Type II/III disease (general
  oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Longer-term follow-up should track for other DICER1-related tumours in DICER1 carriers, and for
  late effects of chemotherapy and radiotherapy in survivors (general oncology standard, not from
  DeVita's section on this disease).

## When to refer

- Any child with a complex or enlarging cystic lung lesion, or one with solid components, should be
  referred promptly for pediatric surgical and pediatric oncology assessment rather than managed as a
  presumed benign congenital pulmonary airway malformation, because delayed resection of a Type I
  lesion risks progression to a higher-grade tumour (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's
  section on this disease).
- Confirmed or suspected PPB should be referred to a specialist pediatric oncology multidisciplinary
  team, ideally one with access to a DICER1/PPB registry, for staging, systemic therapy planning, and
  genetic counselling (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- A family history of DICER1-associated tumours, or neurological signs suggesting cerebral
  metastasis, should prompt urgent referral rather than routine follow-up (general oncology standard,
  not from DeVita's section on this disease).
