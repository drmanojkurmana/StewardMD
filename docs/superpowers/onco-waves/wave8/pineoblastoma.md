# Pineoblastoma - Management Narrative

Pineoblastoma is the highest-grade pineal parenchymal tumour, classified alongside medulloblastoma and other embryonal CNS tumours as a variant of primitive neuroectodermal tumour (PNET). It arises from pineal parenchymal cells, is most common in children, and carries a strong tendency toward cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) dissemination, which drives the need for whole-neuraxis staging and, in most eligible patients, craniospinal treatment fields.

## Diagnostic and staging groundwork before treatment

Because germinomas and other pineal region tumours can mimic pineoblastoma clinically, DeVita's pineal region tumours section is explicit that determining histology, tumour markers, and extent of disease is critical before a treatment plan is chosen. In practice this means:

- Serum and CSF germ cell tumour markers (alpha-fetoprotein, beta-hCG) to exclude germinoma and non-germinomatous germ cell tumours, which are managed differently from pineoblastoma.
- Imaging of the neuraxis (brain plus spine) before surgery, given the recognised potential for leptomeningeal spread.
- CSF cytology to formally document dissemination status.
- In children, evaluation for germline RB1 (trilateral retinoblastoma) and DICER1 predisposition syndromes, with genetic counselling, is standard practice in paediatric neuro-oncology (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Role of surgery

DeVita notes that pineal region tumours are among the most difficult brain tumours to remove because of their central location. Resection is described as important when a tumour is radioresistant or when excision may be curative, giving pineal parenchymal tumours (the category that includes pineoblastoma) as an example where resection matters, in contrast to germinoma, where biopsy alone is sufficient because germinoma responds well to radiation. For pineoblastoma specifically, the treatment aim is maximal safe resection for cytoreduction and to secure tissue for histological and molecular diagnosis, rather than resection at any cost, given the difficulty and vascular risk of pineal region surgery. Endoscopic biopsy combined with third ventriculostomy is a described option when hydrocephalus is also present, allowing CSF diversion, tissue diagnosis, and CSF cytology and marker sampling in a single procedure.

## Role of radiotherapy

Radiotherapy has an established role in curative treatment of pineal parenchymal and germ cell tumours, per DeVita's discussion of pineal region tumours generally. Modern conformal techniques (VMAT, IMRT, proton beam) are used to reduce long-term complications such as neurocognitive dysfunction and second malignancy risk. For pineoblastoma, DeVita's discussion of a high-dose chemotherapy series describes treatment built around craniospinal irradiation (CSI) with an additional boost to the pineal/primary site, reflecting the tumour's propensity for CSF dissemination. In infants, radiotherapy is deferred where possible because of neurotoxicity, with chemotherapy used instead, a point DeVita records explicitly for pineoblastoma. Endocrine deficiencies (diabetes insipidus, hypothyroidism, hypogonadism) occur in roughly half of pineal tumour patients overall; DeVita attributes these mainly to the tumour itself rather than to treatment, though surgery and radiation near the hypothalamic-pituitary axis can also contribute (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease), and warrant endocrine monitoring.

## Role of systemic therapy

The clearest pineoblastoma-specific evidence in DeVita's text is a small series in which patients received induction chemotherapy, then craniospinal irradiation with a boost to the primary site, followed by high-dose chemotherapy with autologous stem-cell rescue. In that cohort, most patients remained disease free, including two infants who never received radiotherapy at all, with reported 4-year progression-free and overall survival rates in the high-60s to low-70s percent range. DeVita frames this approach as still investigational despite favourable results, and notes that high-dose chemotherapy with stem-cell support has been less successful in the recurrent-disease setting, though data there are limited. Beyond this specific regimen, DeVita's broader chemotherapy discussion for pineal tumours centres on platinum-based and alkylating-agent combinations (used for germ cell tumours), which is not stated as the standard backbone for pineoblastoma itself; extrapolating a platinum/alkylator-based systemic approach for pineoblastoma outside of the high-dose consolidation strategy above is general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease.

## Suggested treatment approach by clinical scenario

- **Newly diagnosed, older child, localised disease:** maximal safe resection, followed by craniospinal radiotherapy with a boost to the primary site plus systemic chemotherapy; intensified strategies incorporating high-dose chemotherapy with stem-cell rescue have shown encouraging but still investigational results in this group.
- **Infants and very young children:** chemotherapy-based approach with radiotherapy deferred or omitted where feasible to avoid neurotoxicity, as reflected in the infants who avoided radiotherapy in the DeVita-cited series; this is a higher-risk group overall.
- **Metastatic or disseminated disease at diagnosis:** treatment intent still centres on craniospinal irradiation and systemic therapy, since dissemination is common in this tumour, though DeVita's pineoblastoma-specific data do not give a separate metastatic-disease regimen; the general principle that disseminated embryonal CNS tumours require craniospinal fields is a general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease.
- **Recurrent disease:** DeVita notes that high-dose chemotherapy with stem-cell support has not performed as well after relapse, and treatment of recurrent pineoblastoma should be individualised through a paediatric neuro-oncology multidisciplinary team rather than following a single default pathway.

## Monitoring and follow-up

Longitudinal surveillance imaging of the brain and spine is needed given the risk of late relapse and dissemination, and monitoring for treatment-related endocrine, neurocognitive, and growth effects is warranted given the radiotherapy and chemotherapy exposure this tumour requires (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease, though consistent with the endocrine-deficiency point DeVita makes for pineal tumours generally).

## When to refer

Any suspected pineal region embryonal tumour should be referred promptly to a paediatric neuro-oncology multidisciplinary team (neurosurgery, radiation oncology, paediatric oncology, neuro-radiology, and, where relevant, clinical genetics) because of the complexity of pineal region surgery, the need for craniospinal treatment planning, and the potential need for genetic counselling in RB1- or DICER1-associated cases. Acute obstructive hydrocephalus or rapidly progressive Parinaud syndrome warrants urgent neurosurgical referral for CSF diversion ahead of definitive oncological treatment.

Sources: DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of Oncology, 12th ed.
