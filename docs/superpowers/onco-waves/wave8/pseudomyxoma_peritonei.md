# Pseudomyxoma peritonei (jelly belly, gelatinous ascites, mucinous ascites of the peritoneum) - Management

## Overview and staging logic

Pseudomyxoma peritonei (PMP) is not managed by TNM stage in the way most solid tumors are. Treatment selection is driven instead by three quantitative factors DeVita's chapter on peritoneal metastases identifies as the core prognostic indicators for this disease: the histologic grade of the mucinous neoplasm (low-grade appendiceal mucinous neoplasm, an intermediate type, versus frank mucinous appendiceal carcinoma), the peritoneal cancer index (a composite score of lesion size across abdominopelvic regions describing how much of the peritoneum is involved), and whether a subsequent cytoreductive operation can achieve a complete or near-complete removal of visible disease (the completeness-of-cytoreduction score). DeVita reports that these three histologic subtypes carry markedly different long-term survival even when all are treated with the same cytoreductive surgery plus perioperative chemotherapy strategy, so grade is used up front to set expectations and to decide whether aggressive surgery is worthwhile at all.

## Primary (resectable) disease: cytoreductive surgery plus perioperative intraperitoneal chemotherapy

For patients whose disease is judged resectable, the standard of care described in DeVita is complete cytoreductive surgery (CRS) followed by perioperative intraperitoneal chemotherapy, aiming to leave no visible residual disease. Points grounded directly in the text:

- PMP almost always originates from a perforated low-grade appendiceal mucinous neoplasm; the appendix ruptures and mucin-producing epithelium seeds the peritoneum, so appendicectomy (or right hemicolectomy when the primary is more advanced) is part of the operation.
- Because mucin redistributes to sites of peritoneal fluid resorption (the undersurface of the right hemidiaphragm), sites with lymphatic tissue (the omentum and omental appendages), and dependent sites (the pelvis), while relatively sparing the small bowel, the operation is built around parietal peritonectomies, omentectomy, and resection of the visceral structures fixed to the retroperitoneum (the gastric antrum, ileocolic region, and the rectosigmoid junction) rather than a generic "debulking."
- The heated intraperitoneal chemotherapy delivered at the time of surgery (HIPEC) is intended to eradicate free-floating cancer cells and minute residual nodules not yet trapped in scar tissue or vascularized; DeVita notes the drug used for HIPEC needs a cell-cycle-nonspecific mechanism with a heat-synergistic effect, and describes both the open ("Coliseum") technique developed by Sugarbaker specifically for pseudomyxoma peritonei and closed techniques that maintain hyperthermia more efficiently. An agent from the same broad class described for HIPEC in colorectal peritoneal disease (an alkylating antitumor antibiotic) is the one most consistently used for this indication in the chapter; exact agent selection and any dosing sit in the structured protocol templates, not here.
- DeVita is explicit that, despite decades of work, optimal HIPEC/EPIC (early postoperative intraperitoneal chemotherapy) regimens have not been formally established for any of these peritoneal-surface diseases, and that randomized trials comparing HIPEC, EPIC, and normothermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy strategies are still lacking.
- Outcome after CRS and perioperative chemotherapy is described as "extremely variable" and heavily dependent on the histologic subtype, the peritoneal cancer index, and the completeness-of-cytoreduction score achieved, underscoring that patient selection by an experienced multidisciplinary team matters as much as the operation itself.
- A prior surgical score (quantifying how much abdominal surgery has already occurred, which predicts adhesions and tumor entrapment) is specifically flagged in DeVita as having a detrimental effect on outcome that is "especially obvious" in low-grade appendiceal mucinous neoplasm, which is the direct justification for referring such patients to a specialist center for definitive surgery before repeated partial operations elsewhere.
- DeVita reports a striking historical shift for this disease: unresectable/untreated pseudomyxoma peritonei was essentially uniformly fatal by ten years, whereas the CRS-plus-perioperative-chemotherapy strategy has produced reported survival out to 85% at twenty years in favorable (low-grade, completely resected) disease. This wide range again reflects the strong dependence on grade and completeness of resection rather than a single expected outcome for all patients.
- DeVita also notes there is a documented surgical learning curve for these cytoreductive operations, with outcomes improving over roughly the first 100-plus procedures at a center, which is part of why this operation is concentrated in specialist peritoneal malignancy units rather than performed broadly (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease: national and international guidelines formally recommend referral to a high-volume center for this reason).

## Unresectable or high-grade disease

DeVita distinguishes low-grade disease (disseminated peritoneal adenomucinosis) from high-grade mucinous carcinomatosis, and states that grade strongly influences whether cytoreduction and perioperative chemotherapy remain a valid option. For patients whose disease is too extensive (particularly with diffuse small-bowel or mesenteric involvement) to achieve a complete cytoreduction, or whose histology is high-grade:

- These patients are described in DeVita as having no systemic therapy options specific to this indication, and as often succumbing to bowel obstruction driven by ongoing mucin oversecretion and rising intra-abdominal pressure.
- Palliative laparoscopic evacuation of the mucinous ascites is described as a low-morbidity option for symptom control in unresectable PMP; DeVita reports that in a small single-center series, evacuation gave a median symptom-free interval measured in months before recurrent symptoms required a repeat procedure, without requiring a hospital stay. Standard needle paracentesis is noted to be largely ineffective because of the high viscosity of the mucinous ascites.
- Pressurized intraperitoneal aerosol chemotherapy (PIPAC), delivered laparoscopically, is described elsewhere in DeVita as an option for unresectable peritoneal carcinomatosis broadly (studied mainly in colorectal and gastric peritoneal disease), with response and durability still under study; DeVita's text on PMP specifically discusses laparoscopic evacuation rather than PIPAC as the described palliative option for this disease, so PIPAC's role in PMP itself should be considered general peritoneal-malignancy extrapolation (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- For high-grade mucinous carcinomatosis or truly unresectable disease, systemic chemotherapy is used, following the same fluoropyrimidine/platinum-based systemic approaches used more broadly in gastrointestinal mucinous adenocarcinomas (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease, though DeVita's broader peritoneal-metastases chapter does describe systemic chemotherapy as part of the combined strategy for peritoneal disease overall). Response to systemic therapy in low-grade PMP specifically is limited because of its indolent, low-proliferative biology, which is why surgery and regional treatment dominate the low-grade paradigm.

## Recurrence

DeVita highlights that response to repeat treatment after recurrence is itself a distinguishing feature of appendiceal peritoneal metastases: unlike many other peritoneal-surface cancers, patients with appendiceal/PMP disease who recur after a first CRS and perioperative chemotherapy can still respond well to a second cytoreductive operation, again governed by the same selection factors (grade, extent of disease, and feasibility of a complete cytoreduction). Repeat surgery should therefore be evaluated at a specialist center rather than assumed futile, though the decision again rests on prior surgical burden, grade, and PCI at the time of recurrence.

## Monitoring

- Cross-sectional imaging (CT, and MRI where needed) is used both at diagnosis, to characterize the loculated mucinous ascites and the scalloping of the liver and splenic margins that DeVita associates with mucin deposition against solid organs, and subsequently for surveillance.
- Serum tumor markers (CEA, CA19-9, and CA125) are used at baseline and through follow-up to help track disease burden and detect recurrence, alongside imaging.
- Long-term surveillance is required given the tendency of this disease, even after apparently complete cytoreduction, to recur or progress locally over time, particularly in less favorable histologic subtypes.

## When to refer

- Any suspected or confirmed pseudomyxoma peritonei should be referred promptly to a specialist peritoneal malignancy/surgical oncology center experienced in cytoreductive surgery and perioperative intraperitoneal chemotherapy, before further non-curative interventions accumulate a high prior surgical burden, which DeVita specifically links to worse outcomes in this histology.
- Patients presenting with bowel obstruction, high-volume symptomatic mucinous ascites, or nutritional decline need urgent surgical oncology input, since simple drainage does not address the underlying neoplasm and paracentesis is largely ineffective against the thick mucin.
- Diagnostic uncertainty about the primary site (appendix versus a mucinous ovarian tumor versus another gastrointestinal mucinous primary) should prompt gastrointestinal and pathology review before major surgery is planned, since misattributing the source can change the operative plan.

## What DeVita's PMP-relevant sections do not specify

DeVita's peritoneal-metastases chapter does not give a specific drug, drug class name, or regimen structure for HIPEC or systemic therapy in this disease with the level of detail that would let it be repeated confidently as PMP-specific (its concrete regimen examples in the same general area, such as mitomycin C for colorectal HIPEC or cisplatin/docetaxel for gastric HIPEC, are given for other peritoneal-surface cancers, not stated as the PMP regimen). That gap is deliberately left open here rather than filled in with an assumed drug choice.

Sources: DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of Oncology, 12th ed.
