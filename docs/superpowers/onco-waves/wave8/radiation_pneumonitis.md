# Radiation Pneumonitis — Management (Wave 8)

## Overview and staging by severity

Radiation pneumonitis is a subacute inflammatory lung injury following thoracic radiotherapy. DeVita notes clinically significant radiation pneumonitis occurs in roughly 5% to 50% of lung cancer patients, arising anywhere from 0 to 18 months after treatment with a peak around 2 months, and that more recent series (reflecting image-guided planning) report rates around 10% or lower. Typical features are persistent nonproductive cough, dyspnea, low-grade fever, and fatigue; chest imaging may be normal early on, and radiographic change (ground-glass, then patchy consolidation, then fibrosis) can lag or precede symptoms. Management is driven primarily by symptom severity rather than a formal oncologic stage, since this is a treatment-related toxicity rather than a malignancy in its own right.

- Mild, asymptomatic or minimally symptomatic radiographic change: observation, no specific drug therapy required (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Moderate to severe symptoms (significant cough, dyspnea, fever affecting function): glucocorticoids are the treatment described in DeVita, started after infection has been excluded.
- Chronic pulmonary fibrosis (established, later-stage lung scarring following thoracic radiation): DeVita states this is managed with supportive care such as supplemental oxygen and pulmonary rehabilitation, because no specific intervention, including glucocorticoids, has been shown to slow or reverse fibrosis progression.

## Lines of therapy

There is no multi-line systemic-therapy ladder for radiation pneumonitis itself (it is a toxicity of cancer treatment, not a cancer). What DeVita describes is essentially a single first-line intervention with a taper strategy:

- First line for moderate-to-severe pneumonitis: a corticosteroid, tapered slowly over weeks to months to minimize relapse of symptoms. DeVita is explicit that an infectious cause should be ruled out before starting steroids.
- For corticosteroid-refractory or immunotherapy-associated pneumonitis (patients on concurrent checkpoint inhibitors), escalation to steroid-sparing immunosuppression is standard oncology practice when steroids alone fail to control symptoms (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Once established pulmonary fibrosis has developed, there is no described therapy that reverses it; management shifts entirely to supportive care.

DeVita separately notes that prophylactic antibiotics or anticoagulants do not appear to alter the development of radiation pneumonitis, so these have no role as either prevention or treatment.

## Role of surgery, radiotherapy, and systemic therapy

- Surgery has no role in treating radiation pneumonitis; it is a complication of radiotherapy, not a lesion to resect.
- Radiotherapy planning is central to prevention rather than treatment. DeVita links risk to the volume of lung irradiated and treatment technique: risk and severity are lower with smaller-volume stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) compared with larger-volume conventionally fractionated radiation, and risk may be higher for tumors in the lower lung fields. Elsewhere DeVita ties risk to dose-volume metrics: risk rises with the mean lung dose and with the volume of lung exposed to higher radiation doses, and radiation-oncology dose-volume planning aims to keep this exposure below consensus thresholds to limit pneumonitis risk. These are planning/prevention considerations for the treating radiation oncologist, not treatments for pneumonitis once it has occurred.
- Systemic therapy interacts with risk in both directions. DeVita states the risk for radiation pneumonitis may be increased in patients who are older or who receive concurrent chemotherapy, specifically naming docetaxel, gemcitabine, and especially carboplatin plus paclitaxel. Once pneumonitis is established, the only systemic therapy DeVita describes as effective is the corticosteroid course above; there is no cytotoxic or targeted regimen used to treat pneumonitis itself.
- In patients on immune checkpoint inhibitors, concurrent or prior immunotherapy needs to be considered as a contributor to or confounder of lung toxicity, and immune-related pneumonitis must be distinguished from radiation pneumonitis since management (including consideration of holding the checkpoint inhibitor) can differ (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Monitoring

- Serial clinical assessment of cough, dyspnea, fever, and functional status, since the timeline described by DeVita (onset any time from 0 to 18 months, peak around 2 months) means monitoring needs to extend well beyond the end of radiotherapy.
- Imaging follow-up is informative because DeVita describes an evolving radiographic pattern: ground-glass opacification in the first 1 to 6 months, patchy consolidation from about 4 to 12 months, and fibrosis developing from 6 months out to several years, with early changes centered in the higher-dose region of the radiation field and later changes potentially extending into lower-dose regions.
- Oxygenation should be tracked (pulse oximetry, and blood gas or lung function testing where indicated) to catch progression toward respiratory compromise (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Before attributing new respiratory symptoms to radiation pneumonitis, infection, tumor progression, and pulmonary embolism should be actively excluded, since this remains fundamentally a diagnosis of exclusion.

## When to refer

- Moderate to severe symptoms warrant prompt evaluation for corticosteroid therapy, ideally with radiation oncology and pulmonology input, once infection has been excluded.
- Symptoms that fail to respond to an appropriate corticosteroid course, or recur on tapering, should prompt referral for consideration of steroid-sparing immunosuppression.
- Rapidly worsening breathlessness, hypoxemia, or any suspicion of superimposed infection or of an alternative diagnosis (pulmonary embolism, tumor progression, immune-related pneumonitis in a patient on checkpoint inhibitors) warrants urgent respiratory and oncology reassessment rather than continued empiric treatment for radiation pneumonitis.
- Patients progressing toward chronic pulmonary fibrosis benefit from referral to pulmonology for supportive measures such as oxygen supplementation and pulmonary rehabilitation, since DeVita states no specific intervention has been shown to slow or reverse this process.

Sources: DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of Oncology, 12th ed.
