# Plasmablastic Lymphoma (PBL) — Management

## Overview
Plasmablastic lymphoma is a rare, very aggressive subtype of large B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma that most often arises in the setting of immunodeficiency, classically HIV infection, but also transplantation and other immunocompromised states. It is EBV-positive in many cases, expresses plasma-cell markers (CD38, CD138, MUM1) while often lacking pan-B-cell markers such as CD20, and carries a poor prognosis. There is no separate PBL-specific staging system distinct from the general non-Hodgkin lymphoma (Ann Arbor / Lugano) framework used across other aggressive B-cell lymphomas.

## Initial Work-up Before Starting Therapy
- Confirm HIV status and, if positive, baseline CD4 count and HIV viral load, since these drive both prognosis and treatment intensity.
- Staging with PET-CT is appropriate; PET-CT is described as particularly useful for aggressive lymphomas including plasmablastic lymphoma.
- Examination of the cerebrospinal fluid is considered for all patients with plasmablastic lymphoma given its aggressive, extranodal-prone biology.
- Assess for a coexisting paraprotein and correlate morphology/immunophenotype carefully, since PBL sits at the interface with plasma-cell myeloma and the two must be distinguished before treatment is chosen (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Treatment by Intent

### Systemic therapy is the mainstay; there is no defined role for surgery
Surgery has no curative role in plasmablastic lymphoma; a biopsy is diagnostic only. This mirrors the general principle that non-Hodgkin lymphomas, unlike solid tumors, are treated primarily with systemic therapy rather than resection (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

### First-line systemic therapy
- For plasmablastic lymphoma, or for other AIDS-related large B-cell lymphomas with a very high proliferation index (Ki-67 greater than 80%), an intensified anthracycline-based, continuous-infusion regimen (dose-adjusted EPOCH-type approach) is favored over standard R-CHOP-type therapy.
- Rituximab is added to this backbone only if the tumor is CD20-positive; many plasmablastic lymphomas are CD20-negative, in which case rituximab is omitted.
- Antiretroviral therapy should be continued through chemotherapy if it is tolerated, reflecting the direct link between HIV control and lymphoma outcome in this population.
- Standard supportive prophylaxis during treatment should include coverage against Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, herpes zoster, and Candida.
- Because of the very high proliferative rate and bulky presentations typical of plasmablastic lymphoma, tumor lysis risk should be assessed and mitigated before and during the first cycle (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

### Role of radiotherapy
DeVita's section on this disease does not describe a defined role for radiotherapy in plasmablastic lymphoma specifically. Involved-site radiotherapy is sometimes used for palliation of a localized, symptomatic, or bulky mass in aggressive lymphomas generally, but this is not something grounded in the disease-specific text and should be individualized (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

### Later lines / relapsed or refractory disease
For relapsed AIDS-related aggressive non-Hodgkin lymphoma (the category plasmablastic lymphoma falls under), standard salvage chemotherapy followed by consolidative autologous stem cell transplantation can be considered in appropriate candidates; reported outcomes in this setting have been comparable to HIV-negative patients undergoing the same approach. This is not stated as a plasmablastic-lymphoma-specific pathway in the source text, so it should be read as extrapolated from the broader AIDS-related lymphoma relapse literature rather than dedicated PBL trial data.

## Monitoring
- Response assessment in aggressive lymphomas, including plasmablastic lymphoma, uses PET-CT with the Deauville 5-point scale; post-treatment PET should be timed at least several weeks after chemotherapy (and longer after any radiotherapy) to avoid false-positive inflammatory uptake.
- Serial CD4 count and HIV viral load monitoring should continue alongside oncologic follow-up in HIV-associated cases.
- Given the aggressive natural history, clinical follow-up intervals should be short in the first one to two years, with attention to the original site (oral cavity/jaw is classic) and to other extranodal sites (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## When to Refer
- Any new diagnosis of plasmablastic lymphoma warrants prompt referral to hematology-oncology given the aggressive course and the need for immunodeficiency work-up (HIV testing if not already known) before treatment selection.
- Refer to infectious disease/HIV specialists concurrently when HIV-associated, to coordinate antiretroviral therapy with chemotherapy.
- Consider transplant-center referral for eligible patients with relapsed or refractory disease who may be candidates for salvage chemotherapy and autologous stem cell transplantation.
- Urgent referral is warranted for airway or feeding compromise from a rapidly enlarging oral/facial mass, or for gastrointestinal bleeding or obstruction from mucosal disease, given the tumor's rapid growth rate.

Sources: DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of Oncology, 12th ed.
