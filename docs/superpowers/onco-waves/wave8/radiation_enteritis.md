# Radiation Enteritis (Radiation Enteropathy) — Management Expansion

## Overview and staging by phase

Radiation injury to the small and large bowel is described in DeVita as occurring in two distinct phases, and management differs by phase rather than by tumor "stage" (radiation enteritis is a treatment complication, not a malignancy). DeVita states that acute radiation enteritis/proctitis occurs within six weeks of therapy, with diarrhea, cramping, rectal urgency or tenesmus and, uncommonly, bleeding, and that these symptoms usually resolve without specific therapy within two to six months. Late radiation enteritis/proctitis generally occurs eight to twelve months after therapy and in some cases is delayed by years; it can present as malabsorption and/or diarrhea with rapid transit in the affected bowel, and bacterial overgrowth contributing to nausea, abdominal pain and diarrhea should be suspected, particularly where strictures or enterocolic fistulas are present. DeVita also notes that some patients develop a pancolitis resembling inflammatory bowel disease even though the large intestine is generally less radiosensitive.

## Acute phase management

- Because acute symptoms are usually self-limited (DeVita: resolution within two to six months), the approach during and shortly after pelvic or abdominal radiotherapy is symptomatic: control of diarrhea, cramping and urgency, and attention to hydration.
- (General oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease) antidiarrheal agents, antispasmodics, and a low-residue/low-fat diet during treatment are commonly used to reduce stool frequency and mucosal irritation while acute mucosal injury heals.
- DeVita's diarrhea chapter frames radiotherapy-induced diarrhea mechanistically as resulting from mucosal damage causing prostaglandin release and bile salt malabsorption, both of which increase intestinal peristalsis; this supports (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease) the use of bile acid sequestrants when bile salt malabsorption is suspected as a contributor to diarrhea.
- Radiotherapy is not routinely interrupted for mild-to-moderate acute enteritis; treatment breaks are considered case by case when symptoms are severe (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Late phase / chronic enteropathy management

- DeVita specifically flags bacterial overgrowth as a treatable contributor to the malabsorption, nausea, abdominal pain and diarrhea of late radiation enteritis, and states it should be suspected especially when strictures or enterocolic fistulas are present. Directed evaluation and treatment of bacterial overgrowth is therefore a grounded part of chronic-phase management.
- Where a pancolitis resembling inflammatory bowel disease develops (DeVita notes this can occur even though the colon is less radiosensitive), distinguishing it from true inflammatory bowel disease is necessary, since management approaches differ.
- For late radiation proctitis specifically, DeVita (in the context of pelvic/prostate radiotherapy late rectal toxicity) describes a graded approach: rectal bleeding, mucous discharge and mild incontinence are the common late toxicities; Grade 2 rectal bleeding can be effectively treated with steroid suppositories, sitz baths, and an increase in dietary fiber. Deep rectal biopsies and cauterization procedures should be avoided unless absolutely necessary because of the increased risk of further mucosal trauma and fistula formation. For radiation-induced proctitis not responsive to conservative measures, argon-beam plasma laser coagulation can decrease the frequency of rectal bleeding episodes. Hyperbaric oxygen therapy may also be helpful, on the rationale that it improves oxygen delivery to ischemic rectal mucosa and promotes angiogenesis and mucosal healing.
- Severe complications, including ulcer development and fistula formation, are uncommon (DeVita: observed in a small minority of patients) but when present typically require surgical or endoscopic input.

## Role of surgery

- Surgical intervention is reserved for structural complications: obstruction, stricture, or fistula. Operating on irradiated bowel carries a high complication rate because of the obliterative endarteritis and fibrosis underlying chronic radiation injury (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease, though consistent with DeVita's broader description of late radiation-induced bowel obstruction as a true late effect of radiotherapy).
- DeVita explicitly cautions against deep rectal biopsies and cauterization procedures for radiation proctitis "unless absolutely necessary," given the risk of trauma and fistula formation, favoring conservative and endoscopic (argon-beam) measures first.

## Nutrition support and malignant bowel obstruction overlap

- DeVita identifies radiation enteritis as one of the recognized causes of malignant bowel obstruction (MBO) in cancer patients, alongside tumor-related and benign etiologies such as adhesions. Standard MBO supportive measures apply when obstruction develops: nasogastric decompression, intravenous hydration, and pharmacologic control of nausea, vomiting and pain, with antisecretory agents (e.g., a somatostatin analog, steroids, or scopolamine), pain medications, and antiemetic therapy used to relieve symptoms.
- DeVita also discusses parenteral nutrition (PN) in cancer patients with radiation enteritis as part of home PN in the terminally ill: PN is most often used in patients with bowel obstruction, malabsorption, fistula, and radiation enteritis, but a cited meta-analysis of inoperable malignant bowel obstruction found benefits were limited and costs high, with only a suggestion of survival benefit. Home PN should be reserved for patients with good performance status, an expected survival long enough to justify it (guidelines cited by DeVita suggest offering PN support if survival is expected to exceed roughly 40 to 60 days), and reliable caretaker support, with quality of life as the primary consideration in the palliative setting.

## Monitoring

- Longitudinal monitoring of nutritional status, weight, and evidence of malabsorption or bacterial overgrowth is appropriate for patients with chronic radiation enteropathy (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Patients with strictures or fistula risk warrant monitoring for evolving obstructive symptoms so that surgical referral is not delayed once conservative measures fail.

## When to refer

- Refer to gastroenterology or colorectal/GI surgery when late radiation proctitis fails conservative measures (steroid suppositories, sitz baths, dietary fiber) and endoscopic therapy such as argon-beam plasma coagulation, or hyperbaric oxygen, is being considered.
- Refer for surgical evaluation when obstruction, stricture, or fistula develops, recognizing (as above) the elevated risk of operating on irradiated bowel.
- Refer to nutrition/palliative care services when malabsorption is significant or when parenteral nutrition is being considered, particularly in patients with advanced or terminal disease, given the limited and costly benefit demonstrated in the malignant bowel obstruction literature DeVita cites.

## What DeVita does not specify

DeVita's sections reviewed here do not provide a specific pharmacologic regimen for acute radiation enteritis diarrhea beyond the mechanistic rationale (prostaglandin release, bile salt malabsorption), do not give a dedicated small-bowel (as opposed to rectal) endoscopic or surgical algorithm, and do not discuss antibiotic choice for bacterial overgrowth in this condition. These points are omitted here rather than filled in from general practice beyond what is marked above.

Sources: DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of Oncology, 12th ed.
