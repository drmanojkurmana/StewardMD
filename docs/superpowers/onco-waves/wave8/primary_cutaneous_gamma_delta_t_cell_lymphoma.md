# Primary cutaneous gamma-delta T-cell lymphoma (PCGD-TCL) — Management

## Overview and framing
DeVita describes the cutaneous gamma/delta T-cell lymphomas as characterised by disseminated disease with frequent mucosal and extranodal involvement, and notes an association with haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH). Histopathologically the infiltrate involves dermis, epidermis and fat, with rimming of fat globules and angioinvasion. This is distinct from the alpha/beta form of subcutaneous panniculitis-like T-cell lymphoma (SPTCL), which spares epidermis and dermis, has an excellent prognosis (about 80% five-year survival), and is managed with corticosteroids, single-agent chemotherapy, or radiotherapy. Separating the gamma/delta cutaneous lymphomas from alpha/beta SPTCL is prognostically essential, because the two entities are treated with very different intensity (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease, for the practical point that misclassification changes treatment intensity, though the underlying prognostic split itself is stated in DeVita).

DeVita is explicit that most patients with PCGD-TCL have a poor outcome despite aggressive chemotherapy, citing a median survival of 15 months in a series of 33 patients. This should be communicated to patients and families as a systemic, aggressive disease rather than one that can be controlled by skin-directed measures alone.

## Treatment approach by stage/intent
- Because DeVita characterises this entity as disseminated with frequent mucosal and extranodal involvement, treatment intent is systemic disease control rather than local control, even when the initial clinical presentation appears confined to a limited area of skin.
- Aggressive systemic chemotherapy is described in DeVita as the mainstay, and the same text states this does not translate into good outcomes for most patients (median survival about 15 months), so expectation-setting with the patient about a difficult prognosis is part of initial management.
- Naming a specific first-line multiagent regimen (for example CHOP-like or etoposide-containing combinations) is a general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease; DeVita's own text on PCGD-TCL states only that outcomes with aggressive chemotherapy are poor, without naming a preferred regimen.

## Role of allogeneic transplantation
- DeVita reports that allogeneic haematopoietic stem-cell transplantation (HSCT) has been used as a curative-intent modality in PCGD-TCL: in a combined series of 10 patients with PCGD-TCL and 4 with SPTCL, 7 patients underwent allogeneic HSCT and 4 (57%) were alive at follow-up ranging from about 3 months to nearly 8 years (including 3 with PCGD-TCL).
- Two patients underwent autologous HSCT (one with PCGD-TCL) and were alive at a median follow-up of about 1.9 years in that same report.
- Practical implication grounded in this data: allogeneic HSCT should be considered in fit patients who respond to induction chemotherapy, since it is the modality DeVita associates with the longest observed survivors; discussion of transplant eligibility should therefore happen early, in parallel with induction therapy, rather than being deferred to relapse (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease, for the specific timing recommendation).

## Role of surgery and radiotherapy
- DeVita's section on PCGD-TCL does not describe a role for surgery or radiotherapy as primary or curative treatment; by contrast, it explicitly assigns surgery, radiation, or single-agent chemotherapy to the more indolent, better-prognosis entities discussed in the same passage (alpha/beta SPTCL and CD4+ primary cutaneous PTCL unspecified).
- Radiotherapy for a localised, symptomatic, or ulcerating lesion in PCGD-TCL is a reasonable palliative or adjunctive measure by general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease; it should not substitute for systemic treatment of what DeVita characterises as a disseminated disease.

## Recognising and managing haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis
- DeVita links the cutaneous gamma/delta T-cell lymphomas to HLH as an associated complication. Any patient with PCGD-TCL who develops high fever, cytopenias, or a markedly elevated ferritin should be evaluated for HLH promptly, since this complication is described as part of the disease's aggressive biology.
- Directed treatment of HLH itself (immunosuppressive/HLH-directed therapy) once identified is a general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease, since DeVita's PCGD-TCL passage does not detail an HLH treatment protocol.

## Monitoring
- Given the disseminated, mucosal/extranodal-involving pattern DeVita describes, staging and monitoring should assess beyond the skin; DeVita does not specify an imaging or laboratory monitoring schedule for this entity, so specific monitoring intervals are a general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease.
- Because HLH is a recognised association, surveillance for its laboratory features (cytopenias, ferritin trend) during and after induction therapy is reasonable, following from the association DeVita states, even though DeVita does not prescribe a monitoring cadence.

## When to refer
- All patients with a confirmed or suspected diagnosis of PCGD-TCL should be managed within a haemato-oncology multidisciplinary team, given the aggressive course and poor outcomes with standard chemotherapy that DeVita describes.
- Early referral for transplant evaluation is warranted for patients who are candidates for aggressive therapy, based on the survival benefit DeVita associates with allogeneic HSCT in this disease.
- Clinical trial enrolment should be considered given the poor outcomes with conventional aggressive chemotherapy reported in DeVita (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease, for the specific recommendation to seek trial enrolment, though it follows directly from the poor-outcome data DeVita reports).

## What DeVita does not specify (and is deliberately omitted here)
DeVita's section on PCGD-TCL does not name a specific systemic regimen, does not give response rates for any named drug or combination, and does not give a transplant-conditioning approach. These specifics have been omitted rather than filled in with unsupported detail.

Sources: DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of Oncology, 12th ed.
