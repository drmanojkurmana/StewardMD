# Penile Cancer - Management

## Overview and treatment philosophy
Treatment of penile carcinoma is guided by two independent axes: the local extent of the primary lesion and the status of the regional (inguinal) lymph nodes. Because more than 95 percent of penile cancers are squamous cell carcinoma, most of the management literature below refers to SCC of the penis. Survival in penile cancer is driven largely by how the lymph nodes are managed, not just by control of the primary tumor, so nodal staging and treatment cannot be deferred while only the primary lesion is addressed.

## Carcinoma in situ (Tis, erythroplasia of Queyrat)
For noninvasive disease confined to the epithelium, a conservative, organ-sparing approach is preferred over penectomy.
- Circumcision alone is often adequate for preputial lesions.
- Topical agents (5-fluorouracil cream, imiquimod) have been used for glandular and meatal lesions with good cosmetic results, though skin irritation, pain, dysuria, and nausea limit adherence in a meaningful minority of patients; recurrences can sometimes be salvaged with imiquimod.
- Penile-preserving techniques such as laser ablation (CO2 or Nd:YAG), Mohs micrographic surgery, or glans resection with skin grafting are options when topical therapy fails or is not tolerated. Laser therapy carries the highest recurrence rate among these options, and recurrences can present late, so extended follow-up is needed.
- Progression to invasive disease or nodal metastasis from carcinoma in situ is uncommon.

## Verrucous carcinoma (Ta)
This subtype grows aggressively at the local level but has low metastatic potential. Partial or total penectomy is generally overtreatment; laser ablation, Mohs surgery, or glans resurfacing are favored. Radiation therapy is not routinely used for verrucous carcinoma given historical concerns about degeneration after older RT techniques and a lack of contemporary supportive data.

## Invasive primary tumor (T1-T3)
- Distal lesions where a functional penis (for standing micturition and sexual function) can be preserved are treated with partial penectomy. Historic teaching called for a 2-cm proximal margin, but margin requirements can be reduced for lower-grade tumors, which has increased interest in penile-preserving surgical approaches (glansectomy, partial glansectomy with resurfacing or grafting).
- Extensive lesions approaching the base of the penis usually require total penectomy with perineal urethrostomy.
- Local recurrence after appropriately selected partial penectomy is rare, and most relapses occur within the first 12 to 18 months, so early follow-up should be more frequent; salvage surgery is generally effective when recurrence does occur.
- Penile-preserving techniques (laser, glansectomy) can achieve cancer-specific survival similar to partial penectomy, but at the cost of a substantially higher local recurrence rate, so patients need to be selected carefully and followed closely.
- Radiation therapy (external-beam or brachytherapy) is an organ-preserving alternative, most effective for small T1-T2 lesions, and is best delivered at centers experienced in both modalities. RT carries higher recurrence and toxicity rates than surgery, and a meaningful fraction of RT failures can still be salvaged surgically. Circumcision before RT is recommended to allow direct tumor inspection and to reduce acute toxicity. RT is a reasonable option for patients with small, distal, superficial lesions who wish to preserve the organ, who decline surgery, or who are unfit for major surgery.

## Locally advanced primary tumor (T4)
Large proximal or invasive tumors require total penectomy with perineal urethrostomy, and involvement of adjacent structures may require total emasculation (penectomy, scrotectomy, orchiectomy). For unresectable disease, concurrent chemotherapy and radiation therapy is the modern recommended approach rather than the historically described ultra-radical procedures.

## Management of regional (inguinal) lymph nodes
Nodal status is the single most important prognostic factor in penile cancer, and inguinal metastases are potentially curable by lymphadenectomy alone, unlike in most other genitourinary cancers where nodal spread implies a systemic problem.

**Clinically node-negative disease (cN0).** Management is risk-stratified:
- Low-grade, low-stage primary tumors carry a low risk of occult nodal metastasis and can reasonably be observed.
- High-grade or higher-stage primary tumors carry a substantially higher risk of occult metastasis, and inguinal lymphadenectomy (or dynamic sentinel node biopsy in appropriate candidates) is recommended without delay rather than waiting for clinically evident recurrence, since delayed lymphadenectomy is associated with markedly worse survival than early or prophylactic lymphadenectomy.
- Dynamic sentinel node biopsy is an option to stage the groins in appropriately selected patients but should not replace formal inguinal lymphadenectomy when lymphadenectomy is otherwise indicated; combining it with inguinal ultrasound improves its detection rate.
- A prolonged empiric course of antibiotics for palpable groin nodes, on the assumption they are purely inflammatory, is no longer recommended because it delays definitive nodal management.

**Clinically node-positive disease (cN1-cN3).**
- A modified (limited-field) inguinal lymphadenectomy is now preferred over the traditional complete dissection to reduce morbidity while still removing the deep nodes.
- Bilateral dissection is standard when unilateral nodes are positive at presentation, given the frequency of bilateral lymphatic drainage; a unilateral dissection can suffice when node positivity develops later, on one side only, after the primary tumor has already been treated.
- Extranodal extension or more than two positive ipsilateral nodes on inguinal dissection is an indication for ipsilateral pelvic lymphadenectomy, and adjuvant chemotherapy and radiation should be considered.
- Bilateral mobile nodes (cN2) should be biopsied; if positive, neoadjuvant chemotherapy precedes lymphadenectomy.
- Fixed, bulky inguinal nodes (cN3) require neoadjuvant chemotherapy followed by surgery when resectable, or chemoradiotherapy when the disease is unresectable.
- Surgical resection of inguinal nodes is regarded in the literature as superior to radiation therapy for nodal control, because the inguinal region tolerates radiation poorly and post-radiation changes make later clinical assessment of the groin difficult.

**Systemic therapy around lymphadenectomy for node-positive disease.** A platinum, taxane, and ifosfamide-based triplet regimen has been studied prospectively in bulky nodal disease (cN2/cN3) and produced an objective response in about half of treated patients, with a smaller complete response rate; median survival in that population was measured in months rather than years. A platinum, taxane, and fluoropyrimidine-based triplet has also been used in the adjuvant setting after lymphadenectomy, and is recommended by national consensus and European guidance alike for pN2-3 disease. Some individual retrospective series have questioned whether adjuvant chemotherapy adds benefit in patients with more limited nodal disease (pN0-N2), so its use in that group is less settled than in pN3 disease.

**Adjuvant radiation after lymphadenectomy.** Adjuvant RT to the inguinal (with or without pelvic) nodal basin after lymphadenectomy has shown a survival benefit in retrospective series, particularly in higher pathologic nodal stages, but its role remains controversial, with some guideline bodies recommending it for pN2-3 disease and others not endorsing it. RT can also be used palliatively for fixed, inoperable inguinal nodes or unresectable disease.

## Metastatic disease (M1)
Prognosis for metastatic penile carcinoma is poor, with most patients dying within two years, and those who relapse after neoadjuvant chemotherapy have a median survival under six months.
- Palliative chemotherapy with a platinum, taxane, and ifosfamide-based regimen is used as first-line treatment, extrapolated from its activity in bulky nodal disease.
- A taxane, platinum, and fluoropyrimidine-based regimen has activity but is described as highly toxic, as are bleomycin-containing regimens; patients unfit for further cytotoxic therapy after first-line treatment are best served by supportive/palliative care rather than empiric second-line chemotherapy.
- Molecular targeted therapy (EGFR inhibition) has shown response activity in an early-phase trial in chemotherapy-naive patients with advanced nodal or metastatic disease, and case series suggest activity for another anti-EGFR antibody, but this is investigational and not standard of care.
- Immunotherapy with checkpoint inhibitors is being explored on the rationale that PD-L1 is expressed in a substantial minority of penile cancers, particularly HPV-negative tumors, but this is not yet established therapy (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease, regarding checkpoint inhibitors being an active investigational area across squamous cancers broadly).

## Monitoring and follow-up
- Because most local recurrences after penile-preserving surgery or partial penectomy occur within the first 12 to 18 months, close early follow-up with physical examination of both the primary site and the inguinal basins is warranted (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease, regarding the specific follow-up interval/schedule).
- Patients managed with laser ablation or other organ-sparing techniques need longer-term surveillance, since a meaningful proportion of recurrences in these patients present after five years.
- Distinguishing post-radiation fibrosis, scarring, or necrosis from recurrent cancer can be difficult clinically and may require biopsy.

## When to refer
- Any patient with high-grade or higher-stage (T1G3 or T2-T4) primary disease, or any patient with clinically palpable or biopsy-confirmed inguinal nodes, should be referred promptly to a urologic oncology center experienced in penile cancer, given the well-documented survival cost of delayed or omitted lymphadenectomy and the historic pattern of undertreatment (low rates of guideline-adherent lymphadenectomy) reported in national cancer registries.
- Patients being considered for organ-preserving radiation therapy should be referred to a center with experience in both external-beam and brachytherapy techniques for the penis, given the technical complexity and toxicity profile of these approaches.
- Patients with bulky or fixed nodal disease (cN2/cN3), unresectable primary tumors, or metastatic disease should be referred for multidisciplinary management involving urologic oncology, medical oncology, and radiation oncology, since these presentations require coordinated neoadjuvant or concurrent chemoradiotherapy strategies rather than surgery alone.
