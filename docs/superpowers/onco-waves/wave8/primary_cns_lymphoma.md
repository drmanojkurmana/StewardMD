# Primary CNS Lymphoma (PCNSL) - Management Narrative

## Diagnostic workup before treatment
Whenever PCNSL is suspected, corticosteroids should be avoided before biopsy where possible, because
steroids can rapidly shrink the tumour (the "vanishing tumour" effect) and interfere with histopathologic
diagnosis, causing delay that can harm outcome. Diagnosis is typically established by stereotactic brain
biopsy; when the lesion is not surgically accessible (for example brainstem or spinal cord lesions), CSF
molecular markers can raise suspicion, but no marker is yet established as diagnostic in routine practice.
Staging should establish the extent of CNS disease (brain MRI, CSF cytology and flow cytometry when
lumbar puncture is safe, and a slit-lamp ophthalmologic evaluation) and exclude concurrent systemic
disease outside the CNS (whole-body PET-CT or, if unavailable, CT of the neck/chest/abdomen/pelvis,
testicular ultrasound in men, and bone marrow biopsy/aspiration), since extraneural disease must be
excluded to make the diagnosis of PCNSL. Baseline assessment of fitness, cognition, and organ function
before treatment is emphasised as central to treatment selection.

## Role of surgery
Resection is not part of the standard treatment approach for PCNSL. Surgery's role is essentially limited
to obtaining tissue via stereotactic biopsy; routine debulking has not shown a consistent survival benefit
across studies and carries a risk of permanent neurologic deficit or delay of definitive treatment. Surgical
debulking may be considered in rare cases of large lesions causing acute mass effect or impending
herniation, where relieving pressure can improve performance status ahead of nonsurgical therapy.

## Treatment approach by fitness category
Patients are generally triaged into three groups based on fitness rather than a strict age cutoff:
- Fit enough for intensive immunochemotherapy with consolidative high-dose chemotherapy and
  autologous stem-cell transplantation (including select fit patients over 70).
- Ineligible for transplant-based consolidation but eligible for high-dose methotrexate-based
  immunochemotherapy.
- Ineligible even for methotrexate-based therapy, for whom palliative treatment is appropriate (a
  minority of patients).

### Fit (typically younger) patients
Induction is built around high-dose methotrexate; there is general agreement on including it, though no
consensus on the ideal combination partner. Combining methotrexate with other agents (cytarabine, an
alkylating agent, and/or an anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody such as rituximab) improves radiographic
response rates and survival compared with methotrexate alone, and appears to preserve cognition better.
Evidence on the benefit of adding rituximab is mixed: while its use is grounded in extrapolation from
systemic diffuse large B-cell lymphoma and in favourable meta-analysis signals for progression-free
survival, at least one randomized trial did not show an overall or event-free survival benefit, so it should
be regarded as a widely-used but not universally validated component of induction.

After induction achieves a response, consolidation options include reduced-dose whole-brain
radiotherapy, high-dose chemotherapy with autologous stem-cell transplantation, or nonmyeloablative
consolidation chemotherapy. Randomized comparisons have found high-dose chemotherapy with
autologous transplant to be broadly comparable in survival to whole-brain radiotherapy consolidation
but associated with better preservation of cognitive function, which has shifted many fit patients toward
transplant-based consolidation where available. Deferring or omitting whole-brain radiotherapy from
initial therapy (reserving it for relapse) can preserve progression-free survival benefit from combined
modality treatment without the added neurotoxicity risk that accompanies radiotherapy, and does not
appear to compromise overall survival when substituted with an active consolidation chemotherapy or
transplant strategy.

### Elderly or less fit patients
There is no single established standard of care for elderly patients, but induction should still be built
around high-dose methotrexate-based combination chemotherapy when organ function and fitness
permit; a baseline assessment of organ function and overall fitness should be done first. Because of the
markedly higher risk of neurotoxicity in this population, whole-brain radiotherapy should be deferred to
relapse rather than used up front. Oral alkylating agents (such as procarbazine or temozolomide)
combined with high-dose methotrexate have been associated with improved survival in elderly cohorts.
Maintenance therapy (for example with temozolomide, or with lenalidomide after response to
methotrexate-based induction) has been explored in this group as a way to extend remission without the
toxicity of further intensive chemotherapy. Transplant-based consolidation can be feasible in carefully
selected "fit" older patients up to around age 70, without excessive treatment-related mortality, but this
should not be generalised to the whole elderly population.

### Historical role of radiotherapy
Whole-brain radiotherapy alone was the historical primary treatment but produced a high rate of early
relapse despite frequent radiographic response, and carries a real risk of delayed neurotoxicity
(cognitive decline), especially in combination with chemotherapy or in older patients. Because PCNSL is
an infiltrative, often multifocal disease, focal radiation or radiosurgery is not appropriate. Today,
whole-brain radiotherapy as sole initial treatment is reserved for patients who are not candidates for
chemotherapy.

## Ocular involvement
The disease file's own clinical pearls note that intraocular involvement occurs in roughly one in five
patients and can be directed with intravitreal chemotherapy or ocular radiotherapy (general oncology
standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease reviewed here). DeVita's section reviewed here
covers ocular evaluation as part of staging but the specific ocular-directed treatment detail was not found
in the passages grounded for this note.

## Monitoring
After completing treatment, regular surveillance neuroimaging is important, particularly in the first years
after achieving complete response, because relapse is common (including in patients who are otherwise
asymptomatic at relapse) and earlier detection is associated with better performance status and
outcomes at the time of relapse. Response should be assessed against the disease's dedicated response
criteria, which account for brain imaging, corticosteroid dose, ophthalmologic findings, and CSF cytology
together, not imaging alone.

## Relapsed/refractory disease
A substantial proportion of patients (roughly a fifth to half) do not achieve complete remission with
current induction regimens, and even after remission relapse remains common, particularly in older
patients; outcomes after relapse are poor. No single standard of care exists for relapsed/refractory
disease, and enrolment in a clinical trial should be the preferred option whenever one is available.
Outside a trial, options include rechallenge with high-dose methotrexate-based therapy in patients who
previously responded to it, high-dose chemotherapy with autologous transplant in fit patients who did
not already receive myeloablative therapy, and whole-brain radiotherapy in patients who did not receive
it as part of initial treatment (recognising a meaningful neurotoxicity risk, especially in patients over 60
or those relapsing within six months of initial methotrexate-based therapy).

## Special populations
In immunodeficiency-associated PCNSL, the disease is strongly associated with Epstein-Barr virus, and
its incidence has risen alongside growing numbers of immunosuppressed patients, notably those with
HIV infection and transplant recipients on immunosuppressants. Optimising antiretroviral therapy in HIV
or reducing iatrogenic immunosuppression after transplant is a reasonable general principle in this
setting (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease), though the specific
sequencing with lymphoma-directed treatment was not detailed in the DeVita passages grounded here.

## When to refer
Given the complexity of staging, the need for expert stereotactic biopsy and neuropathology, and the
narrow, fitness-dependent choices between methotrexate-based induction, transplant-based
consolidation, and radiotherapy, patients with suspected or confirmed PCNSL should be managed within
a combined haemato-oncology and neuro-oncology multidisciplinary team from the point of diagnosis,
with prompt referral for stereotactic biopsy before corticosteroids are given wherever it is safe to delay
them.

## What was deliberately omitted
Specific regimen names, drug combinations beyond generic class description, response-rate and
survival statistics, and radiotherapy dose ranges reported in DeVita were intentionally omitted or
generalised here because they either constitute numeric dosing/schedule information (excluded by
policy) or because including precise trial-level statistics without full context risked overstating certainty
in a prose management summary; the underlying evidence base (multiple randomized and phase II
trials of methotrexate-based induction, transplant versus radiotherapy consolidation, and salvage
strategies) is real and grounded, but is intentionally not restated at the level of individual trial numbers.
