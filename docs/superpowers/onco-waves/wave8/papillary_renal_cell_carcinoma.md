# Papillary renal cell carcinoma - management narrative

## Biology relevant to treatment planning

Papillary RCC (also called chromophil RCC) is molecularly and clinically distinct from clear cell RCC, and type 1 and type 2 papillary tumours behave differently. Type 1 tumours are driven by MET pathway activation (germline MET mutation in the hereditary papillary renal carcinoma syndrome, or somatic MET alterations in the sporadic form) and follow a more indolent course. Type 2 tumours are more molecularly heterogeneous, marked by CDKN2A silencing, SETD2 mutation, TFE3/TFEB fusions and activation of the NRF2 antioxidant pathway, and carry a worse prognosis; a subset with CpG island hypermethylation and coexisting fumarate hydratase mutation (seen in hereditary leiomyomatosis and renal cell cancer, HLRCC) has the most aggressive course and the worst survival. This type/genotype distinction should inform how aggressively a given tumour is treated and followed.

## Localised disease

Management follows the same risk-stratified approach used across renal cell carcinoma histologies (clear cell, papillary, chromophobe), since most localised-disease evidence in DeVita's kidney cancer chapter is reported across histologies rather than papillary-specific:
- Small, incidentally-detected renal masses can be managed with radical nephrectomy, partial (nephron-sparing) nephrectomy, thermal ablation, or active surveillance; no single approach is correct for every patient, and the choice depends on tumour size, location, patient comorbidity and renal function.
- Partial nephrectomy is now standard of care for small renal masses because of the recognised harm of unnecessary loss of renal parenchyma with radical nephrectomy.
- Active surveillance with serial imaging (roughly every three to six months) is an accepted option in selected patients, particularly for lesions under 2 cm, complex cystic lesions, or where competing comorbidity outweighs the benefit of active treatment; most small renal masses under surveillance grow slowly and have a low rate of metastatic progression over the first two to three years.
- A urologist with expertise in kidney cancer should lead selection of the local treatment strategy.

## Locally advanced disease

- Papillary histology has been identified as an independent predictor of worse cancer-specific survival compared with other histologies in patients undergoing radical nephrectomy and inferior vena cava thrombectomy for tumour venous extension, alongside fat invasion and thrombus level. Surgery (radical nephrectomy with thrombectomy, escalating to more complex vascular reconstruction techniques as thrombus level increases) remains the primary treatment for RCC with venous/caval extension regardless of histology.
- Regional lymphadenectomy functions mainly as a staging tool rather than a therapeutic one in node-negative disease; it should be considered selectively (for example in younger patients, FH-deficient/type 2 papillary tumours, or those being considered for adjuvant trials) rather than performed routinely, since most patients do not derive a survival benefit and lymphadenectomy carries its own morbidity. In patients with radiographically involved nodes, regional lymphadenectomy is still considered standard.

## Adjuvant systemic therapy

Adjuvant systemic therapy after resection of high-risk RCC has an inconsistent evidence base and several relevant trials specifically enrolled non-clear-cell histologies (which include papillary RCC), not clear cell alone:
- The ASSURE trial randomised patients with high-risk clear cell and non-clear cell RCC (including papillary) to adjuvant sunitinib, sorafenib, or placebo and found no disease-free survival benefit for either drug.
- The S-TRAC trial (sunitinib versus placebo) showed a modest disease-free survival improvement with sunitinib but no overall survival benefit; this trial supported the regulatory approval of adjuvant sunitinib in high-risk kidney cancer, though the benefit is modest and toxicity/discontinuation rates were substantial in both this and the ASSURE trial.
- Other completed adjuvant trials (axitinib, pazopanib) have been largely negative or inconsistent, and adjuvant checkpoint-inhibitor trials that enrolled all RCC histologies (including papillary) had not reported results in the source reviewed.
- Given this mixed and largely negative record, and the fact that papillary-specific adjuvant data are lacking, adjuvant systemic therapy for papillary RCC is not clearly established outside of a clinical trial and should be individualised with a genitourinary oncology multidisciplinary team; enrolment in a trial should be favoured when available (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease, since DeVita's discussion is at the whole-RCC-cohort level and does not isolate outcomes by histology).

## Metastatic / advanced disease

- Direct evidence for papillary RCC comes from a phase II trial of foretinib, a MET/VEGFR2 multikinase inhibitor, in patients with bilateral, multifocal, or metastatic papillary RCC (including hereditary papillary renal carcinoma with germline MET mutation). Foretinib showed an objective response rate of about 13.5% and a progression-free survival of about 9.3 months, with the best responses seen in patients carrying a germline MET mutation. This supports MET pathway inhibition as a biologically rational, but only modestly effective, option in MET-driven papillary disease, and argues for MET mutation testing to help select patients most likely to respond.
- Beyond this trial, DeVita's kidney cancer chapter does not lay out a papillary-specific systemic therapy algorithm. In general oncology practice, metastatic RCC of any histology (including papillary) is treated with VEGF-pathway tyrosine kinase inhibitors, mTOR inhibitors, and immune checkpoint inhibitor-based combinations, sequenced by risk group and prior therapy (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease). Because papillary and other non-clear-cell histologies were historically under-represented in the pivotal trials that established these regimens, treatment of metastatic papillary RCC should be discussed with a genitourinary oncology multidisciplinary team, with a strong preference for clinical trial enrolment given the lack of high-quality papillary-specific efficacy data.
- Mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway inhibitors are approved across RCC histologies; efficacy is maintained across histologic subtypes but has generally been more pronounced in chromophobe than in other non-clear-cell histologies, which by extension likely includes papillary RCC (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease, as this observation is drawn from a pharmacology discussion rather than DeVita's kidney cancer chapter).

## Hereditary disease and genetics

- A germline MET mutation should be suspected in patients with multifocal, bilateral type 1 papillary tumours (hereditary papillary renal carcinoma), an autosomal-dominant syndrome with near-complete penetrance by age 80. These patients should be referred for genetic assessment, and relatives offered cascade testing and renal imaging surveillance.
- Aggressive, typically solitary type 2 papillary tumours associated with fumarate hydratase mutation should raise suspicion for hereditary leiomyomatosis and renal cell cancer (HLRCC), especially with a personal or family history of cutaneous or uterine leiomyomas; these tumours behave aggressively and warrant prompt genetic referral and closer surveillance rather than a watchful approach.

## Monitoring

DeVita's kidney cancer chapter does not give a papillary-specific surveillance schedule; the chapter notes that pathologic stage and risk features dictate surveillance intensity and eligibility for adjuvant therapy after resection, without specifying a fixed protocol for papillary disease. In practice, post-treatment monitoring is with cross-sectional imaging (chest and abdomen), timed and intensified according to stage, grade and histologic risk (higher intensity for type 2 or FH-deficient tumours), consistent with general RCC follow-up practice (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## When to refer

- Refer to urology/uro-oncology for any renal mass requiring biopsy, surgical planning, or ablation candidacy assessment.
- Refer to a genitourinary oncology multidisciplinary team for locally advanced disease (nodal or venous/caval involvement), for any adjuvant therapy decision, and for all metastatic disease.
- Refer to clinical genetics whenever multifocal/bilateral type 1 tumours, a young patient with aggressive type 2 disease, or cutaneous/uterine leiomyomas raise suspicion for hereditary papillary renal carcinoma or HLRCC.
- Favour referral to a clinical trial for metastatic papillary RCC given the limited histology-specific evidence base.

Sources: DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of Oncology, 12th ed.
