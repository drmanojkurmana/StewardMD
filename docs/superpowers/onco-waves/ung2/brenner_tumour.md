# Brenner Tumour (Ovarian Transitional Cell Tumour) - Management

## Grounding note

DeVita's ovarian cancer chapter lists Brenner tumour only inside the WHO epithelial ovarian tumour classification table (benign, borderline/atypical proliferating, and malignant Brenner tumour as the three tiers) and again as one of the benign solid ovarian tumours in the differential of an adnexal mass. It does not carry a dedicated management or treatment paragraph for Brenner tumour specifically. Everything below on staging, surgical extent, systemic therapy, and follow-up is therefore general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease, and is marked as such. Only the tumour's classification tier (benign / borderline / malignant) and its place in the differential of a solid ovarian mass are DeVita-grounded.

## Treatment approach by tier

Brenner tumour is graded, per WHO classification, into three tiers, and management follows the tier:

- **Benign Brenner tumour** (the great majority of cases): treated as a benign ovarian neoplasm. Simple excision of the mass, or unilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, is curative (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease). No adjuvant therapy, staging surgery, or oncology referral is required once benign histology is confirmed on final pathology.
- **Borderline (atypical proliferative) Brenner tumour**: managed like other borderline ovarian tumours. Complete surgical removal of the involved ovary (or fertility-sparing cystectomy/oophorectomy in a woman wishing to preserve fertility) is generally sufficient; these tumours behave indolently after complete excision and do not routinely require chemotherapy (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- **Malignant Brenner tumour**: rare, and is staged and treated like other malignant epithelial ovarian cancers (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease). In practice this means comprehensive ovarian cancer staging surgery (total hysterectomy, bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, omentectomy, peritoneal staging, and lymph node assessment as indicated) followed by consideration of adjuvant platinum-based systemic chemotherapy for higher-stage or high-risk disease, following the same stage-adapted approach used for other epithelial ovarian cancers (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Role of surgery, radiotherapy, and systemic therapy

- Surgery is the primary and, for benign and borderline disease, usually the only treatment needed (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Radiotherapy has no established role in Brenner tumour of any tier (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Systemic therapy (platinum-based combination chemotherapy) is reserved for malignant Brenner tumour with adverse features or advanced stage, mirroring adjuvant chemotherapy use in other epithelial ovarian cancers; there is no disease-specific evidence base for a particular regimen in Brenner tumour, so treatment choice should follow the same stage- and risk-adapted pathways used for other malignant epithelial ovarian tumours (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Lines of therapy

- First line for malignant Brenner tumour: surgical staging/cytoreduction, plus adjuvant platinum-based systemic chemotherapy when indicated by stage or risk features, as for other epithelial ovarian cancers (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Beyond first line: for recurrent or progressive malignant Brenner tumour there is no dedicated evidence base; management should follow recurrent epithelial ovarian cancer principles (further platinum-based or non-platinum systemic therapy depending on treatment-free interval) with case-by-case oncology input (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Monitoring

- Benign Brenner tumour: no oncologic surveillance is needed beyond routine postoperative follow-up once benign histology is confirmed (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Borderline Brenner tumour: periodic clinical and imaging follow-up (pelvic examination and ultrasound) is reasonable given the small but real potential for late recurrence described for borderline ovarian tumours generally (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Malignant Brenner tumour: surveillance follows standard epithelial ovarian cancer protocols, combining clinical review, tumour marker trends (for example CA125), and imaging as clinically indicated (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## When to refer

- Any solid or complex adnexal mass suspicious for a borderline or malignant Brenner tumour on imaging (a predominantly solid, often calcified mass, especially with rapid growth, ascites, or suspected extra-ovarian spread) should be referred to gynaecologic oncology before definitive surgery, consistent with general practice for any suspicious ovarian mass.
- Confirmed malignant Brenner tumour on final histology, or any tumour with stromal invasion or high-grade cytological atypia identified intraoperatively or on frozen section, warrants gynaecologic oncology referral for complete staging and consideration of adjuvant therapy.
- Post-menopausal bleeding associated with a Brenner tumour (reflecting hormonally active, oestrogen-secreting stroma) warrants endometrial assessment and gynaecology input in parallel with management of the ovarian mass.

Sources: DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of Oncology, 12th ed.
