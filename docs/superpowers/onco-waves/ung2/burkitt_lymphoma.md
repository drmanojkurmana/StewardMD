# Burkitt lymphoma (MYC) - management expansion

## Overall approach

Burkitt lymphoma (BL) is a haematological emergency at diagnosis because of its extremely high proliferative rate (Ki-67 approaching 100 percent) and because tumour lysis can begin spontaneously before any chemotherapy is given. Once biopsy-confirmed, treatment intent is curative in essentially all adult patients and should not be delayed for exhaustive staging. Adults with BL are treated with regimens originally designed for paediatric populations, since BL in adults behaves biologically the same as childhood BL.

A standard diffuse large B-cell lymphoma-type regimen with only intrathecal methotrexate added should be regarded as insufficient treatment for BL; short-duration, dose-dense, multi-agent chemoimmunotherapy with built-in central nervous system (CNS) prophylaxis is the standard of care.

## Lines of therapy

- First line: an intensive, short-course, multi-agent regimen from the Magrath-type family (combining an alkylating agent, an anthracycline, a vinca alkaloid, high-dose methotrexate, and ifosfamide/etoposide/cytarabine components) or a dose-adjusted continuous-infusion anthracycline-based regimen, in both cases built around a defined, closely spaced cycle structure rather than a standard three-weekly schedule. Both regimen families were developed specifically for BL's biology and both incorporate systemic and intrathecal CNS-directed therapy.
- The addition of the anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody rituximab to chemotherapy is now considered standard of care in BL; a randomised phase III trial showed rituximab addition improved event-free survival.
- Duration is guided by response: therapy is continued for a defined number of cycles past confirmed complete response rather than a fixed total cycle count decided up front.
- Relapsed/refractory disease has a poor outcome in adults; autologous stem-cell transplant rarely cures adults who relapse after primary therapy. Given this, relapsed patients should be referred early for a clinical trial or transplant-centre evaluation rather than managed with sequential standard salvage regimens alone.

## Role of surgery and radiotherapy

The DeVita section reviewed for this disease does not describe a role for radiotherapy in BL, and does not support surgery as a primary or adjunct curative modality; surgery's role is limited to obtaining diagnostic tissue and to managing an acute surgical complication (for example bowel obstruction or perforation from bulky abdominal disease) (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease). No radiotherapy claim is made here because it was not grounded in the reviewed text.

## Special populations and disease settings

- Endemic (African) BL classically presents as a jaw or facial bone mass and is strongly Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) associated; sporadic BL classically presents as bulky abdominal disease with ascites and renal, testicular, or ovarian involvement; immunodeficiency-associated BL (notably HIV) more often presents with nodal disease and peripheral blood involvement. All three forms spread preferentially to bone marrow and CNS, so staging and prophylaxis must address both compartments regardless of presentation.
- HIV-associated BL should prompt HIV testing where status is unknown, with antiretroviral therapy started or optimised alongside lymphoma treatment (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- A validated prognostic index for BL (the Burkitt Lymphoma International Prognostic Index) stratifies patients using age over 40, performance status greater than 2, elevated LDH, and CNS involvement, separating groups with markedly different overall survival; this can help set expectations and intensity of monitoring but should not itself be used to withhold standard curative-intent therapy.

## Monitoring

- Before and during induction: aggressive tumour lysis syndrome prophylaxis and monitoring, including hydration, urate-lowering therapy, and frequent electrolyte and renal-function checks, since spontaneous and treatment-induced tumour lysis is a defining early risk in BL.
- Baseline and interim assessment of bone marrow and cerebrospinal fluid to define extent of disease and guide CNS-directed therapy.
- Response assessment with imaging and repeat marrow/CSF evaluation as clinically indicated to confirm complete response, since treatment duration in the regimens above is response-adapted.
- Supportive-care monitoring for cytopenias, infection, and mucositis given the intensity of these regimens.

## When to refer

- All suspected or confirmed BL should be referred urgently to a specialist haemato-oncology centre experienced in delivering intensive BL-specific regimens; this is not disease to manage with a generic lymphoma protocol in a non-specialist setting.
- Refer urgently for renal/critical-care input if tumour lysis syndrome or acute kidney injury develops.
- Refer relapsed or refractory disease early to a transplant/clinical-trial centre given the poor salvage outcomes described above.
- Fertility-preservation counselling should be offered before treatment starts wherever the treatment timeline allows it (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Deliberately omitted

No specific claim about radiotherapy's role, and no numeric doses, cycle counts, or response-rate statistics beyond what is stated qualitatively above, were included, because they either were not found in the reviewed DeVita section or would have required reproducing numeric dosing/schedule information excluded by policy.
