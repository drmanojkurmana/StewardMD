# Chordoma: Management Narrative

## Overview and treatment intent

Chordoma is a rare, locally destructive, slow-growing malignant bone tumour arising along the notochordal axis (skull base/clivus, mobile spine, sacrum). Because gross total resection with wide margins is frequently not achievable, especially at the skull base, treatment is built around a combination of maximal safe surgical resection and adjuvant high-dose radiotherapy; there are only limited systemic therapy options. There are no validated clinical or molecular prognostic factors that reliably predict recurrence, so essentially all patients require a long surgical/radiotherapy-led course followed by prolonged surveillance rather than a fixed number of discrete "lines" of therapy in the way used for chemosensitive cancers.

Chondrosarcoma is discussed alongside chordoma because the two are sometimes pooled at the skull base and can be difficult to distinguish without immunohistochemistry (chordoma is keratin- and epithelial-membrane-antigen-positive; chondrosarcoma stains for S100 and is typically keratin-negative). Chondrosarcoma has a more favourable long-term outcome than chordoma and, for low-grade skull base disease, deferring immediate postoperative radiotherapy in favour of MRI surveillance after maximal safe resection is a reasonable, safe strategy, since a similar 10-year disease-free survival can be achieved in both groups because of successful salvage treatment at progression.

## Role of surgery

Surgery is the backbone of treatment. It is essentially obligatory: it provides diagnostic tissue, improves the effectiveness of subsequent radiotherapy by reducing tumour bulk (particularly around the optic apparatus and brain stem, allowing safer delivery of very high radiation doses), and improves the patient's clinical condition. An aggressive initial surgical approach may improve overall outcome, and complete resection, if achievable, is the optimal goal. Skull base approaches typically involve combined neurosurgical and otolaryngology teams, with growing use of endoscopic approaches in appropriately selected patients. The extent of resection correlates with both recurrence and survival, but surgical morbidity is significant, and dural invasion (occurring in roughly half of cranial chordomas) can complicate resection with cerebrospinal fluid leak as a recognised risk. When cranial chordomas recur after surgery and radiotherapy, reoperation aimed at symptomatic improvement is often the only remaining option, though reoperation carries higher complication rates because of prior surgical scarring and radiation change.

For chondrosarcoma, because the paramedian location differs from midline chordoma and tumour control with adjuvant high-dose radiation is high, complete excision is less critical than it is for chordoma; surgery is often tailored to debulk portions abutting critical structures (optic chiasm, brain stem) to allow safe, effective radiotherapy.

## Role of radiotherapy

Because radical excision with negative margins is often not feasible, adjuvant radiotherapy is a mainstay of treatment following resection, and can also be used as primary treatment when surgery is not possible. Local control of chordoma is dose-dependent, and particle beam therapy (proton or carbon-ion) is regarded as the most effective radiotherapy modality for skull base and spinal chordoma, since it allows delivery of the higher doses needed for this radioresistant tumour type while sparing adjacent critical structures (brain stem, spinal cord, optic apparatus) compared with photon plans. Favourable prognostic factors for local control include a smaller residual tumour volume, adequate target dose coverage, and absence of optic apparatus compression.

Highly conformal single-session stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) is an option for small-volume residual or recurrent skull base or spinal chordoma, used as an adjuvant or salvage treatment where the disease volume is limited and critical structures can be spared.

## Role of systemic (medical) therapy

Systemic options for chordoma are limited and are reserved for advanced, recurrent, or metastatic disease rather than being part of routine primary management:
- A tyrosine kinase inhibitor (imatinib) has shown a meaningful clinical benefit rate (disease control including stable disease) in advanced chordoma in a phase II study, and patients who progress after an initial response to imatinib alone may respond to imatinib combined with an mTOR inhibitor (sirolimus).
- For IDH1-mutant chondrosarcoma specifically, a selective mutant-IDH1 inhibitor (ivosidenib) produced a period of progression-free disease in a phase I study of advanced disease; this applies to chondrosarcoma, not chordoma.
- Emerging biological rationale supports investigational use of PARP inhibition (based on defective homologous-recombination DNA repair described in some heavily pretreated advanced recurrent sacral chordomas) and immune checkpoint inhibition (based on PD-L1 expression and tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes described in spinal chordoma); these remain under clinical trial evaluation rather than established standard practice.
- There is no established cytotoxic chemotherapy regimen with proven benefit in chordoma; DeVita's section does not describe a standard chemotherapy backbone for this disease.

## Monitoring and surveillance

Given the tendency for chordoma to recur locally (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease: the tendency to recur after a prolonged disease-free interval and to metastasise late is well-established chordoma biology but is not stated in the grounded DeVita management passage), long-term surveillance with cross-sectional imaging (MRI of the primary site) is required, together with a routine baseline chest x-ray; because of the low risk of nodal or haematogenous spread, further staging imaging beyond the primary site and the chest x-ray is not typically required unless metastatic disease is suspected clinically (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease: routine periodic surveillance imaging intervals and total duration of follow-up are not specified in the grounded section, so no specific schedule is given here). A baseline endocrine evaluation and neuro-ophthalmologic examination are recommended for skull base tumours where imaging or symptoms suggest involvement of these structures, and this should be revisited as part of ongoing follow-up when relevant. For low-grade skull base chondrosarcoma managed with surveillance alone after resection, follow-up MRI surveillance is the explicit alternative strategy to immediate postoperative radiotherapy.

## When to refer

Chordoma and chondrosarcoma should be managed within a specialist multidisciplinary team from the point of diagnosis, because of the technical demands of resection and the need for specialised radiotherapy planning:
- Skull base disease needs joint neurosurgical and otolaryngology input, with access to endoscopic skull base expertise where appropriate.
- Access to particle beam (proton or carbon-ion) radiotherapy should be sought given its role as the preferred adjuvant modality for skull base and spinal disease; this is a specialised resource not universally available, so early referral to a centre offering it is appropriate.
- Recurrent, advanced, or metastatic disease being considered for systemic therapy (imatinib-based regimens, or clinical trials of PARP inhibition or checkpoint inhibition) should be referred to a centre with sarcoma/neuro-oncology expertise and access to relevant trials.
- Suspected familial chordoma warrants referral for genetic counselling (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease: the germline TBXT-related susceptibility mechanism is established genetics literature but is not described in the grounded DeVita passage for this disease).
