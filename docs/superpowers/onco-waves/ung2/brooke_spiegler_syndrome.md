# Brooke-Spiegler Syndrome (CYLD Cutaneous Syndrome) - Management

FLAG: needs manual sourcing. Brooke-Spiegler syndrome (and its allelic spectrum, familial
cylindromatosis and multiple familial trichoepithelioma) is not covered as an oncology
chapter topic in DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles and Practice of
Oncology. It is a rare adnexal skin-tumour genodermatosis managed mainly by
dermatology/dermatologic surgery rather than medical or radiation oncology, and DeVita's
text does not contain a section addressing it. Every claim below is therefore marked as
general oncology/dermatology standard rather than DeVita-sourced.

## Overall approach (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease)

Brooke-Spiegler syndrome is a chronic, non-malignant condition in the great majority of
patients: the driving problem is a lifelong burden of multiple benign adnexal tumours
(cylindromas, spiradenomas, trichoepitheliomas, sometimes combined as
spiradenocylindromas) rather than invasive cancer. Management intent is therefore mostly
symptom control, disfigurement prevention, and surveillance for the uncommon event of
malignant transformation, not systemic anticancer treatment. There is no defined
"stage" of this syndrome in the way a solid tumour is staged; treatment intensity is
driven instead by lesion number, size, site, growth rate, and functional/cosmetic impact.

## Local (definitive) treatment - the primary modality

- Surgical excision is the mainstay for lesions that are symptomatic, enlarging, ulcerating,
  or cosmetically significant. For confluent scalp cylindromas forming "turban tumours,"
  this can mean wide scalp excision with skin grafting or flap reconstruction.
- Recurrent lesions and new lesions arising over years are expected given the underlying
  germline predisposition, so patients typically undergo repeated excisions over a lifetime
  rather than a single curative operation.
- For numerous smaller facial or scalp lesions where wide excision is impractical, ablative/
  destructive techniques are used for debulking and cosmesis: carbon-dioxide laser ablation,
  electrosurgery/electrodesiccation, dermabrasion, and similar destructive modalities. These
  reduce lesion burden but do not address the underlying genetic driver and recurrence is
  common.
- Radiation therapy is not a standard treatment for the benign tumours of this syndrome and,
  if anything, should be used cautiously given case reports of radiation as a possible trigger
  for later malignant transformation in adnexal tumours; RT's role is essentially limited to
  the rare confirmed malignant transformation case, following standard skin-cancer RT
  principles.

## Systemic (medical) therapy - limited and reserved for malignant transformation

- There is no established systemic medical or targeted therapy for the benign tumours of
  Brooke-Spiegler syndrome itself.
- Systemic therapy only becomes relevant if a lesion undergoes malignant transformation
  (for example into a cylindrocarcinoma/malignant cylindroma, spiradenocarcinoma, or
  basal-cell-carcinoma-like malignancy). In that uncommon scenario, treatment follows the
  same principles used for the corresponding sporadic cutaneous adnexal or skin malignancy
  (surgery as primary treatment, with systemic therapy considered only for unresectable or
  metastatic disease), rather than any regimen specific to this syndrome.
- Because the CYLD gene product normally restrains NF-kB signalling, CYLD loss upregulates
  NF-kB-driven survival signalling in these tumours; this is a plausible biological rationale
  for interest in NF-kB pathway-directed approaches, but no established, clinically validated
  targeted drug regimen for CYLD-mutant tumours can be cited here without inventing a claim,
  so none is given.

## Monitoring and surveillance

- Lifelong dermatological follow-up is required because new lesions continue to appear
  through adult life and existing lesions can slowly enlarge.
- Any lesion that changes behaviour, rapid growth, ulceration, bleeding, or new pain or
  neurological symptoms from a deep/large tumour, should prompt biopsy to exclude malignant
  transformation, since this is the one recognised (if uncommon) life-threatening
  complication of the syndrome.
- Because the condition is autosomal dominant, first-degree relatives should be offered
  clinical skin surveillance and CYLD germline genetic counselling/testing, with screening
  extended to relatives found to carry the mutation.

## When to refer

- Refer to dermatology/dermatologic surgery for diagnosis confirmation, biopsy, and staged
  management of tumour burden.
- Refer for plastic/reconstructive surgery input when confluent scalp disease requires wide
  excision and grafting or flap closure.
- Refer to clinical genetics for germline CYLD testing and cascade family counselling.
- Refer to medical/surgical oncology only if biopsy confirms malignant transformation of a
  lesion, at which point management follows standard cutaneous malignancy pathways for the
  specific transformed histology.
- Psychological or psychosocial support referral should be considered given the chronic
  disfigurement and repeated surgery this condition can involve.

Sources: NCCN Guidelines (general cutaneous malignancy management principles, applied by
analogy only where a lesion transforms; no NCCN guideline exists specifically for Brooke-
Spiegler syndrome). DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles and Practice of
Oncology, 12th ed. was searched and contains no dedicated section on this syndrome or its
synonyms (Brooke-Spiegler syndrome, CYLD cutaneous syndrome, familial cylindromatosis,
turban tumour syndrome); it is cited here only to document that absence, not as a source
for any claim above.
