# Management: Desmoplastic infantile ganglioglioma (DIG) / desmoplastic infantile astrocytoma (DIA)

## Sourcing note
DeVita's oncology text does not carry a dedicated section on desmoplastic infantile ganglioglioma or its astrocytic counterpart (DIA); a grep for the disease name and its synonyms across the text returned no matches. DeVita does carry a general "Gangliogliomas" section (a related but distinct WHO grade I-II glioneuronal entity, typically presenting later than infancy). Because DIG/DIA is the infantile member of the same glioneuronal family and its surgical-first management philosophy overlaps with what DeVita describes for gangliogliomas generally, that section is used below for the principles it actually supports (surgery as the mainstay, radiotherapy's limited and STR-driven role, and chemotherapy's reserved/salvage role), with each claim marked as coming from the general ganglioglioma section rather than a DIG-specific one. Anything specific to DIG's infantile biology, BRAF-targeted therapy in this entity, or hydrocephalus/ICP management is marked as general oncology standard, since DeVita's ganglioglioma section does not address it.

## Treatment approach by presentation

DIG/DIA is a WHO grade 1 tumour of infancy with an overwhelmingly benign clinical course once treated, so the approach is fundamentally surgical rather than staged by "intent" in the way used for malignant tumours (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

- **Newly diagnosed, resectable disease (the great majority of cases):** upfront maximal safe surgical resection is the treatment of first choice. In the closely related ganglioglioma family, DeVita's ganglioglioma section states that gross total resection provides long-term tumour control in the large majority of cases and is the preferred treatment for both newly diagnosed and recurrent disease, and that surgery alone is often curative because these astroglial-variant tumours rarely progress to a higher grade. The same surgery-first, resection-is-usually-curative philosophy applies to DIG/DIA (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease, extrapolated from the adjacent ganglioglioma entity).
- **Unresectable, recurrent, or progressive disease:** when a complete resection cannot be achieved safely (for example because of eloquent-region involvement or dural/leptomeningeal extent), options move toward molecular-directed therapy and, less often, radiotherapy or chemotherapy (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Role of surgery

Surgery is the backbone of management. For the ganglioglioma family, DeVita notes that resection is directed at the contrast-enhancing portion of the tumour, that lesions in eloquent regions can be technically challenging because the tumour-brain boundary is not always distinct even with modern surgical adjuncts (operating microscope, computer-assisted navigation, functional mapping), and that complete resection is associated with very favourable long-term survival; resection can also achieve seizure control. Applied to DIG/DIA specifically: gross total resection is the mainstay and is typically curative, and perioperative attention is needed for raised intracranial pressure and hydrocephalus (with cerebrospinal fluid diversion or shunting where required) given the typical infantile presentation with a large mass and tense fontanelle (general oncology standard for the ICP/hydrocephalus and infantile-specific points, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Role of radiotherapy

DeVita's ganglioglioma section describes a limited and mostly salvage or subtotal-resection-driven role for radiotherapy in this tumour family: there is no clear consensus on adjuvant radiation, and it is generally considered after subtotal resection, particularly in eloquent locations where further surgery risks significant morbidity; retrospective data suggest a local-control benefit from adjuvant radiation after subtotal resection. Stereotactic radiosurgery has been used for limited recurrence after resection. Given that DIG/DIA occurs in infancy, where radiotherapy carries a markedly higher risk of long-term neurocognitive and developmental harm, radiotherapy is not first-line and would only be considered in the rare setting of progressive, unresectable, treatment-refractory disease, and only after other options (repeat surgery, targeted therapy) have been exhausted (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease, reflecting the infantile-specific radiotherapy-avoidance principle that does not appear in DeVita's ganglioglioma text).

## Role of systemic and targeted therapy

For the ganglioglioma family, DeVita states that chemotherapy is generally reserved for young children who have undergone subtotal resection and show disease progression, and in older patients is used as salvage after failure of surgery and radiotherapy; no optimal chemotherapy regimen has been defined for these astroglial-variant tumours, and disease stabilisation (rather than tumour response) is considered a successful outcome. This reserved, progression-driven role for chemotherapy is consistent with how DIG/DIA is managed: chemotherapy is reserved for progressive or incompletely resected tumours rather than used routinely.

A meaningful proportion of DIG/DIA cases carry MAPK-pathway alterations, most notably BRAF V600E. Molecular testing for BRAF V600E and broader MAPK-pathway alterations should be performed, and in unresectable or recurrent disease this can guide the use of a targeted BRAF inhibitor or MEK inhibitor as an alternative to conventional chemotherapy or radiotherapy (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease; DeVita's ganglioglioma section notes BRAF V600E as a diagnostic marker present in a substantial minority of gangliogliomas but does not describe BRAF-targeted treatment for this family).

## Monitoring

- Postoperative surveillance brain MRI to confirm extent of resection and to detect recurrence.
- Serial head circumference measurement and formal developmental assessment given the infantile presentation and the risk that even a benign tumour or its treatment can affect neurodevelopment.
- Seizure surveillance and antiepileptic therapy where seizures have occurred.
(General oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease.)

## When to refer / escalate

- All suspected or confirmed cases should be managed within a paediatric neuro-oncology multidisciplinary team (paediatric neurosurgery, paediatric neuro-oncology/neurology, and rehabilitation services).
- Escalate to the multidisciplinary team for incomplete (subtotal) resection, tumour recurrence or progression, new or worsening seizures, or signs of raised intracranial pressure (rapidly increasing head circumference, tense fontanelle, vomiting, setting-sun sign), so that repeat surgery, molecular testing, or targeted/adjuvant therapy can be considered promptly.
(General oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease.)

## What was deliberately omitted

No numeric radiation dose, chemotherapy regimen, or trial-derived survival statistic specific to DIG/DIA is included, because DeVita has no dedicated section on this entity and no such DIG/DIA-specific figures were found elsewhere in the grounding text; the dose and outcome figures that do appear in DeVita's text (radiation doses, local-control and overall-survival percentages) belong to the general ganglioglioma section and were excluded rather than misattributed to this specific infantile entity.
