# Oral Cancer (Oral Cavity Squamous Cell Carcinoma) — Management

## Overview and initial workup

More than 90 to 95 percent of oral cavity tumours are squamous cell carcinoma, and management is
organised around treating the primary tumour and the neck (the lymphatic drainage) together.
Every patient should have a thorough history and examination, including flexible laryngoscopy to
assess the airway and to look for a second primary tumour, and the primary lesion should be
biopsied in clinic. Staging imaging should cover the oral cavity, neck, and chest, most commonly
with contrast-enhanced CT of the maxillofacial region, neck, and chest, or a PET-CT with a
diagnostic neck CT; MRI is reserved for cases where dental artefact obscures CT, where perineural
invasion is suspected, or where the extent of marrow involvement from bone invasion needs
clarification. Once staging is complete, every case should be discussed at a multidisciplinary tumour
board including head and neck surgeons, radiation oncologists, medical oncologists, radiologists, and
pathologists, ideally alongside dentists, speech-language pathologists, dieticians, and physical
therapists, so that a consensus treatment recommendation can be reached before the patient is
counselled. Any oral lesion that fails to heal within a few weeks should be biopsied rather than
treated repeatedly as trauma or infection, and premalignant lesions such as leukoplakia,
erythroplakia, and oral submucous fibrosis warrant ongoing surveillance and biopsy when they change.

## Treatment approach by stage and intent

The great majority of oral cavity cancer is best treated with primary surgery. Radiotherapy is
typically used in the adjuvant setting after surgery, and chemotherapy is added to radiotherapy in
selected cases rather than used as a routine third arm.

- **Early lesions (T1 to T2, superficial):** Surgical resection (for example partial glossectomy for
  tongue lesions, wide local excision with neck dissection for floor-of-mouth lesions) is generally
  preferred over primary radiotherapy for most oral cavity subsites, because primary radiotherapy
  carries a comparatively high risk of soft tissue or bone necrosis, dental damage, and severe
  xerostomia. For some early lip lesions, surgery and radiotherapy can be equally curative and the
  choice is guided by cosmetic and functional considerations.
- **Moderately advanced lesions (T2 to T3):** Preferred treatment is resection of the primary
  (glossectomy, partial excision, or marginal/segmental mandibulectomy as needed for bone
  involvement), neck dissection, reconstruction, and postoperative radiotherapy or
  chemoradiotherapy guided by pathological findings.
- **Advanced lesions (T4):** Combined-modality treatment (extensive resection, which may include
  near-total or total glossectomy, segmental mandibulectomy, or laryngectomy, plus neck dissection,
  reconstruction, and postoperative radiotherapy or chemoradiotherapy) is required. With aggressive
  multimodal treatment many patients with T4 disease can still be cured, though functional outcomes
  (speech, swallowing) are more variable and patient selection matters.
- **Lip cancer specifically:** Early lower lip lesions up to about 2 cm not involving the commissure
  are usually managed by simple surgical excision with excellent cosmetic results; radiotherapy is a
  reasonable alternative for lesions involving the commissure, lesions over 2 cm, or upper lip
  carcinomas. Advanced lip lesions with bone, nerve, or nodal involvement need resection, neck
  dissection, reconstruction, and adjuvant radiotherapy, with chemotherapy considered alongside
  radiotherapy for more advanced disease.

Postoperative radiotherapy, with concurrent chemotherapy added when pathological findings warrant
it (chemoradiotherapy), is the general pattern for adverse features after resection across oral cavity
subsites (moderately advanced and advanced floor-of-mouth, tongue, alveolar ridge, and hard palate
cancers). DeVita's oral cavity chapter does not spell out a single explicit checklist of which
pathological features trigger postoperative chemoradiotherapy versus radiotherapy alone; the decision
is described as being based on "pathologic findings" and made at tumour board. Positive margins are
treated as an indication for further resection when feasible (frozen section control during surgery is
used specifically to catch this).

Definitive (non-surgical) chemoradiotherapy or radiotherapy alone can be considered for locally
advanced disease when a patient cannot tolerate an extensive operation or when function-preserving
surgery is not feasible, but surgery-first remains the default approach for resectable oral cavity
disease. DeVita's oral cavity chapter is focused on surgery and radiotherapy technique and does not
detail systemic therapy regimens or lines of therapy for recurrent or metastatic oral cancer; management
of recurrent, unresectable, or metastatic disease with systemic chemotherapy, EGFR-targeted therapy,
or immunotherapy follows general head and neck squamous cell carcinoma principles and is
(general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Role of surgery

Surgery is the primary modality for most resectable oral cavity cancer. Extent of resection is guided
by subsite and depth of invasion: partial glossectomy for tongue lesions, wide local excision with
marginal or segmental mandibulectomy where bone is threatened or invaded, and free-flap
reconstruction once defects become large enough to compromise function. Frozen-section margin
control during resection is used to catch positive margins intraoperatively, since positive margins are
an indication for further excision.

## Role of neck (lymph node) management

Cervical nodal metastasis is common in oral cancer, upstages the patient to at least stage III on its
own, and roughly halves overall survival even with a single involved node, so neck evaluation and
treatment are integral to management, not an afterthought.

- Depth of invasion (DOI) predicts occult nodal disease and is now incorporated into AJCC staging.
  Tumours with DOI greater than 2 mm carry a meaningfully increased risk of occult neck disease and
  should be considered for at least ipsilateral elective neck dissection; T3 tumours with DOI greater
  than 10 mm should additionally be considered for elective contralateral neck treatment. In practice
  this means essentially all T2 to T4a cancers with a clinically negative neck (cN0) warrant at least
  ipsilateral elective neck treatment.
- For a clinically node-negative (cN0) neck, elective treatment should address levels I, IIa, and III at
  minimum, with levels IIb and IV strongly considered.
- For a clinically node-positive (cN+) neck, treatment should include levels I through IV, adding level V
  when disease is multilevel.
- Bilateral neck treatment is recommended when the tumour originates in or extends to the floor of
  mouth, extends into the soft palate or base of tongue, lies within 1 cm of the midline, or has DOI
  greater than 10 mm; disease confined to the retromolar trigone, hard palate, alveolus, or buccal
  mucosa without midline extension can often be treated with ipsilateral neck management alone.
- An adequate level I to III neck dissection should yield at least 18 lymph nodes; a lower yield is
  associated with worse locoregional control and should prompt consideration of adjuvant radiotherapy
  or closer observation.
- The number of positive nodes, nodal size, laterality, and presence of extranodal extension are all
  incorporated into staging and prognosis, and drive the decision for postoperative neck irradiation in
  addition to dissection.

## Monitoring and follow-up

After treatment, distinguishing recurrence from treatment effect is a recognised diagnostic challenge.
An ulcer appearing within the original tumour bed within the first two years after radiotherapy may
represent either recurrence or soft tissue necrosis; if it looks like necrosis, a trial of conservative
management is reasonable, but failure to resolve, or any lesion with a rolled or elevated border, should
prompt biopsy, and a single negative biopsy does not rule out recurrence if suspicion remains high (deep
or repeat biopsy may be needed). After surgery, follow-up examination can be harder to interpret where
skin grafts or flaps have altered the local tissue. Recurrence after radiotherapy is generally salvaged by
surgery; recurrence after surgery is occasionally salvaged by re-resection with postoperative
radiotherapy-based treatment, while recurrence within the soft tissues of the neck is rarely curable by
any further procedure. New nodal disease appearing in a previously untreated neck is managed with
neck dissection followed by postoperative radiotherapy or chemoradiotherapy.

## When to refer

Any persistent oral ulcer, red or white patch, or non-healing sore that does not resolve within a few
weeks needs biopsy rather than continued empirical treatment, and any patient with a biopsy-proven
oral squamous cell carcinoma, or a firm/fixed/enlarging cervical node, should be referred promptly to a
head and neck multidisciplinary tumour board (head and neck surgery, radiation oncology, and medical
oncology) for staging and a consensus treatment plan, with dental assessment and nutritional evaluation
arranged before treatment, particularly before radiotherapy, given the risks of osteoradionecrosis and
xerostomia.
