# Neuroendocrine tumour (NET/NEN): management narrative

Neuroendocrine tumours are a heterogeneous family arising in the gastrointestinal tract, pancreas
and lungs, spanning indolent well-differentiated tumours through to aggressive, poorly
differentiated neuroendocrine carcinomas. Tumour grade (mitotic count and Ki-67 index) and
differentiation are the central determinants of treatment approach, more so than site alone, so the
plan below is organised by grade/stage/intent rather than by a single "one size fits all" pathway.

## Localised, well-differentiated disease

- Surgical resection is the only potentially curative approach for localised well-differentiated
  NETs and should be pursued in medically fit patients.
- For pancreatic NETs specifically, consensus guidance (AHPBA/ENETS, as summarised in DeVita)
  recommends resection for localised functional or symptomatic tumours regardless of size, and for
  non-functioning pancreatic NETs of 2 cm or larger; non-functioning tumours of 1 cm or smaller can
  reasonably be observed with interval imaging, and management of the 1-2 cm group is genuinely
  unsettled and individualised by age, comorbidity and growth on surveillance imaging.
- For resectable pulmonary carcinoid (stage I-III), surgical resection is the recommended approach;
  patients who are not surgical candidates are considered for stereotactic ablative radiotherapy,
  particularly for early-stage peripheral tumours. No adjuvant therapy has shown benefit after a
  complete (R0) resection; adjuvant chemotherapy may be considered when there is nodal (N2)
  involvement or aggressive histologic features, and an incomplete (R1) resection warrants referral
  to radiation oncology to discuss adjuvant radiotherapy.
- There is currently no established role for adjuvant systemic therapy after resection of localised
  pancreatic NETs outside a clinical trial; a randomised trial comparing surgery alone with
  surgery plus adjuvant chemotherapy in higher-risk well-differentiated pancreatic NETs was
  actively accruing at the time DeVita was written, underscoring that this remains an open question
  rather than standard practice.

## Advanced/metastatic well-differentiated NET

There is no single systemic therapy that reliably produces disease regression in this group; choice
is sequenced by receptor status, tumour burden, symptom burden and rate of progression.

- **Somatostatin analogues** (octreotide, lanreotide) are first-line for progressive metastatic
  disease, particularly midgut and enteropancreatic tumours, and also control hormonal symptoms.
  Randomised placebo-controlled data (PROMID for octreotide in midgut NETs, and a separate
  placebo-controlled lanreotide trial in enteropancreatic NETs) showed significant prolongation of
  time to tumour progression, without a demonstrated overall survival benefit; long-acting
  somatostatin analogues are considered appropriate first-line therapy for progressive metastatic
  midgut NETs regardless of whether carcinoid syndrome is present. Benefit appears greatest with
  lower hepatic tumour burden and a resected primary.
- **Peptide receptor radionuclide therapy (PRRT)** with a lutetium-177-labelled somatostatin
  analogue is an option for somatostatin-receptor-positive disease that progresses on a
  somatostatin analogue; a phase III trial (NETTER-1) demonstrated a significant progression-free
  survival benefit over high-dose octreotide in advanced somatostatin-receptor-positive GI NETs,
  leading to regulatory approval for gastroenteropancreatic NETs. Smaller studies suggest similar
  activity in bronchial carcinoid, though this is outside the labelled indication.
- **mTOR-directed therapy (everolimus)** has randomised evidence of progression-free survival
  benefit both in combination with octreotide (in low/intermediate-grade carcinoids) and as
  monotherapy versus placebo in non-functional lung and GI NETs, and is an approved option,
  including specifically for pulmonary NETs and for progressive pancreatic NETs.
- **Cytotoxic chemotherapy** has a limited but real role. Regimens used for small-cell lung cancer
  are sometimes extrapolated to advanced, symptomatic bronchial carcinoid for lack of alternatives,
  with objective response rates in the range of 20-30% in small series; a combination of
  temozolomide with capecitabine (CAP-TEM) has shown high response and disease-control rates in
  pulmonary carcinoid and pancreatic NETs metastatic to the liver in the literature reviewed. For
  pancreatic NETs, cytotoxic chemotherapy is a recognised systemic option alongside somatostatin
  analogues and everolimus.
- **Immunotherapy** (checkpoint inhibitors) has been studied in well-differentiated thoracic
  carcinoid and other NETs, with response rates that vary substantially between trials and remain
  modest overall; it is not an established standard of care for well-differentiated NET and is best
  considered investigational or reserved for select refractory cases.
- **Liver-directed therapy** (resection, thermal ablation, transarterial bland embolisation,
  chemoembolisation or radioembolisation) is used for liver-dominant metastatic burden, either
  alone or alongside systemic therapy.
- Asymptomatic patients with low-volume, slowly progressive metastatic disease can reasonably be
  followed with active surveillance (imaging and clinical review) before starting systemic therapy,
  reserving treatment for symptomatic or radiologic progression.

## Poorly differentiated neuroendocrine carcinoma

Poorly differentiated neuroendocrine carcinomas behave like aggressive high-grade malignancies
rather than indolent NETs and are managed with platinum-based chemotherapy rather than the
somatostatin-analogue/mTOR-inhibitor pathway used for well-differentiated disease. (general
oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease, though the general principle that
grade drives treatment intensity is explicitly grounded in the reviewed material).

## Carcinoid syndrome, carcinoid crisis and carcinoid heart disease

- Carcinoid syndrome symptoms (flushing, diarrhoea, wheeze) are treated first with somatostatin
  analogues; tachyphylaxis can develop after several months and can often be managed by dose
  escalation.
- For diarrhoea inadequately controlled on a somatostatin analogue, telotristat, an inhibitor of
  peripheral serotonin synthesis, has randomised placebo-controlled evidence of reducing bowel
  movement frequency and urinary serotonin metabolite excretion, and is a recognised add-on option.
  Bile-acid binders, pancreatic enzyme supplementation, antibacterial therapy for overgrowth, and
  anti-motility agents (loperamide, tincture of opium) address diarrhoea through complementary
  mechanisms.
- Any invasive procedure, surgery or anaesthesia in a patient with carcinoid syndrome carries a
  risk of life-threatening carcinoid crisis (flushing, hypotension, bronchospasm, arrhythmia).
  Perioperative somatostatin analogue cover before the procedure is standard to prevent this, and
  an evolving crisis is managed by pausing the procedure, giving intravenous fluids under
  haemodynamic monitoring, and further somatostatin analogue and corticosteroid dosing.
  Adrenergic agents should be avoided as they can precipitate mediator release.
- Carcinoid heart disease (tricuspid and pulmonary valve fibrosis from serotonin-driven collagen
  deposition) develops in a substantial minority of patients with carcinoid syndrome and correlates
  with higher urinary 5-HIAA excretion. Baseline and periodic echocardiographic screening is
  recommended, and patients with significant valvular disease should be referred early to
  cardiology and cardiac surgery for consideration of valve replacement.

## Monitoring

- Chromogranin A and, for serotonin-secreting tumours, urinary 5-HIAA are used to track disease
  activity, though the correlation with radiologic activity is inconsistent and neither is uniformly
  recommended as a routine surveillance tool by current European and US practice guidelines for
  pulmonary carcinoid specifically.
- Somatostatin-receptor PET imaging (gallium-68 DOTATATE and related tracers) is superior to older
  octreotide scintigraphy for localisation and staging, particularly for liver and bone lesions, and
  also predicts response to somatostatin-receptor-targeted therapy.
- After resection of localised pulmonary carcinoid, surveillance with cross-sectional imaging and
  clinical review roughly every 12 to 24 months is reasonable.
- Patients with carcinoid syndrome and higher 5-HIAA excretion warrant more structured cardiac
  surveillance, as above.

## When to refer

- Refer for surgical evaluation whenever the disease is localised and resection is technically and
  medically feasible, since this remains the only curative option.
- Refer to a specialist neuroendocrine tumour multidisciplinary team for grading-dependent systemic
  therapy sequencing, somatostatin-receptor imaging, and PRRT eligibility, since these decisions
  depend on receptor status and tumour biology.
- Refer to cardiology/cardiac surgery early once significant carcinoid heart valve disease is
  identified, rather than waiting for overt right heart failure.
- Refer to genetics/endocrinology when a hereditary syndrome (MEN1, von Hippel-Lindau,
  neurofibromatosis type 1, tuberous sclerosis) is suspected, since this changes surveillance and
  family screening (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease for the
  screening implications, though the associated syndromes themselves are named in the reviewed
  pancreatic NET section).

Sources: DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of Oncology, 12th ed.
