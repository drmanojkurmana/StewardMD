# Nodal marginal zone lymphoma (NMZL) — management

Nodal marginal zone lymphoma is a rare, indolent B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma (under 1% of all NHL) that is by definition confined to lymph nodes, without the splenic or mucosa-associated (MALT) primary that defines the other two marginal zone lymphomas. Most patients are diagnosed with advanced (stage III/IV) disease, and the majority are asymptomatic at presentation; bone marrow involvement is present in under half of cases, which is less frequent than in most other indolent lymphomas.

## Treatment approach by stage/intent

- **Limited-stage disease.** For the minority of patients with truly localised nodal MZL, involved-site radiotherapy is the treatment of choice, following the same principles used for limited-stage follicular lymphoma.
- **Advanced or symptomatic disease.** Most patients with nodal MZL present with advanced-stage disease. Historically these patients have been treated with chemoimmunotherapy, typically an alkylating agent or a purine analogue combined with the anti-CD20 antibody rituximab; such regimens produce response rates in excess of 80%. A randomised phase III comparison of rituximab-CHOP against bendamustine-rituximab in marginal zone lymphoma (not otherwise specified) showed no significant difference in progression-free survival between the two regimens.
- **Asymptomatic, low-burden disease.** As with other indolent lymphomas, a period of observation (watch-and-wait) is a reasonable option for asymptomatic patients with low tumour burden before committing to systemic therapy (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Role of surgery, radiotherapy, and systemic therapy

- **Surgery** has no defined role in nodal MZL; it is a nodal-only disease and diagnosis is made by lymph node biopsy rather than resection of a primary mass.
- **Radiotherapy** is used with curative intent for limited-stage, localised nodal disease, mirroring limited-stage follicular lymphoma practice.
- **Systemic therapy** is the mainstay for advanced or symptomatic disease:
  - Chemoimmunotherapy (an alkylating agent or purine analogue plus rituximab) remains the historical backbone for previously untreated patients needing treatment.
  - The Bruton tyrosine kinase inhibitor ibrutinib has been studied in relapsed marginal zone lymphoma and produced an overall response rate of roughly half of patients, with a progression-free survival of about a year; it is an approved option for relapsed disease.
  - Zanubrutinib, another BTK inhibitor, has shown activity in relapsed marginal zone lymphoma in a phase II trial, with a majority of patients responding and about a quarter achieving complete response; it is an approved option for relapsed or refractory disease.
  - A PI3K inhibitor (umbralisib) has regulatory approval in the relapsed or refractory marginal zone lymphoma setting based on phase II data.
  - CD19-directed CAR T-cell therapy has been explored in relapsed or refractory marginal zone lymphoma in the third line and beyond, with encouraging response rates in early reporting, though follow-up remains immature.
  - For patients with an associated infection or autoimmune trigger, treating the underlying driver (for example antiviral therapy for hepatitis C, which has induced lymphoma regression in related marginal zone lymphomas) is a reasonable adjunct where relevant (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Lines of therapy

- **First line:** observation for asymptomatic low-burden disease, radiotherapy for truly localised disease, or chemoimmunotherapy (rituximab plus an alkylator or purine analogue) for symptomatic/advanced disease.
- **Relapsed/refractory disease:** BTK inhibitors (ibrutinib or zanubrutinib), a PI3K inhibitor (umbralisib), or re-use of a prior effective chemoimmunotherapy regimen are options; CD19-directed CAR T-cell therapy is being explored for later-line relapsed or refractory disease.

## Monitoring

- Nodal MZL follows an indolent course, but transformation to a more aggressive (large B-cell) lymphoma can occur, as with other indolent lymphomas, and should prompt re-biopsy of any rapidly enlarging or discordant node.
- The reported 5-year survival for nodal MZL is in the range of 55% to 79%, so longitudinal follow-up for both disease progression and histologic transformation is appropriate (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease, for the specifics of a surveillance schedule).

## When to refer

- All patients with nodal MZL should be managed by, or in conjunction with, a haemato-oncology specialist/multidisciplinary team, given the need to confirm the diagnosis by excluding a splenic or extranodal primary, to select between observation, radiotherapy, and systemic therapy, and to access BTK inhibitor, PI3K inhibitor, or CAR T-cell options in the relapsed setting (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Rapid clinical change (abrupt nodal enlargement, new B symptoms, or a rising marker of aggressive disease) should prompt urgent re-biopsy and referral to rule out transformation.
