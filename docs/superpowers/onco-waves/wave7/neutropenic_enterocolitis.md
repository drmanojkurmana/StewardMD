# Neutropenic Enterocolitis (Typhlitis) - Management

Neutropenic enterocolitis is not a solid tumor with a stage/intent framework in the usual sense; it is an acute, potentially life-threatening complication of profound neutropenia following intensive chemotherapy, most often for acute leukemia or in the myeloablative/transplant setting. Management is staged by severity and response to initial therapy rather than by cancer stage.

## Initial approach: medical management first

DeVita describes both medical and surgical roles, but treatment begins medically in essentially all patients:

- Broad-spectrum antibiotics covering enteric gram-negative organisms, gram-positive organisms, and anaerobes. Reasonable initial choices named in the text are antipseudomonal beta-lactam/beta-lactamase-inhibitor monotherapy or a carbapenem, or dual therapy with an antipseudomonal cephalosporin plus an anti-anaerobic agent. Causative organisms cited include Pseudomonas, Staphylococcus aureus, E. coli, and group A Streptococcus.
- Granulocyte colony-stimulating factor support.
- Nasogastric decompression, intravenous fluids, and bowel rest.
- Serial abdominal examinations to track clinical trajectory.

In most patients these measures are sufficient, and symptoms resolve as the neutropenia corrects. This makes hematologic recovery (marrow recovery / neutrophil count) the central "endpoint" driving resolution, rather than a fixed treatment duration.

If the patient fails to respond to antibacterial therapy, antifungal coverage (amphotericin is the agent named) should be added, because concurrent fungemia is common. Blood transfusion may be required because the associated diarrhea is often bloody. Anticholinergic, antidiarrheal, and opioid agents should be avoided, since they can precipitate or worsen ileus.

A specific antidote-based approach applies only to a narrow trigger: if neutropenic enterocolitis develops within about 96 hours of a fluoropyrimidine (e.g. 5-fluorouracil or capecitabine) dose, an antidote agent for fluoropyrimidine toxicity should be given on a defined multi-dose oral schedule (schedule/dose intentionally omitted here; lives in the structured protocol template).

## Role of surgery

Surgical intervention is reserved for a defined set of indications rather than used routinely, and DeVita is explicit that its indications and timing remain controversial:

- Persistent gastrointestinal bleeding after correction of thrombocytopenia and coagulopathy.
- Radiologic evidence of bowel perforation (free intraperitoneal air) or abscess formation.
- Clinical deterioration despite maximal supportive/medical therapy.
- Need to exclude another intra-abdominal process (e.g. bowel obstruction or acute appendicitis).
- Bowel wall thickness greater than roughly 10 mm on imaging has been proposed as an added indication because of the high mortality seen at that thickness.

When exploratory surgery is performed, all grossly necrotic bowel must be resected, typically via right hemicolectomy with ileostomy and mucous fistula; primary anastomosis is generally avoided in this severely immunocompromised population because of an increased risk of anastomotic leak. Failure to remove the necrotic focus is often fatal.

Mortality in patients who fail medical therapy is high, and DeVita notes many of these patients "may not be salvageable" even with surgery; the surgical decision is therefore a balance between avoiding progressive necrosis/perforation/sepsis and the risk of operating on a fragile, coagulopathic, profoundly neutropenic patient.

## Role of radiotherapy and systemic anticancer therapy

DeVita's section on this entity does not describe any role for radiotherapy or for continuing/modifying the causative systemic anticancer regimen as part of acute management; the acute problem is managed as an infectious/inflammatory surgical-medicine emergency, separate from oncologic treatment planning. Any decision about resuming or modifying the chemotherapy regimen that precipitated the episode is an oncologic judgment made once the acute episode has resolved (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Monitoring

- Neutrophil count trend, since resolution tracks marrow recovery.
- Serial abdominal examination for evolving peritonism, distension, or tenderness.
- Repeat imaging (ultrasound is noted elsewhere in the source as useful to follow bowel-wall thickness over time) to track improvement or detect perforation.
- Coagulation status and platelet count, particularly before any consideration of surgery, since bleeding after correcting thrombocytopenia/coagulopathy is itself a surgical trigger if it persists.
- Ongoing surveillance for fungemia if fever persists despite antibacterial therapy.

## When to refer

- Any evidence of perforation (free air), uncontrolled gastrointestinal bleeding, or clinical deterioration despite maximal medical therapy warrants urgent surgical consultation.
- Persistent fever or sepsis unresponsive to broad-spectrum antibacterial therapy warrants reassessment for fungal infection and, given the multi-organism microbiology described, infectious disease input (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Patients with marked bowel-wall thickening on imaging should be flagged early to surgery given the mortality gradient described with increasing wall thickness.

## What was deliberately omitted

DeVita's text for this entity does not give a numeric dosing regimen for the antibiotics beyond naming drug classes/specific agents, so no doses are reproduced here (by design, per the dose-free rule). It also does not discuss any role for radiotherapy, targeted therapy, or immunotherapy in managing the acute episode itself, so none is asserted. Long-term outcome/survival statistics beyond the qualitative mortality comparisons for bowel-wall thickness were not included because they were tied to a specific ultrasound sub-study rather than to management per se, and the disease's own KB entry (not DeVita) already covers prognosis narratively.
