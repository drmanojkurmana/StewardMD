# Nodular fasciitis: management narrative

## Diagnosis before treatment
Nodular fasciitis is a benign lesion typically seen in adults aged 20 to 40 years, arising in the subcutaneous fascia or superficial deep fascia of an extremity or the trunk. It characteristically grows rapidly over several weeks, usually reaching only 1 to 2 cm and rarely 5 cm or more, and is often painful or tender. This rapid growth, high cellularity, and brisk mitotic activity commonly cause it to be mistaken for fibrosarcoma or another spindle-cell sarcoma, so histologic confirmation is the first step in management rather than immediate wide resection. Over 90 percent of cases carry a USP6 gene rearrangement, most commonly a MYH9-USP6 translocation, and this molecular finding can support the diagnosis when histology is ambiguous (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease, though the underlying USP6 rearrangement rate and MYH9-USP6 fusion frequency are stated directly in DeVita's section on this disease).

## Treatment approach by intent
There is no malignant potential to treat and no staged, intent-based oncologic pathway analogous to a sarcoma. Because the lesion generally regresses spontaneously and recurrence after local excision is uncommon when the diagnosis is confirmed, management is conservative:
- Once biopsy (core or excisional) confirms nodular fasciitis and excludes a spindle-cell sarcoma, simple local excision is curative and is reasonable when the diagnosis is uncertain or the lesion is symptomatic (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Observation alone is a reasonable option given the tendency toward spontaneous regression, particularly once the diagnosis is secure (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Radical or wide excision with oncologic margins is not indicated, since nodular fasciitis is not a malignancy and does not behave like one; over-resection is a recognized pitfall (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Lines of therapy
There is no systemic or radiotherapeutic line of therapy for nodular fasciitis. It is not treated with chemotherapy, targeted agents, or radiation. Surgery (simple excision) is the only active intervention used, and only when excision is chosen over observation; DeVita's section on this disease describes the natural history (spontaneous regression, uncommon recurrence after local excision) but does not lay out a formal treatment algorithm beyond this.

## Role of surgery, radiotherapy, and systemic therapy
- Surgery: limited to simple, non-radical local excision, performed either to confirm the diagnosis (excisional biopsy) or to remove a symptomatic lesion. There is no role for compartmental or radical resection.
- Radiotherapy: no role; nodular fasciitis is benign and self-limited, so radiotherapy is not part of standard management.
- Systemic therapy: no role. There is no chemotherapy or targeted-agent indication for this diagnosis.

## Monitoring
Because recurrence after excision is uncommon, follow-up can be brief and clinical. If a lesion recurs after apparently complete excision, this should prompt re-review of the original diagnosis rather than escalation of local therapy, since recurrence raises the possibility of a misdiagnosed sarcoma (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## When to refer
Referral to a soft-tissue pathology or sarcoma service is appropriate when the clinical or histologic picture is atypical for nodular fasciitis, for example a larger lesion, deep location, atypical mitoses, necrosis, or continued growth beyond the expected self-limited course, since these features should raise concern for a true sarcoma rather than nodular fasciitis (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease). Molecular testing for USP6 rearrangement can be arranged through a pathology service in diagnostically difficult cases.

## What DeVita's section on this disease does not address
DeVita's section on this disease is brief and describes epidemiology, clinical growth pattern, histology, and the USP6/MYH9 molecular finding, together with the statement that these lesions generally regress spontaneously and that recurrence after local excision is uncommon. It does not provide a structured management algorithm, does not mention intralesional corticosteroid injection, and does not discuss observation as an explicit alternative to excision; those points above are marked as general oncology standard rather than DeVita-sourced.

Sources: DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of Oncology, 12th ed.
