## Overview

RGNT is a rare, indolent CNS WHO grade 1 glioneuronal neoplasm that arises characteristically in the midline posterior fossa around the fourth ventricle, with occasional occurrence near the aqueduct or pineal region (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease). Because it is a low-grade, slow-growing lesion, management is oriented around safe surgical control of the mass and its mechanical effects (obstructive hydrocephalus) rather than around cytotoxic systemic therapy (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Treatment approach by intent

### Newly diagnosed, resectable disease
- Maximal safe surgical resection is the primary and often definitive treatment; gross total resection can be curative given the grade 1 biology (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Because the tumour sits in an eloquent midline location adjacent to the brainstem and cerebellum, the extent of resection is deliberately balanced against the risk of new neurological deficit; subtotal resection is an accepted outcome when further dissection risks brainstem or cerebellar injury (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Any accompanying obstructive hydrocephalus from fourth-ventricular involvement is addressed directly, typically with endoscopic third ventriculostomy or ventriculoperitoneal shunting, either before or at the time of resection (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

### Residual or unresectable disease after surgery
- Asymptomatic residual tumour after subtotal resection is generally observed with surveillance imaging rather than treated immediately, reflecting the tumour's indolent course (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Adjuvant radiotherapy is reserved for disease that is progressive, recurrent, or causing symptoms that cannot be managed surgically; it is not routine first-line treatment for a grade 1 lesion (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

### Recurrent or progressive disease
- Repeat resection is considered when technically feasible and when it can relieve symptoms or mass effect (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- For disease that recurs or progresses despite surgery and is not amenable to further resection, radiotherapy is the next line considered (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Because RGNT recurrently harbours FGFR1 alterations (sometimes with PIK3CA or NF1 changes), FGFR-targeted therapy has been proposed as an option for progressive or unresectable disease in individual cases and small series, though this is not established first-line systemic therapy (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease). There is no established role for conventional cytotoxic chemotherapy in RGNT and none should be inferred.

## Role of surgery, radiotherapy, and systemic therapy
- **Surgery** is the mainstay of treatment at every stage where it is technically feasible, both for tumour control and for relief of hydrocephalus (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- **Radiotherapy** plays a secondary, reserve role, used only for progressive, recurrent, or unresectable disease, not upfront (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- **Systemic/targeted therapy** (FGFR-directed agents) has a narrow, case-based role limited to progressive disease that has exhausted surgical and radiotherapy options; it is not part of routine initial management (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Monitoring
- Serial surveillance brain MRI is used to detect residual tumour growth or recurrence after initial surgery, given the tumour's slow but not universally static behaviour (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Monitoring of ventricular size is warranted post-operatively and at follow-up to detect recurrent obstructive hydrocephalus (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Any unexpectedly rapid growth on surveillance imaging should prompt reconsideration of the diagnosis, since this would be atypical for a grade 1 tumour (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## When to refer
- All patients should be managed within a neuro-oncology multidisciplinary team from diagnosis, given the need to weigh resection extent against eloquent-location morbidity (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Refer for neurosurgical assessment promptly if there is evidence of obstructive hydrocephalus or progressive brainstem/cerebellar signs, as these indicate a need for urgent surgical intervention (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Refer for neurorehabilitation when postoperative neurological deficits persist, given the eloquent posterior fossa location (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Consider referral to a centre with FGFR-targeted therapy experience or a relevant clinical trial for progressive or unresectable disease that has exhausted standard surgical and radiotherapy options (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
