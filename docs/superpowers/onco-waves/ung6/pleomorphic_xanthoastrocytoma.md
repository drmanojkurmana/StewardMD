# Pleomorphic xanthoastrocytoma (BRAF V600E) - Management

## Overview and treatment intent

Pleomorphic xanthoastrocytoma (PXA) belongs to a group DeVita explicitly calls "well-circumscribed
gliomas," alongside pilocytic astrocytoma, which "may be cured surgically if resected in their
entirety," in contrast to diffuse gliomas that infiltrate and recur even after complete radiographic
resection. DeVita also groups PXA together with gangliogliomas, pilocytic astrocytomas, and
subependymal giant cell astrocytomas as "astroglial variant forms of low-grade gliomas" that are
more circumscribed than diffuse low-grade gliomas, are classified as lower grade, and "do not
typically invade the normal brain," such that "surgery alone is often curative" and progression to a
higher grade is less frequent than with diffuse gliomas. Treatment intent is therefore curative for
newly diagnosed, resectable, lower-grade (CNS WHO grade 2) disease, and control/palliative-leaning
for anaplastic (grade 3), unresectable, or recurrent disease.

## Surgery

- Maximal safe resection is the primary and preferred treatment for newly diagnosed PXA, mirroring
  DeVita's statement for astroglial-variant low-grade gliomas that gross total resection provides
  long-term tumor control in the great majority of cases and is the preferred treatment for both
  newly diagnosed and recurrent tumors.
- Complete resection is associated with a very favorable long-term outcome, while the degree of
  anaplasia (i.e., grade 3 versus grade 2) is a key determinant of prognosis, per the same DeVita
  discussion of this tumor group.
- Repeat resection at recurrence is a reasonable option when a meaningful proportion of tumor can
  safely be removed (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease; extrapolated
  from DeVita's discussion of the closely related cerebellar astrocytoma group).

## Radiotherapy

- DeVita's discussion of the closely related ganglioglioma group notes there is "no clear consensus
  regarding the role of adjuvant radiation" in ganglioglioma, but that adjuvant radiation should be
  considered after subtotal resection, particularly in eloquent locations where further surgery risks
  significant morbidity, and that retrospective data suggest adjuvant radiation improves local control
  (and shows a non-significant trend toward improved survival) after subtotal resection compared with
  subtotal resection alone; extrapolated to PXA (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section
  on this disease).
- For higher-grade (anaplastic) histology, DeVita's discussion of the closely related ganglioglioma
  group notes that higher radiotherapy doses are warranted, especially after subtotal resection, given
  the lower likelihood of durable local control from surgery alone (general oncology standard, not
  from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Highly conformal techniques are favored to limit long-term CNS toxicity given the younger patient
  population typically affected, and stereotactic radiosurgery may be considered for limited
  recurrence after resection.
- A reasonable approach for grade 2 PXA after gross total resection is close imaging surveillance
  without upfront radiotherapy, reserving radiotherapy for subtotal resection, anaplastic histology,
  or progression (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease, consistent
  with NCCN practice for CNS WHO grade 2 to 3 astrocytic tumors).

## Systemic and targeted therapy

- DeVita's discussion of the closely related ganglioglioma group states that for astroglial variants
  such as ganglioglioma, no optimal chemotherapeutic regimen has been defined, that chemotherapy in
  ganglioglioma is generally reserved for young children after subtotal resection with disease
  progression, and that in older patients it is used mainly as salvage therapy after surgery and
  radiotherapy have failed, with disease stabilization (rather than complete response) considered a
  successful outcome; extrapolated to PXA (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on
  this disease).
- BRAF V600E mutation is a defining molecular feature of PXA, and DeVita separately discusses
  BRAF-targeted therapy for BRAF V600E-mutant glioma broadly: in a basket trial of BRAF V600E-mutant
  glioma of various histologies treated with a BRAF inhibitor, objective responses were seen in a
  minority of patients, and among responders the duration of response and treatment exceeded one
  year overall and exceeded two years specifically in xanthoastrocytoma. DeVita further notes that
  BRAF mutations are more frequent in pediatric and young-adult gliomas, that BRAF- or
  BRAF/MEK-directed targeted therapy can produce long-lasting responses, and that small series
  report responses and clinical benefit from combined BRAF and MEK inhibition in recurrent and
  disseminated BRAF-mutated glioma, though DeVita also cautions that none of the currently available
  BRAF/MEK agents were developed specifically for primary brain tumors and it remains unclear
  whether particular agents are preferable for CNS penetration.
- On this basis, combined BRAF-inhibitor plus MEK-inhibitor therapy (drug classes, not a specific
  dosed regimen) is a reasonable option for BRAF V600E-mutant PXA that is recurrent, unresectable, or
  anaplastic, grounded in DeVita's broader BRAF-mutant glioma discussion rather than a PXA-specific
  trial.
- Temozolomide has been used with limited reported experience in the closely related low-grade
  glioma group per DeVita, but DeVita does not describe a defined role for it specifically in PXA, so
  no recommendation is made here beyond noting it as an option considered on a case-by-case basis in
  a multidisciplinary setting (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Seizure management

- Antiseizure medication is an important part of supportive care given the cortical, temporal
  location that predisposes to seizures at presentation, and DeVita's general neuro-oncology
  guidance favors single-agent antiepileptic therapy with slow dose titration and, when a patient has
  been seizure-free and off active tumor treatment, considers eventual tapering under specialist
  guidance (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease specifically, drawn
  from DeVita's general CNS tumor supportive-care discussion).

## Monitoring

- Serial contrast-enhanced brain MRI is the mainstay of surveillance after resection, both to detect
  recurrence and to identify radiological signs of transformation to a higher grade.
- Because DeVita's PXA-relevant discussion emphasizes prognosis being driven by grade and resection
  extent, follow-up intensity should be higher for subtotally resected or anaplastic disease and can
  be less frequent after a gross total resection of grade 2 disease (general oncology standard, not
  from DeVita's section on this disease).

## When to refer

- All patients should be managed through a neuro-oncology multidisciplinary team from diagnosis.
- Escalate for neurosurgical or radiation oncology input at initial diagnosis to plan resection and
  weigh adjuvant radiotherapy, and re-refer promptly for radiological or clinical progression, new
  focal deficits, refractory seizures, or histological evidence of anaplastic transformation.
- Molecular/BRAF V600E testing should prompt referral to a center with access to BRAF/MEK-targeted
  therapy when disease is recurrent, unresectable, or anaplastic.

## What DeVita is silent on for this disease

DeVita does not contain a dedicated PXA management section; its PXA-relevant content is folded into
its discussion of "well-circumscribed" or "astroglial variant" low-grade gliomas (shared with
pilocytic astrocytoma, ganglioglioma, and subependymal giant cell astrocytoma) and into its general
BRAF-mutant glioma section, which happens to mention xanthoastrocytoma by name for BRAF-inhibitor
response duration. DeVita does not give a PXA-specific chemotherapy regimen, a PXA-specific
radiotherapy dose, or PXA-specific survival statistics; these were deliberately omitted rather than
inferred.

Sources: DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of Oncology, 12th ed.
NCCN Guidelines (CNS Cancers) where noted above.
