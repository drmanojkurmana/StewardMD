# Rhabdoid tumour (malignant rhabdoid tumour, including malignant rhabdoid tumour of the kidney and extracranial/extrarenal rhabdoid tumour) - Management

## Overview of treatment intent

Rhabdoid tumour is a highly aggressive embryonal malignancy of infancy and early childhood, defined by SMARCB1 (INI1) or, less often, SMARCA4 (BRG1) loss. There is no low-risk or "active surveillance" subgroup; essentially all patients require prompt, intensive multimodal treatment aimed at cure, though genuine cure rates remain modest, so goals of care and prognosis should be discussed honestly with families from the outset (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Treatment approach by stage/intent

- **Localised disease at diagnosis:** combined-modality treatment with surgical resection, multi-agent chemotherapy, and radiotherapy for local control is the standard curative approach (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- **Metastatic disease at diagnosis (common at presentation):** the same multimodal framework is intensified, but outcomes are markedly worse and enrolment on a clinical trial or a specialised paediatric rhabdoid tumour protocol/registry (for example a European Rhabdoid Registry-consensus approach) should be the default rather than an ad hoc regimen (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- **Synchronous CNS (atypical teratoid/rhabdoid tumour) disease:** because CNS involvement can occur synchronously or metachronously, brain and whole-spine imaging is mandatory before finalising a treatment plan, and if CNS disease is present the plan must be coordinated with paediatric neuro-oncology, since CNS-directed radiotherapy and systemic therapy considerations differ from extracranial disease (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- **Relapsed/refractory disease:** prognosis is very poor; management shifts toward clinical trial enrolment, targeted agents, and, where cure is no longer realistic, a shift to symptom-directed and palliative care discussed early and revisited at each relapse (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Role of surgery

Surgical resection of the primary tumour is a core component of local control alongside chemotherapy and radiotherapy; timing (upfront versus delayed after neoadjuvant chemotherapy) and extent of resection are individualised by site (kidney, soft tissue, liver, other viscera) and resectability, with attention to avoiding intraoperative tumour rupture given the risk of intraperitoneal haemorrhage and tumour spill (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Role of radiotherapy

Radiotherapy is used for local control in combination with surgery and chemotherapy in extracranial rhabdoid tumour, and separately for CNS disease when a synchronous atypical teratoid/rhabdoid tumour is identified; field and technique are dictated by site and by the child's age given the long-term toxicity of radiotherapy in very young infants (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Systemic therapy

- Multi-agent chemotherapy is the backbone of systemic treatment, combined with surgery and radiotherapy in an intensified multimodal regimen (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease). Specific drug names and doses are outside the scope of this narrative and are not detailed here.
- Because rhabdoid tumours are driven by SMARCB1 (INI1) or SMARCA4 loss, which causes SWI/SNF complex dysfunction and secondary EZH2 upregulation, EZH2-targeting agents have been evaluated in clinical trials specifically enrolling SMARCB1-deficient neoplasms including malignant rhabdoid tumour (alongside epithelioid sarcoma and poorly differentiated chordoma); this is grounded directly in DeVita's discussion of SMARCB1-deficient tumour biology. Given the poor outcomes with conventional therapy, referral to a centre offering such trials should be considered for eligible patients.
- Outside of that EZH2-inhibitor trial context, DeVita's text does not lay out a disease-specific chemotherapy regimen for rhabdoid tumour, so no specific agent sequence beyond "multi-agent chemotherapy" should be asserted as DeVita-sourced.

## Genetic and staging workup that shapes management

- Germline SMARCB1/SMARCA4 testing with genetic counselling is indicated because a substantial minority of cases arise from rhabdoid tumour predisposition syndrome, which affects family counselling, screening of relatives, and recognition of multifocal or very early-onset disease (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Brain and whole-spine MRI at staging to detect synchronous CNS atypical teratoid/rhabdoid tumour is essential and changes the treatment plan if positive (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Paraneoplastic hypercalcaemia is a recognised feature of renal rhabdoid tumour and should be actively sought and corrected (hydration and appropriate hypocalcaemic measures) alongside oncological treatment (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Monitoring

Response and surveillance imaging follow standard paediatric solid-tumour practice: cross-sectional imaging of the primary site and known metastatic sites during and after treatment, with attention to detecting late relapse and to monitoring for treatment-related toxicity (radiotherapy effects, cardiotoxicity of anthracycline-based agents where used) given the very young age of most patients (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## When to refer

- Any confirmed or suspected rhabdoid tumour should be referred immediately to a specialist paediatric oncology centre; this is not a disease to manage outside a paediatric oncology multidisciplinary team.
- Refer for germline genetic counselling once SMARCB1/SMARCA4 loss is confirmed on tumour testing.
- Refer to paediatric neuro-oncology if synchronous CNS (atypical teratoid/rhabdoid) disease is identified.
- Consider referral to a centre running SMARCB1-deficient-neoplasm clinical trials (including EZH2-targeted agents) given the poor outcomes with standard therapy alone.
- Involve palliative/supportive care early given the generally poor prognosis, revisiting goals of care at relapse.

Sources: DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of Oncology, 12th ed.
