# Rhabdomyosarcoma (embryonal/alveolar) - Management

## Overview of treatment approach

Rhabdomyosarcoma (RMS) is the most common soft tissue sarcoma of infancy and childhood, and treatment nearly always requires multimodal therapy combining surgery, radiation, and chemotherapy rather than any single modality alone. The chemotherapy backbone described is a vincristine-dactinomycin-cyclophosphamide combination (drug classes/agents named generically here; specific dosing lives only in structured protocol templates, not in this narrative).

The three classically described histologic subtypes are embryonal, alveolar, and pleomorphic rhabdomyosarcoma, and treatment intent and expected response differ meaningfully across them:

- **Embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma** usually arises in the orbit or genitourinary tract in children. Chemotherapy and radiation are described as very effective for pediatric embryonal tumors, even in metastatic disease. In adults, embryonal tumors usually regress with chemotherapy but overall survival is worse than in children; metastasis at presentation and poor chemotherapy response are associated with worse prognosis in adults.
- **Alveolar rhabdomyosarcoma** is more common in adolescents and adults than in younger children, and in younger children it carries a worse prognosis than embryonal disease. Most alveolar tumors carry a PAX3-FOXO1 or PAX7-FOXO1 fusion; PAX3-FOXO1 fusions may carry a worse prognosis than PAX7-FOXO1, so confirming fusion status (FISH and/or molecular testing) is described as helping optimize treatment plans.
- **Pleomorphic rhabdomyosarcoma** is the most common form in adults and can follow prior radiation exposure. Prognosis is poor. Treatment is surgical, and because of the poor prognosis eligible patients are given adjuvant radiation and chemotherapy. These tumors are less sensitive to systemic therapy than embryonal or alveolar disease, though some respond to an anthracycline-and-ifosfamide-based approach, and anecdotal reports describe some sensitivity to gemcitabine-based chemotherapy.

Spindle cell and sclerosing rhabdomyosarcomas (associated with MYOD1 mutation or VGLL2-related fusions) are a small minority of cases; a distinct treatment approach for this rare group was not described in the grounding source.

## Role of surgery, radiation, and systemic therapy

- **Surgery**: primary means of local control, particularly for pleomorphic RMS, and part of multimodal local control across subtypes.
- **Radiation therapy**: used for local control alongside or instead of surgery depending on site and resectability, and as adjuvant treatment in pleomorphic RMS given its poor prognosis (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease, for the specific point that radiation is typically reserved for cases with residual disease after surgery or unresectable primary sites; DeVita's section confirms radiation as a core multimodal component but does not detail site-specific indications).
- **Systemic therapy**: chemotherapy is central to treatment of embryonal and alveolar RMS and is described as highly effective even in metastatic pediatric embryonal disease. Response is less reliable in pleomorphic RMS.

## Risk stratification and treatment intensity

Risk-stratified treatment intensity based on fusion status, stage, clinical group, primary site, age, and histology is standard practice for rhabdomyosarcoma (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease). ## Monitoring

Ongoing monitoring after chemotherapy, radiation, and/or surgery for response and recurrence, and surveillance for late treatment effects, is standard oncology practice (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease). The grounding source does not detail a specific imaging or follow-up interval schedule for rhabdomyosarcoma.

## When to refer

- Any suspected or confirmed rhabdomyosarcoma should be referred to a multidisciplinary sarcoma/pediatric oncology team before biopsy or definitive local therapy, given the reliance on combined chemotherapy, surgery, and radiation and on fusion-status testing to guide treatment (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease; DeVita's section grounds the need for multimodal therapy and fusion testing but does not itself state referral triggers).
- Adult patients with embryonal, alveolar, or pleomorphic RMS should be managed by clinicians experienced with this disease given the described worse outcomes in adults compared with children, and the generally poor prognosis of pleomorphic disease.
