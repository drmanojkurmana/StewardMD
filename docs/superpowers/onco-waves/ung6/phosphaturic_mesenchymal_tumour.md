# Phosphaturic Mesenchymal Tumour (Tumour-Induced/Oncogenic Osteomalacia) - Management

## Overview of treatment intent

Phosphaturic mesenchymal tumour (PMT) is almost always a benign, slow-growing mesenchymal neoplasm that causes a paraneoplastic syndrome (tumour-induced or oncogenic osteomalacia) through autonomous secretion of FGF23 (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease). Because the tumour itself is usually indolent, the treatment goal is definitive removal of the FGF23 source rather than cytotoxic cancer therapy, plus correction of the resulting metabolic bone disease while the tumour is being localised or if it cannot be found or removed (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Treatment by scenario

- Localised, resectable tumour (the majority of cases): complete surgical excision with negative margins is the treatment of choice and is potentially curative, with rapid normalisation of serum phosphate and clinical improvement afterward (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Tumour identified but anatomically difficult to resect completely (for example skull base or sinonasal sites): image-guided or minimally invasive surgical approaches, and image-guided ablative techniques (such as radiofrequency or cryoablation) have been used as alternatives or adjuncts to open surgery in inoperable or high-risk locations (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Tumour not yet localised despite biochemical confirmation of FGF23-mediated hypophosphataemia: functional imaging to find the culprit lesion takes priority over empiric treatment escalation, since medical therapy is a bridge, not a cure (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Inoperable, unresectable, multifocal, or persistently occult disease: long-term medical management of the hypophosphataemia is required, with an FGF23-directed biologic now available as a targeted option when conventional phosphate/vitamin D therapy is insufficient or poorly tolerated (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Rare malignant variant (phosphaturic mesenchymal tumour, malignant, as distinguished in current WHO soft tissue tumour classification) or metastatic disease: management follows the same surgical-first principle for local disease, with multidisciplinary sarcoma-type input for any residual, recurrent, or metastatic disease, since data on systemic (chemotherapy/targeted) therapy in this rare setting are limited (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Lines of therapy

- First line: complete surgical resection of the localised tumour once identified by functional imaging (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Bridging/adjunctive medical therapy (used pre-operatively, while localisation is pending, or when surgery is deferred): oral phosphate salts combined with active vitamin D (calcitriol-class agent) to correct hypophosphataemic osteomalacia and reduce fracture risk and bone pain (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Second line / for inoperable or persistent disease: an anti-FGF23 monoclonal antibody (burosumab) can be used to control hypophosphataemia when the tumour cannot be resected or recurs, and may allow reduction or discontinuation of phosphate/vitamin D supplementation (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Ablative techniques (radiofrequency ablation or similar) are a reasonable alternative to repeat surgery for a localised but surgically inaccessible or recurrent lesion (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Role of surgery, radiotherapy and systemic therapy

- Surgery is the definitive and preferred treatment whenever the tumour can be localised and safely accessed, because it addresses the underlying cause rather than just the metabolic consequence (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Radiotherapy does not have an established primary role in benign PMT; it is not a standard first-line modality for this tumour type based on available evidence, and its use, if considered at all, would be reserved for exceptional situations such as unresectable or malignant disease and should go through multidisciplinary discussion (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Systemic (cytotoxic) therapy is not standard for the typical benign PMT; conventional chemotherapy has an uncertain and unestablished role, and would only be considered in the rare malignant or metastatic variant under sarcoma-directed multidisciplinary care (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- FGF23-targeted biologic therapy (burosumab) and phosphate/active-vitamin-D repletion are the systemic medical measures with an established role, but they treat the paraneoplastic biochemical syndrome, not the tumour itself (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Monitoring

- Serum phosphate, calcium, alkaline phosphatase and parathyroid hormone should be monitored regularly, both to confirm cure after resection and to titrate medical therapy in unresected or persistent disease (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Patients on phosphate and active vitamin D supplementation should be screened periodically for nephrocalcinosis and secondary/tertiary hyperparathyroidism as a treatment complication (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- After apparently complete resection, persistence or recurrence of hypophosphataemia and symptoms should prompt repeat functional imaging to look for residual tumour or a second occult lesion, rather than assuming a new unrelated disease (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Long-term surveillance is warranted for incompletely excised tumours, recurrence, and the rare malignant/metastasising variant (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## When to refer

- Refer for endocrinology input as soon as tumour-induced osteomalacia is biochemically suspected (inappropriately normal or low active vitamin D with renal phosphate wasting and hypophosphataemia), to guide diagnostic work-up and functional imaging (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Refer to a centre with access to somatostatin-receptor functional imaging and to surgeons experienced with these anatomically diverse (often acral or sinonasal/craniofacial) lesions once localisation is attempted, since the tumours are frequently small and easily missed (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Refer to a multidisciplinary team (endocrinology, oncology/surgery, radiology and pathology) for any tumour that is unresectable, recurrent, incompletely excised, or shows malignant histology, and for management of persistent hypophosphataemia requiring FGF23-targeted biologic therapy (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
