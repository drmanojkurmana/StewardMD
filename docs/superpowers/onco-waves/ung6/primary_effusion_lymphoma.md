# Primary effusion lymphoma (HHV-8) - Management

## Disease context (grounded)
Primary effusion lymphoma (PEL, also called body cavity-based lymphoma) is a rare, aggressive large B-cell neoplasm that grows within serous body cavities (pleura, pericardium, peritoneum, and rarely the cerebrospinal fluid) rather than as a discrete mass. A solid-tumour variant can rarely occur in the gastrointestinal tract. The malignant cells are large and pleomorphic, typically lack CD20 and CD19 but may express CD79a, CD45, CD30, and CD138, and are uniformly associated with HHV-8 (Kaposi sarcoma-associated herpesvirus), most often with EBV co-infection. It arises almost exclusively in immunocompromised hosts, most commonly advanced HIV infection, and rarely occurs in immunocompetent people. PEL is grouped with the AIDS-defining lymphomas, which overall carry long-term disease-free survival above 50 percent, though the primary-effusion subgroup is one of the rarer and more difficult-to-treat categories within that group.

## Treatment approach by setting

DeVita does not lay out a PEL-specific regimen recommendation the way it does for AIDS-related DLBCL or Burkitt lymphoma. Its discussion of AIDS-related non-Hodgkin lymphoma gives a general framework for the whole group (systemic DLBCL, Burkitt lymphoma, plasmablastic lymphoma, and PEL together), from which the following can reasonably be drawn:

- Treat as an AIDS-defining or immunodeficiency-associated aggressive lymphoma, not as a localised process; systemic combination chemotherapy is the mainstay rather than local therapy.
- Because PEL cells, like plasmablastic lymphoma cells, are typically CD20-negative, an anthracycline-based combination regimen without an anti-CD20 antibody is the usual backbone; a monoclonal anti-CD20 antibody is only added if the tumour is shown to express CD20.
- Continue or start effective antiretroviral therapy alongside chemotherapy; immune reconstitution is treated as integral to disease control, not an adjunct.
- Examination of the cerebrospinal fluid is considered for AIDS-related lymphoma patients generally, given the recognised risk of central nervous system involvement in this group.
- Prophylaxis against Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, herpes zoster, and Candida is part of routine supportive care during treatment of AIDS-related lymphoma.
- (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease) Because PEL causes recurrent malignant effusions, therapeutic drainage (thoracentesis, pericardiocentesis, or paracentesis) is used for symptom relief and to relieve tamponade or respiratory compromise, alongside systemic therapy rather than as a substitute for it.
- (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease) There is no defined role for surgery or curative-intent radiotherapy in PEL; radiotherapy is used only for localised palliation of a symptomatic solid deposit in the rare extracavitary/solid variant.
- (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease) Coexisting HHV-8-driven disease, such as Kaposi sarcoma or multicentric Castleman disease, should be actively looked for and managed alongside the lymphoma, since they share the same driving infection and immunodeficiency.

## Lines of therapy

- First line: an anthracycline-based combination chemotherapy regimen, with or without an anti-CD20 antibody depending on CD20 expression, given together with antiretroviral therapy. DeVita's related discussion of AIDS-associated lymphoma notes that an infusional, dose-adjusted anthracycline-based regimen is favoured over standard cyclical dosing in the CD20-negative, high-proliferation subset that PEL resembles immunophenotypically.
- (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease) Relapsed or refractory disease has no established standard; management is individualised, and clinical-trial enrolment should be considered given how uncommon and aggressive this lymphoma is.
- (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease) In fit patients who achieve a response, autologous stem-cell transplantation consolidation has been used as in other aggressive AIDS-related lymphomas, extrapolating from outcomes reported for AIDS-related lymphoma as a group rather than PEL specifically.

## Monitoring

- (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease) Monitor effusion re-accumulation clinically and with imaging, HIV viral load and CD4 count, and standard chemotherapy toxicity parameters (blood counts, renal and hepatic function, tumour-lysis risk) through treatment.
- (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease) Response is usually assessed by resolution of the effusion and cytological clearance rather than by a solid-tumour size measure, since PEL characteristically produces no discrete mass.

## When to refer

- (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease) Refer promptly to a haemato-oncology or lymphoma multidisciplinary team as soon as HHV-8-positive malignant effusion cytology is confirmed; this is an aggressive lymphoma requiring urgent systemic treatment planning.
- (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease) Involve HIV medicine or infectious disease specialists early for antiretroviral optimisation, and interventional radiology or a proceduralist for effusion drainage when tamponade or respiratory compromise is present.

## What DeVita is silent on

DeVita's text does not give a PEL-specific chemotherapy regimen, a stated duration of treatment, response-assessment criteria, or salvage-therapy recommendations for this histology; the framework above for first-line therapy is extrapolated from its discussion of AIDS-related lymphoma as a group (particularly its comments on CD20-negative, high-proliferation disease) rather than a dedicated PEL passage, and is marked as such above.
