# Sclerosing stromal tumour of the ovary (aliases: ovarian sclerosing stromal tumour, sclerosing stromal tumor) - Management

FLAG: needs manual sourcing. DeVita's oncology text does not carry a dedicated section on sclerosing stromal tumour of the ovary by name; it is not indexed and does not appear in the ovarian sex cord-stromal tumour discussion in the gynaecologic oncology chapter. The chapter does, however, cover the broader category of ovarian sex cord-stromal tumours (granulosa cell tumour, juvenile granulosa cell tumour, Sertoli-Leydig cell tumour, gynandroblastoma, gonadoblastoma) as a group, and the general surgical principle it states for that group is used below where it plausibly extends to this entity. Everything else is marked as general oncology standard.

## Treatment approach by presentation and intent

Sclerosing stromal tumour is a benign sex cord-stromal neoplasm, almost always unilateral, most often diagnosed in adolescents and women in their second and third decades. Because it is benign, there is no "intent" spectrum of curative-versus-palliative therapy as with malignant ovarian tumours; the entire treatment approach is surgical excision of the mass, with the only real decision being how conservative the surgery can be (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

- First-line and only line of therapy: surgical removal of the affected ovary or the mass alone, which is curative (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Fertility-sparing cystectomy (ovary-conserving excision) or unilateral oophorectomy is favoured whenever technically feasible, given the young age of the typical patient and the tumour's benign, unilateral behaviour (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Frozen-section or intraoperative pathological assessment is used to confirm a benign sex cord-stromal process before committing to a more limited procedure, since the histology (cellularity, prominent vasculature) can superficially mimic a malignant stromal tumour (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- If there is genuine diagnostic uncertainty on frozen section, or imaging/histology raises concern for a malignant stromal tumour, a more complete staging procedure (removal of the ovary with careful inspection of the contralateral ovary and peritoneal surfaces) should be favoured over a purely conservative approach until malignancy is excluded (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Lines of therapy

There is no concept of sequential lines of systemic therapy for this tumour, because it is benign and does not require chemotherapy, endocrine therapy, or targeted therapy. This distinguishes it sharply from the malignant ovarian sex cord-stromal tumours (granulosa cell tumour, Sertoli-Leydig cell tumour) discussed in DeVita's gynaecologic oncology chapter, where advanced or recurrent disease may be treated with a platinum-based combination regimen extrapolated from germ cell tumour therapy, or with hormonal manipulation for Sertoli-Leydig tumours; none of that applies to a sclerosing stromal tumour, which by definition behaves in a uniformly benign fashion.

## Role of surgery, radiotherapy, and systemic therapy

- Surgery is the entire treatment. There is no established role for radiotherapy or systemic (cytotoxic, hormonal, or targeted) therapy in sclerosing stromal tumour, because the tumour does not recur or metastasise once excised (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Lymphadenectomy or formal surgical staging is not indicated for this benign lesion. Notably, for the malignant ovarian sex cord-stromal tumours as a group, DeVita's gynaecologic oncology chapter states that lymphadenectomy can probably be omitted from surgical staging in the absence of grossly enlarged nodes, reflecting a broader move away from routine nodal dissection for sex cord-stromal neoplasms; this principle reinforces (but does not by itself establish) the conservative surgical approach appropriate to a benign tumour such as this one.
- If the tumour is producing oestrogen (a recognised feature of this entity, with hormonal effects sometimes accentuated in pregnancy), management of any resulting endometrial hyperplasia should follow standard gynaecological practice for unopposed oestrogen exposure, with endometrial sampling if abnormal bleeding is present (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).

## Monitoring

- After curative excision, routine gynaecological follow-up is sufficient; there is no established surveillance imaging or tumour marker protocol for this tumour because recurrence and metastasis are not expected (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Resolution of oestrogenic symptoms (menstrual irregularity, abnormal bleeding) and normalisation of the endometrium after removal should be confirmed clinically (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- There is no validated serum biomarker specific to this tumour used for monitoring, in contrast to inhibin A used for granulosa cell tumour surveillance in DeVita's discussion of that related but distinct entity.

## When to refer

- Refer to gynaecological oncology (rather than general gynaecology) whenever there is diagnostic uncertainty on imaging or frozen section that raises concern for a malignant ovarian stromal tumour, so that appropriate intraoperative decision-making and staging capability are available (general oncology standard, not from DeVita's section on this disease).
- Refer pregnancy-associated cases, where hormonal activity may be more pronounced and surgical planning is more complex, to a gynaecological oncology or maternal-fetal-medicine-linked team for joint management.
- Refer for endometrial assessment (gynaecology or gynaecological oncology) if there is unopposed oestrogen effect with abnormal uterine bleeding.

Sources: DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of Oncology, 12th ed.
