# Chondroblastoma: Management

## Overview of treatment intent

Chondroblastoma is a benign, locally aggressive cartilage-forming tumour of the epiphysis or
apophysis in skeletally immature patients. Because it is benign, the management goal is local
control of the lesion with preservation of the adjacent growth plate and joint surface, not
systemic cancer therapy. There is no established role for chemotherapy or radiotherapy as primary
treatment in typical, non-metastatic disease.

## Primary treatment: surgery

- The mainstay of treatment is intralesional curettage of the lesion followed by filling of the
  resulting cavity with bone graft or bone cement.
- Local adjuvants applied to the curetted cavity (high-speed burring of the cavity wall, cryotherapy,
  or phenol) are used by many surgeons to reduce the chance of local recurrence.
- Radiofrequency ablation is a reasonable alternative for small lesions in a favourable location,
  as a less invasive option than open curettage.
- Because lesions sit at the epiphysis or apophysis in growing bone, surgical planning must protect
  the growth plate and the articular cartilage; this is a technical priority distinct from most
  other bone tumour surgery, given the risk of growth arrest or joint damage in a young patient.
- Wide resection is not standard first-line treatment for a benign lesion and should be reserved
  for extensive, destructive, or repeatedly recurrent disease where curettage has failed and
  joint-sparing options are exhausted.

## Lines of therapy / recurrence

- There is essentially one line of definitive local therapy (curettage plus adjuvant and grafting).
- Local recurrence after curettage is not uncommon, especially with incomplete removal or
  aggressive-appearing lesions, and is managed with repeat curettage using the same principles,
  again with attention to growth plate and joint preservation.
- There is no established role for systemic (chemotherapy) treatment in ordinary chondroblastoma.
  Any discussion of systemic therapy applies only to the rare situation of pulmonary implants or
  atypical, aggressive behaviour, and should prompt referral rather than empirical treatment.

## Role of radiotherapy

Radiotherapy has no established primary role in chondroblastoma management given the benign
nature of the tumour and the growth-plate proximity in children and adolescents, in whom
radiation carries added long-term risk. It is not part of standard first-line or recurrence
management.

## Monitoring

- Surveillance imaging (plain radiographs, supplemented by MRI as needed) after curettage to
  detect local recurrence, with closer follow-up after incomplete excision or in aggressive-looking
  lesions.
- Clinical follow-up for joint function and, in children, for any evidence of growth disturbance
  from growth-plate involvement or surgical violation of the physis.
- Chest imaging is reasonable when the lesion behaves aggressively, because rare pulmonary implants
  have been described with chondroblastoma; these are usually indolent when they occur.

## When to refer

- All suspected chondroblastoma should be managed within an orthopaedic oncology multidisciplinary
  team from the point of diagnosis, given the need for image-guided or open biopsy, careful surgical
  planning around the physis and joint, and histological/molecular confirmation.
- Refer promptly for atypical radiological or histological features that raise concern for clear
  cell chondrosarcoma, which can mimic chondroblastoma radiologically but carries malignant
  potential and a different treatment pathway.
- Refer for specialist orthopaedic oncology input whenever there is extensive growth-plate
  involvement threatening future limb growth, articular collapse, or lesions that recur after an
  initial curettage.
- Refer for pulmonary nodules identified on chest imaging, to distinguish benign pulmonary
  implants from other pathology.
