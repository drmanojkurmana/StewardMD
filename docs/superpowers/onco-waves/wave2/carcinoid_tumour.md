# Carcinoid tumour (well-differentiated neuroendocrine tumour): management expansion

## Overview of the management framework

Management of well-differentiated neuroendocrine tumours (NETs, historically called carcinoid tumours) splits into two parallel tracks that run together rather than in strict sequence: therapy aimed at the tumour itself, and therapy aimed at controlling hormonal (carcinoid syndrome) symptoms. Treatment intensity and the balance between these two tracks is set mainly by grade (Ki-67 and mitotic index), site of origin, resectability, and whether liver metastases and functional symptoms are present. Well-differentiated, low-grade tumours are managed very differently from poorly differentiated, high-grade neuroendocrine carcinomas, which behave more like small-cell carcinoma and are treated primarily with chemotherapy rather than the NET-specific pathway below.

## Treatment by stage and intent

### Localised, resectable disease
Surgical resection is the mainstay and the primary route to cure or long-term disease control across primary sites (small bowel, appendix, colon, rectum, stomach, duodenum, bronchus, thymus).

- Small, low-risk lesions (small appendiceal tumours confined to the tip, small rectal polyps, small non-ampullary duodenal lesions) can often be managed with local or endoscopic excision alone, reflecting their low rate of nodal or distant spread.
- Larger or higher-risk lesions (tumour size above a few centimetres, invasion through the wall, base-of-appendix location, nodal involvement) require a more extensive oncologic resection with regional lymphadenectomy, for example a right hemicolectomy for larger appendiceal tumours or bowel resection with mesenteric lymph node clearance for small bowel primaries.
- Bronchial and thymic well-differentiated tumours are treated surgically (lobectomy for bronchial NETs, radical thymectomy for early-stage thymic NETs); poorly differentiated/small-cell histology at either site is treated primarily with chemotherapy instead, with radiotherapy considered for incomplete thymic resection.
- Even with numerous distant metastases, resection of a symptomatic or obstructing primary (particularly ileal primaries causing cramping or obstruction from the associated mesenteric desmoplastic reaction) is often still pursued for symptom control, distinct from resection performed with curative intent.
- Perioperative somatostatin analogue cover is required before any surgery, anaesthesia, or invasive procedure (including embolisation, ablation, and dental work) in patients with functional disease, to prevent carcinoid crisis (flushing, hypotension, bronchospasm, arrhythmia).

### Unresectable or metastatic disease
- In newly diagnosed, asymptomatic patients with unresectable or metastatic low-grade disease, active surveillance without immediate systemic therapy is a reasonable strategy given the typically indolent course.
- Systemic or liver-directed therapy is reserved for patients with symptoms, significant tumour bulk, or documented progression.
- Liver metastases, the dominant site of spread, are addressed with liver-directed options when disease is confined mainly to the liver: surgical resection/ablation (when the great majority of visible tumour can be removed or ablated), radiofrequency or microwave ablation for smaller lesions away from major vessels, and transarterial embolisation or chemoembolisation for diffuse bilobar disease. Liver transplantation is used in carefully selected patients.
- Poorly differentiated neuroendocrine carcinoma of any primary site is treated like small-cell lung cancer, with a platinum-based chemotherapy regimen, rather than the NET pathway.

## Lines of systemic therapy for well-differentiated, progressive/metastatic disease

- Somatostatin analogues (octreotide, lanreotide) are considered appropriate first-line antitumour therapy for progressive metastatic midgut NETs, on top of their role in symptom control, and can stabilise tumour growth in a substantial proportion of patients; benefit is greatest with lower hepatic tumour burden and a resected primary.
- Peptide receptor radionuclide therapy (targeting the somatostatin receptor) has shown superior progression-free and overall survival compared with high-dose somatostatin analogue therapy in inoperable, progressive, somatostatin-receptor-positive midgut disease, and is used for progression on standard-dose somatostatin analogues.
- Everolimus (an mTOR inhibitor) has a clearly established role in metastatic pancreatic NETs; its benefit in midgut/small-bowel carcinoid is less certain, and major guideline groups have not endorsed it for that setting specifically, so it is used more selectively there.
- Antiangiogenic tyrosine kinase inhibitors (such as sunitinib) and other VEGF-pathway inhibitors have shown activity in NETs generally, with a clearer, approved role in pancreatic NETs than in carcinoid/midgut tumours.
- Interferon-alpha has symptomatic activity but a low objective antitumour response rate and is now a later-line option given its side-effect burden.
- Cytotoxic chemotherapy has been disappointing in well-differentiated small bowel carcinoid tumours and is not generally recommended outside a clinical trial for that specific group; this contrasts with pancreatic NETs, where chemotherapy has more activity, and with poorly differentiated neuroendocrine carcinoma, which is chemotherapy-treated by default.
- Checkpoint inhibition (anti-PD-1 therapy) has shown a modest response rate in PD-L1-positive NETs and is not an established standard first-line option.

## Management of carcinoid syndrome (functional disease)

- Somatostatin analogues are the foundation of symptom control, reducing flushing and diarrhoea in a majority of patients and normalising biochemical markers in a substantial proportion; dose escalation or more frequent dosing is used for breakthrough symptoms or tachyphylaxis.
- Telotristat, an inhibitor of peripheral serotonin synthesis, is added for diarrhoea that remains inadequately controlled on a somatostatin analogue alone.
- Adjunctive measures for diarrhoea include bile-acid binders, pancreatic enzyme supplementation, antibacterial treatment for bacterial overgrowth, and anti-motility agents.
- Bronchospasm during flushing episodes should not be treated with beta-2 agonists, which can provoke further mediator release.
- Niacin supplementation is used for pellagra arising from tryptophan diversion into serotonin synthesis.
- Perioperative/pre-procedural somatostatin analogue cover is mandatory to prevent carcinoid crisis, with escalation of dosing and supportive care (volume, steroids) if crisis occurs.

## Monitoring

- Chromogranin A and, when functional disease is suspected, 24-hour urinary 5-HIAA are used to track hormonal activity and tumour burden, with awareness of drugs (proton pump inhibitors, certain antihistamines and analgesics) and dietary items that cause false elevation.
- Cross-sectional imaging (multiphasic CT or MRI) and somatostatin-receptor imaging (DOTATATE PET/CT) are used for staging and for following known disease, particularly to assess liver metastases.
- Echocardiography is used to screen for carcinoid heart disease at diagnosis of carcinoid syndrome and periodically thereafter (more frequently if urinary 5-HIAA excretion is markedly elevated), because valvular involvement can progress to right heart failure requiring valve replacement.
- FDG-PET has a larger role in higher-grade, less-differentiated tumours, reflecting their higher metabolic activity, compared with well-differentiated disease.

## When to refer

- All patients with a confirmed or suspected neuroendocrine tumour should be managed by a multidisciplinary neuroendocrine tumour team, given the range of surgical, liver-directed, and systemic options and the importance of grade in choosing between them.
- Refer for surgical opinion at diagnosis of any localised, potentially resectable primary, and for consideration of resection or liver-directed therapy even in the presence of metastatic disease if the primary is causing or threatens obstruction.
- Refer to cardiology early once carcinoid syndrome is confirmed, and more urgently if echocardiography shows valvular disease, for consideration of valve replacement.
- Refer for peptide receptor radionuclide therapy assessment (somatostatin-receptor imaging plus renal/marrow function review) when disease progresses on standard-dose somatostatin analogue therapy.
- Any evidence suggestive of poorly differentiated/high-grade neuroendocrine carcinoma on biopsy should prompt urgent medical oncology referral, since management and prognosis differ substantially from well-differentiated disease.
