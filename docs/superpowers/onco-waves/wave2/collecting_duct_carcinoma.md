# Collecting Duct Carcinoma (Bellini Duct Carcinoma) - Management

## Overview

Collecting duct carcinoma (CDC) is a rare, high-grade renal carcinoma arising from the principal cells of the distal collecting ducts in the renal medulla. It shows a male preponderance and typically presents around the mid-50s. Histologically it is high grade and can resemble the urothelial spectrum of cancers, and it carries an overall poor prognosis. Unlike clear cell renal cell carcinoma, CDC does not carry VHL gene mutations, which helps explain why it does not behave, or respond to therapy, like typical clear cell RCC. Its genetics are highly variable, with losses described across multiple chromosomal regions.

Because CDC is rare, there is no large randomized trial base specific to this histology. Treatment is extrapolated from general renal cell carcinoma principles, from its biological overlap with high-grade urothelial cancer, and from expert/guideline consensus. Where evidence is genuinely lacking, that gap is called out below rather than filled in.

## Localised disease

- Surgery is the mainstay of curative treatment. Radical nephrectomy with regional lymph node dissection is the standard approach for localised, resectable disease.
- Complete surgical resection of localised disease offers the best chance of prolonged survival; this is the single most important determinant of outcome in CDC.
- Given the tendency for early lymph node and distant spread, thorough preoperative staging (cross-sectional imaging of the abdomen/pelvis and chest imaging) is important before committing to surgery, since occult metastatic disease is common at presentation.
- There is no established, histology-specific role for routine adjuvant systemic therapy in CDC. In renal cell carcinoma broadly, adjuvant trials of targeted kinase inhibitors after resection of high-risk disease (which enrolled mixed clear cell and non-clear cell populations) have shown inconsistent results, with most trials showing no disease-free or overall survival benefit and only one trial showing a modest disease-free survival benefit that did not translate into an overall survival benefit. Adjuvant immunotherapy, hormone therapy, radiotherapy, and tumour vaccines have not shown a survival benefit in the adjuvant RCC setting historically. There is no dedicated CDC adjuvant evidence, so this remains an area of uncertainty best discussed on a case-by-case basis, ideally with clinical trial consideration.

## Advanced or metastatic disease

- Because CDC behaves more like an aggressive urothelial carcinoma than a typical renal cell carcinoma, systemic treatment approaches for CDC generally follow urothelial-type cytotoxic chemotherapy principles rather than the clear cell RCC targeted-therapy or immunotherapy paradigm.
- Platinum-based cytotoxic combination chemotherapy is the systemic treatment most commonly used for advanced or metastatic CDC, reflecting its urothelial-like biology.
- Standard clear cell RCC systemic therapies (VEGF-pathway tyrosine kinase inhibitors, mTOR inhibitors, and immune checkpoint inhibitor combinations) are the evidence-backed standard for clear cell and, to a variable extent, other non-clear cell histologies, but CDC's biology (absence of VHL pathway alterations) means these agents cannot be assumed to work the same way in CDC as they do in clear cell disease. Response to conventional targeted or immune-based RCC regimens is generally poor in CDC.
- Enrolment in clinical trials should be considered whenever available, given the limited evidence base and poor response of CDC to conventional systemic agents; checkpoint-inhibitor-based approaches are being explored in this setting but are not an established standard specifically validated for CDC.
- Best supportive and palliative care with attention to symptom control is appropriate at any point where the disease burden, patient fitness, or goals of care favour it, and should run in parallel with active treatment discussions from diagnosis of advanced disease.

## Monitoring and follow-up

- Following resection of localised disease, structured surveillance imaging is warranted to detect recurrence or metastatic spread early, given the aggressive natural history of this tumour.
- Baseline and interval renal function assessment should accompany oncological staging and follow-up, both because of the disease itself and because of the nephrectomy.
- Any new haematuria, flank pain, palpable mass, or constitutional symptoms after treatment should prompt prompt re-imaging given the tumour's propensity for early and aggressive recurrence.

## When to refer

- All suspected or confirmed CDC should be managed through a specialist uro-oncology multidisciplinary team from diagnosis, given the rarity of the histology, the difficulty distinguishing it from upper tract urothelial carcinoma or renal medullary carcinoma, and the lack of a standardised systemic therapy pathway.
- Refer early for clinical trial evaluation when advanced or metastatic disease is identified, given the poor response to conventional therapy and the absence of a histology-specific standard of care.
- Refer for genitourinary pathology review with immunohistochemistry when the diagnosis is uncertain, to exclude upper tract urothelial carcinoma and renal medullary carcinoma before finalising a treatment plan.

## What is deliberately not stated here

DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of Oncology does not contain a dedicated treatment algorithm or trial data specific to collecting duct carcinoma; its coverage of CDC is limited to histologic/genetic classification and general non-clear cell RCC context. Specific regimen choices, checkpoint-inhibitor trial results, and adjuvant strategies for CDC itself are not described in the source and are therefore not asserted as evidence-based specifics above; where a claim above draws on general RCC-wide evidence rather than CDC-specific data, this is noted in context.
