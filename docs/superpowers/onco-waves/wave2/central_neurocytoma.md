## Overview

Central neurocytoma is an intraventricular neuronal tumor (WHO CNS grade 2) that most often arises
near the septum pellucidum or foramen of Monro in young to middle-aged adults. Because of its
location, most patients present with obstructive hydrocephalus and raised intracranial pressure
rather than with a focal neurologic deficit from tumor bulk alone. Overall behaviour is typically
indolent, but a subset of atypical tumors with a higher proliferation index behaves more aggressively
and recurs.

## Treatment approach by presentation and intent

- **Symptomatic obstructive hydrocephalus at presentation**: relief of CSF obstruction is addressed
  urgently, either as part of the definitive resection or, if the patient is acutely unwell, via a
  separate CSF diversion procedure (external ventricular drain, endoscopic third ventriculostomy, or a
  shunt) before or at the time of definitive surgery.
- **Localized, resectable tumor (the great majority of cases)**: maximal safe surgical resection is
  the primary and usually definitive treatment, with gross total resection frequently curative. Surgical
  approach (transcortical or transcallosal) is chosen by the neurosurgical team based on ventricular
  anatomy and tumor extent.
- **Incompletely resected, recurrent, or atypical tumors** (tumors with an elevated proliferation
  index or atypical histologic features): adjuvant radiotherapy is considered, since these tumors carry
  a materially higher risk of local recurrence than typical central neurocytoma.
- **Small residual or recurrent disease, particularly in a location where repeat open resection carries
  high risk**: focused radiotherapy in the form of stereotactic radiosurgery is a reasonable option,
  favoring durable local control while limiting exposure of adjacent brain and ventricular structures.
- **Systemic (drug) therapy** does not have an established, guideline-supported role in central
  neurocytoma; this entity is managed with local therapy (surgery, and radiotherapy when indicated)
  rather than systemic anticancer drug regimens. No cytotoxic, targeted, or other drug-class recommendation
  can be grounded for this tumor type, so none is given here.

## Lines of therapy

There is no established multi-line systemic therapy pathway for central neurocytoma, in contrast to
most solid tumors. The practical sequence is:

1. Maximal safe resection at diagnosis.
2. Observation with surveillance imaging if resection is complete and histology is typical.
3. Adjuvant radiotherapy (external beam or stereotactic radiosurgery, depending on residual volume and
   location) reserved for incomplete resection, atypical histology, or recurrence.
4. Repeat resection and/or radiotherapy at the time of recurrence, individualized by the
   neuro-oncology multidisciplinary team.

## Role of surgery, radiotherapy, and systemic therapy

- **Surgery** is the backbone of curative treatment. Extent of resection is the dominant driver of
  long-term outcome.
- **Radiotherapy** (conventional fractionated or stereotactic radiosurgery) is an adjunct for residual,
  recurrent, or atypical disease, not a routine treatment for every patient after gross total resection
  of a typical tumor.
- **Systemic therapy** has no defined role; this is a locally-managed tumor.

## Monitoring

- Serial contrast-enhanced brain MRI to detect recurrence or progression of residual disease, with
  surveillance intervals individualized to extent of resection and histologic risk (atypical tumors
  with a high proliferation index warrant closer follow-up than tumors with complete resection and
  typical histology).
- Clinical monitoring for symptoms of raised intracranial pressure or new hydrocephalus, which should
  prompt urgent imaging.
- Histopathologic confirmation of neuronal differentiation (synaptophysin) and assessment of
  proliferation index (Ki-67/MIB-1) at diagnosis to stratify a patient into standard versus atypical,
  higher-recurrence-risk categories, which in turn informs how closely they are followed.

## When to refer

- All patients with a newly identified intraventricular mass suspicious for central neurocytoma should
  be referred to a neurosurgical team with experience in intraventricular tumor resection, and evaluated
  by a neuro-oncology multidisciplinary team once histology is confirmed.
- Urgent neurosurgical referral is required for acute or rapidly progressive obstructive hydrocephalus
  or deteriorating consciousness.
- Referral to radiation oncology is appropriate whenever resection is incomplete, histology is atypical
  with an elevated proliferation index, or the tumor recurs.
- Neuropathology review (including synaptophysin, IDH, 1p/19q and ATRX status) should be sought when
  the differential with oligodendroglioma or another glial tumor is not already resolved, since
  management differs substantially between these entities.
