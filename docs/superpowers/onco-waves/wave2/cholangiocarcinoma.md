# Cholangiocarcinoma (Bile Duct Cancer, CCA) — Management

Cholangiocarcinoma is not a single disease. Management differs meaningfully by anatomic subtype
(intrahepatic, perihilar, and distal), and treatment planning should always start by establishing
which of these three the patient has, since the surgical options, staging systems, and expected
outcomes diverge from that point onward. A multidisciplinary discussion (hepatobiliary surgery,
medical oncology, radiation oncology, interventional radiology and gastroenterology) is the
standard starting point once imaging and tissue sampling establish the diagnosis.

## Treatment approach by stage and intent

### Resectable disease (any subtype)
Surgery is the only treatment with curative intent and remains the backbone of management when
resection is feasible. There is no single agreed anatomic definition of resectability; feasibility
depends on the tumour's relationship to the hilar vessels and bile ducts, the volume of liver that
would remain, and the surgeon's experience. A staging laparoscopy before formal laparotomy is
commonly used to rule out otherwise-occult peritoneal or distant spread before committing to a
major resection.

- Intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma: resection ranges from a limited nonanatomic resection to a formal
  segmental lobectomy, aiming for a microscopically clear (R0) margin while preserving adequate
  future liver function. Multiple intrahepatic satellite lesions do not automatically rule out
  resection, but widespread hepatic disease, extrahepatic spread, or advanced cirrhosis with portal
  hypertension generally does.
- Perihilar cholangiocarcinoma: resection typically requires removal of at least one liver lobe with
  the extrahepatic bile duct and a complete periportal lymphadenectomy, often including the caudate
  lobe given its frequent microscopic involvement. Bilateral involvement of all sectoral ducts, of
  the main portal vein trunk, or of the hepatic artery and portal vein bilaterally generally makes a
  tumour unresectable.
- Distal cholangiocarcinoma: resection is considered when disease is locally confined without major
  vascular involvement or distant spread; this is generally approached similarly to a
  pancreaticoduodenectomy given the shared anatomic territory with the pancreatic head.

Margin status is the dominant driver of outcome after resection at every anatomic site: a clear (R0)
margin is associated with markedly better survival than a microscopically positive (R1) margin.

### Liver transplantation
Transplantation has a defined but narrow role and is subtype-specific. For carefully selected,
early-stage, unresectable perihilar tumours, a protocol combining neoadjuvant chemoradiation with
staging laparoscopy followed by transplantation can achieve long-term survival and represents the
only curative option in this group; extrahepatic nodal disease or metastasis is a contraindication.
By contrast, transplantation for intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma has generally given disappointing
results and is not routinely offered, though it may have a role in highly selected patients with
underlying cirrhosis and a small tumour.

### Adjuvant systemic therapy after resection
The evidence for adjuvant chemotherapy in resected biliary tract cancer is mixed across trials that
enrolled biliary cancer as a mixed population rather than by anatomic subtype. A course of adjuvant
capecitabine after resection has shown a survival and recurrence-free survival benefit in a large
randomized trial and is the most consistently supported approach at this time. Adjuvant gemcitabine-
based regimens have not shown a clear survival benefit in comparable randomized trials. Because
trials to date have mixed intrahepatic, perihilar, distal, and gallbladder cancers, the true benefit
by anatomic subtype remains uncertain, and enrollment in a clinical trial should be considered
alongside standard adjuvant therapy where available.

### Unresectable, locally advanced disease
- Local-regional therapies (arterial embolization techniques, ablation) may be considered for
  intrahepatic disease confined to the liver, though the survival benefit of these approaches is not
  well established from randomized data.
- Radiation therapy, including dose-escalated or stereotactic approaches, may achieve durable local
  control in selected unresectable intrahepatic or perihilar disease, but data are limited to small
  series without randomized comparison, and treatment is constrained by the proximity of radiosensitive
  organs (stomach, duodenum, small bowel, liver, kidneys, spinal cord).
- Photodynamic therapy combined with biliary stenting has shown a survival and quality-of-life
  benefit over stenting alone for unresectable perihilar disease in some randomized data, but a later
  randomized trial was stopped early after showing shorter survival in the photodynamic-therapy arm.
  The evidence is therefore conflicting, and its role remains uncertain and centre-dependent; it may
  be considered at experienced centres but should not be presented as an established survival benefit.
- Systemic therapy (below) is appropriate for disease that is unresectable but confined locally, in
  parallel with local measures, and is the mainstay once disease is metastatic.

### Metastatic or extrahepatic disease
Systemic therapy is the mainstay of treatment, with the specific approach guided by molecular
profiling wherever feasible.

## Lines of systemic therapy

- First line: a platinum-gemcitabine doublet is the established standard of care for advanced
  biliary tract cancer, supported by a randomized trial showing improved overall survival and
  progression-free survival compared with gemcitabine alone. Enrollment in a clinical trial is an
  appropriate alternative for eligible patients.
- Second line: for a long time there was no agreed standard after progression on a
  gemcitabine-platinum doublet, historically outcomes with subsequent chemotherapy were modest.
  A fluoropyrimidine-oxaliplatin combination (FOLFOX) has since shown superior overall survival
  compared with best supportive care alone after progression on first-line gemcitabine and
  cisplatin, supporting its use as a second-line option.
- Targeted therapy in molecularly selected patients: genomic profiling of the tumour should be
  pursued, particularly for intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, because targetable alterations are
  more common in this subtype.
  - FGFR2 fusions or rearrangements: selective oral FGFR inhibitors have shown meaningful response
    rates in previously treated, unresectable or metastatic disease with an FGFR2 alteration and are
    approved for this indication.
  - IDH1 mutations: a selective oral IDH1 inhibitor improved progression-free survival compared with
    placebo in previously treated, IDH1-mutant advanced disease, with an overall survival benefit
    that reached significance only after accounting for placebo-to-drug crossover; it is approved for
    this indication.
  - Other emerging targets described in the disease include BRAF V600E mutations, NTRK fusions,
    and HER2/neu amplification (the latter particularly relevant in gallbladder carcinoma); targeted
    agents for these are best accessed through molecular tumour board review or clinical trial.

## Role of surgery, radiation, and systemic therapy summarised

- Surgery: the only potentially curative modality; role and technique depend heavily on anatomic
  subtype and extent of vascular/ductal involvement.
- Liver transplantation: curative option in a narrow, carefully selected perihilar population;
  generally not indicated for intrahepatic disease.
- Radiation therapy: adjunct for local control in selected unresectable disease and for palliation
  of jaundice; not a substitute for surgery when resection is feasible.
- Systemic therapy: standard for unresectable/metastatic disease, and the only modality with
  randomized survival data driving current first- and second-line choices; molecular profiling
  should inform later-line therapy.

## Monitoring

There are no formally established, evidence-based surveillance guidelines after curative resection
for any subtype of cholangiocarcinoma. In practice, surveillance commonly consists of liver function
tests and CA 19-9, together with cross-sectional imaging, at intervals of roughly every three months
for the first two years and every six months thereafter, adjusted to the individual's recurrence
risk. CA 19-9 is not a validated surveillance tool on its own, but a persistently rising level may
precede radiologic evidence of recurrence. Surgery is generally not offered for recurrent disease
after prior resection, which is why close surveillance mainly serves to identify recurrence early
enough for systemic therapy or clinical trial enrollment.

## When to refer

- Refer to a hepatobiliary/HPB surgical team as soon as cholangiocarcinoma is suspected, for
  staging (including consideration of staging laparoscopy) and a resectability assessment, before
  any biliary intervention that could complicate later surgery or transplant candidacy (for example,
  avoid EUS-FNA of a hilar lesion if transplantation is being considered, given the risk of tumour
  seeding).
- Refer to a transplant hepatobiliary programme for potentially unresectable but localized perihilar
  disease, since transplantation protocols are only available at select centres.
- Refer to medical oncology for systemic therapy and molecular tumour profiling in anyone with
  unresectable, recurrent, or metastatic disease, and to a clinical trial when available given the
  paucity of options beyond first- and second-line therapy.
- Refer to interventional radiology or gastroenterology (ERCP/PTC) for biliary decompression in
  anyone with obstructive jaundice or cholangitis, regardless of resectability, since relief of
  obstruction is central to symptom control and to enabling further treatment.
- Refer patients with primary sclerosing cholangitis or choledochal cysts for ongoing surveillance
  given their markedly increased lifetime risk of cholangiocarcinoma.
