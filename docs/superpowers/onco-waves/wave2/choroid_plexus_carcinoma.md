# Choroid Plexus Carcinoma (CPC): Management

Choroid plexus carcinoma is a rare, WHO grade III malignant intraventricular tumour that occurs mainly
in infants and young children. Because it is rare, no single treatment sequence is firmly established
by randomised trial, and management draws on retrospective series and multimodality principles rather
than a fixed algorithm.

## Treatment approach by stage / intent

**Newly diagnosed, localized disease**
- Maximal safe surgical resection is the cornerstone of initial treatment. Gross total resection is
  associated with better outcomes than subtotal resection, though the deep intraventricular location,
  high vascularity, and proximity to critical structures can make complete removal difficult.
- Perioperative planning should anticipate brisk intraoperative bleeding from the tumour's rich
  vascularity, and CSF diversion may be needed to manage hydrocephalus, which is common at
  presentation from obstruction, CSF overproduction by the tumour, or a damaged resorptive bed.
- Radiotherapy has a role after resection. Craniospinal irradiation (CSI) as part of a multimodality
  approach has been associated with better progression-free survival than whole-brain and tumour-bed
  radiotherapy alone in retrospective series, and CSI is used because CPC seeds along CSF pathways.
  The decision to irradiate, and the field chosen, must weigh this benefit against the risks of
  radiotherapy in young children, so age is a major factor in sequencing (see below).
- Chemotherapy is used for CPC, unlike the benign papilloma, for which chemotherapy has no role.
  There is no single agreed regimen; reported experience (largely anecdotal or from small series)
  describes activity with platinum compounds, alkylating agents, etoposide, methotrexate, and
  possibly anthracycline-based therapy. A pooled analysis found that patients who received chemotherapy
  had statistically better survival than those who did not, supporting its inclusion in the initial
  treatment plan rather than reserving it only for relapse.

**Infants and very young children**
- In young infants, radiotherapy carries substantial neurodevelopmental risk, and chemotherapy has
  been used specifically to delay or avoid irradiation until the child is older. This age-based
  deferral of radiotherapy, using chemotherapy as a bridge, is a recognised strategy rather than a
  universal cure-sparing measure, and radiotherapy is still considered once the child is older or if
  disease progresses.

**Residual, recurrent, or progressive disease**
- Repeat surgery is a reasonable option when resectable residual or recurrent tumour is accessible.
- Radiotherapy (fractionated external-beam or stereotactic radiosurgery) is used for residual or
  recurrent disease, particularly after a subtotal resection, where poor local control has been
  observed without adjuvant treatment. Higher fractionated doses have correlated with better local
  control in reported series.
- Stereotactic radiosurgery has been used for progressive or recurrent choroid plexus tumours,
  including some grade III cases, generally after prior surgery, with variable prior treatment history.
- Chemotherapy can be reconsidered at relapse, again without a defined standard regimen; agent
  selection should follow the same classes noted above and be individualized with pediatric
  neuro-oncology input.

## Monitoring

- Serial MRI surveillance is warranted given reported recurrence rates even after gross total
  resection, and because CPC can disseminate through CSF pathways, imaging of the full neuraxis
  (brain and spine) is appropriate for surveillance in line with the rationale for craniospinal
  treatment.
- Monitor for and manage hydrocephalus proactively, since ventricular obstruction and CSF
  overproduction can recur or persist independent of tumour control.
- Genetic evaluation for germline TP53 mutation (Li-Fraumeni syndrome) should be considered, as CPC
  is commonly associated with this mutation; this has implications for surveillance of the child and
  counselling of the family, though DeVita does not detail a specific extracranial surveillance
  protocol tied to this association.

## When to refer

- Any suspected or confirmed choroid plexus carcinoma should be managed at a centre with pediatric
  neurosurgery, pediatric neuro-oncology, and radiation oncology experienced in CSI, given the rarity
  of the tumour, the technical demands of resecting a highly vascular intraventricular mass, and the
  lack of a standardized chemotherapy regimen.
- Refer for clinical genetics assessment when TP53 mutation / Li-Fraumeni syndrome is suspected,
  particularly in children with a family history of early-onset cancers.
- Consider referral to a center that can offer clinical trial enrollment, since treatment is derived
  largely from small retrospective series rather than randomized evidence.

## What was deliberately left out

DeVita does not specify a preferred first-line chemotherapy regimen, sequencing of specific agents,
or a standardized dose/fractionation schedule for radiotherapy in CPC, and none is stated here.
Any specific regimen selection and dosing belongs in the R1-gated structured protocol templates, not
in this narrative.

Sources: DeVita, Hellman, and Rosenberg's Cancer: Principles & Practice of Oncology, 12th ed.
