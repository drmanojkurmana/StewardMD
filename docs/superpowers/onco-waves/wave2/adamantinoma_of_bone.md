# Adamantinoma of long bones (adamantinoma of bone) — Management Expansion

## Grounding note

DeVita's chapter on sarcomas of bone mentions adamantinoma only in passing: as one of the
"Other mesenchymal" entities in the WHO classification table of primary bone malignancies, and
in a single line noting that "adamantinoma is most frequently encountered in the tibia and/or
fibula." It does not carry a dedicated treatment section for this entity comparable to the
detailed osteosarcoma or Ewing sarcoma discussions elsewhere in the chapter. Because of this,
most of the management content below leans on uncontroversial, guideline-general orthopedic
oncology principles for a chemo/radiation-resistant low-grade bone malignancy rather than on
DeVita-specific dosing or regimen language, which DeVita does not provide for this tumor. Where
DeVita is silent, that is stated plainly rather than filled in. FLAG: chemotherapy and
radiotherapy response data, systemic-therapy specifics, and outcome statistics for adamantinoma
are not detailed in the DeVita text located and would benefit from manual sourcing against a
dedicated bone-sarcoma or WHO soft tissue and bone tumor reference.

## Treatment approach by stage and intent

Adamantinoma is treated with curative intent in essentially all newly diagnosed, localised
cases, because it behaves as a low-grade but genuinely malignant tumor that is locally
infiltrative rather than acutely aggressive. There is no separate palliative-intent pathway for
localized disease; the goal at diagnosis is always complete removal.

- Localized disease confined to the tibia or fibula: definitive wide surgical excision with
  clear margins, planned around limb preservation where feasible.
- Locally advanced disease with cortical breach or soft-tissue extension: still surgically
  driven, with more extensive resection and reconstruction, guided by imaging of the full
  extent of marrow and soft-tissue involvement.
- Metastatic disease (uncommon, and typically a late event): individualized, most often
  centered on resection of limited pulmonary or nodal metastatic deposits when they are
  surgically approachable, since the primary tumor and its metastases share the same relative
  resistance to non-surgical therapy.
- Recurrent disease after inadequate (intralesional or marginal) prior surgery: re-excision
  with wider margins is the priority, since recurrence after incomplete surgery is the
  dominant failure pattern for this tumor.

## Lines of therapy

Adamantinoma does not fit the conventional "first line, second line systemic therapy" model
used for chemotherapy-responsive sarcomas. There is effectively one definitive line of therapy,
surgery, with systemic and radiation options held in reserve for situations where surgery alone
cannot achieve control.

- First-line and, in most patients, only necessary treatment: wide en bloc surgical excision.
- If margins are inadequate or recurrence occurs: repeat wide excision is preferred over
  escalating to non-surgical therapy, given the tumor's known resistance to chemotherapy and
  radiotherapy.
- Systemic therapy (a cytotoxic sarcoma-type regimen) is not a standard, reliably effective
  option for this histology and should be considered only in a multidisciplinary sarcoma tumor
  board setting for unresectable or diffusely metastatic disease where no surgical option
  exists, with expectations set accordingly.

## Role of surgery, radiotherapy, and systemic therapy

- Surgery is the mainstay and, for most patients, the entirety of definitive treatment. Wide
  excision with negative margins, ideally with limb-sparing resection and reconstruction of the
  tibial defect, is the standard of care. Intralesional or marginal excision should be avoided,
  as it is the principal driver of local recurrence.
- Radiotherapy has a limited, largely adjunct or salvage role. It is not considered reliably
  effective against this tumor's biology and is not a substitute for adequate surgical margins;
  it may be considered case-by-case when surgical margins cannot be adequately achieved, but
  this is an individualized multidisciplinary decision rather than a routine part of care.
- Systemic (chemotherapeutic) therapy has a similarly limited role, as the tumor is largely
  insensitive to it. It is not part of routine management of localized disease and is reserved
  for exceptional circumstances, such as unresectable or disseminated disease, discussed at a
  bone sarcoma multidisciplinary tumor board.
- Regional lymph nodes and, when present, distant metastatic deposits (chiefly pulmonary) are
  managed surgically where feasible, following the same principle that resection, not systemic
  or radiation therapy, drives outcome for this tumor.

## Monitoring

- Long-term clinical and radiographic follow-up is required after resection because local
  recurrence and metastasis can present late, sometimes years after initial treatment.
- Surveillance should include periodic local imaging of the operated limb to detect recurrence
  and periodic chest imaging (chest CT) to detect delayed pulmonary metastases, since the lungs
  are the most common site of distant spread.
- Regional lymph node basins should also be assessed periodically, since nodal metastasis,
  while uncommon, has been described.
- Follow-up should not be curtailed prematurely; the indolent natural history of this tumor
  means recurrence or metastasis can be missed if surveillance stops too early.

## When to refer

- Any suspected adamantinoma, or a tibial lesion in an adolescent or young adult with the
  characteristic soap-bubble lytic-sclerotic radiographic pattern, should be referred to a bone
  sarcoma multidisciplinary team before biopsy, since biopsy tract planning affects future
  limb-sparing surgical options.
- Refer promptly if there is diagnostic uncertainty between adamantinoma, its
  osteofibrous-dysplasia-like variant, and osteofibrous dysplasia itself, since this
  distinction changes management and is difficult on limited biopsy material without
  cytokeratin immunohistochemistry.
- Refer for specialist orthopedic oncology input whenever cortical destruction, a soft-tissue
  mass, or a pathological fracture is identified, and whenever recurrence is suspected after
  prior surgery elsewhere.
- Refer for pulmonary or nodal metastasis evaluation and possible metastasectomy if surveillance
  imaging identifies new lesions.
