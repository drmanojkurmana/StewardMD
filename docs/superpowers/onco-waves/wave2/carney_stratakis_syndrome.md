# Carney-Stratakis Syndrome (Dyad) — Management

Carney-Stratakis syndrome is an autosomal dominant condition defined by the combination of gastrointestinal stromal tumor (GIST) and paraganglioma, arising from a germline mutation in one of the succinate dehydrogenase (SDH) complex subunits (SDHA, SDHB, SDHC, or SDHD). It is distinct from the Carney triad (GIST, pulmonary chondroma, paraganglioma), which is not typically inherited. Because the syndrome produces two biologically different tumor types, management is best thought of as two coordinated tracks, SDH-deficient GIST and paraganglioma, run through a genetics-aware, multidisciplinary lens rather than a single protocol.

## General approach

- Confirm the molecular subtype. Any GIST that is negative for KIT and PDGFRA mutations should have SDHB immunohistochemistry performed; a negative SDHB stain identifies SDH-deficient GIST and should prompt germline SDHx testing and genetic counseling, since a positive result confirms Carney-Stratakis syndrome and has implications for at-risk relatives.
- Because this is a hereditary, multi-tumor syndrome, care should be coordinated between a sarcoma-experienced surgical/medical oncology team and an endocrine oncology team managing the paraganglioma, with genetic counseling involved from diagnosis onward.
- Given the rarity of both SDH-deficient GIST and hereditary paraganglioma, referral to a center or network with specific experience in sarcoma and endocrine tumors is appropriate, particularly for surgical planning of multifocal disease or when systemic therapy for advanced disease is being considered.

## GIST component

**Localized disease**
- Surgery (wedge or segmental resection) is the primary treatment for resectable GIST, as in sporadic disease. However, SDH-deficient GISTs are often multifocal and tend to be confined to the stomach, in contrast to KIT/PDGFRA-mutated tumors, so the extent of resection needs individualized planning that weighs the generally indolent behavior of these tumors against the possibility that an individual lesion behaves aggressively.
- SDH-deficient GIST is essentially insensitive to imatinib, and adjuvant trial data do not support its use in this molecular subtype. Patients with SDH-deficient GIST are therefore not currently selected for adjuvant tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) therapy after resection, unlike KIT- or PDGFRA-mutated GIST.
- Because these tumors respond poorly to available TKIs, there should be a lower threshold to consider surgery for presentations that would otherwise be managed medically in mutated GIST.

**Metastatic or unresectable GIST**
- Imatinib is not a reliable option for SDH-deficient disease. Sunitinib and regorafenib have shown some activity in SDH-deficient GIST and are the systemic options with the most support when TKI therapy is considered.
- Because the natural history of SDH-deficient GIST is often less aggressive than KIT-mutated disease, treatment decisions (including whether to treat asymptomatic low-volume metastatic disease at all) should factor in the generally indolent course rather than defaulting to immediate systemic therapy.
- Surgical resection of limited, resectable metastatic or recurrent disease can be considered on a case-by-case basis given the relative lack of durable systemic options.

## Paraganglioma component

- Surgery is the treatment of choice for a resectable, hormonally active or potentially active paraganglioma. Preoperative alpha-adrenergic blockade (agents such as phenoxybenzamine, prazosin, or doxazosin), titrated over roughly ten to fourteen days with salt and fluid loading, is required before any surgery to prevent life-threatening hemodynamic instability; a beta-blocker can be added afterward if needed for tachycardia, but only once alpha-blockade is established, to avoid unopposed alpha-receptor stimulation.
- Intraoperative blood pressure control requires invasive monitoring and rapidly acting antihypertensives, and postoperative care needs close monitoring, typically in an intensive care setting, given the risk of hemodynamic swings after tumor removal.
- Local recurrence is also managed surgically where feasible, which often also relieves catecholamine excess.
- For metastatic (AJCC stage IV) paraganglioma, an asymptomatic, slowly progressive picture is common in SDHB-associated disease, and a watch-and-wait approach with symptom control is often appropriate rather than immediate systemic therapy.
- When treatment of symptomatic or progressive metastatic paraganglioma is needed, options include debulking or metastasectomy for limited resectable burden, locoregional techniques (radiofrequency ablation, transarterial chemoembolization) for unresectable metastases, external-beam radiation, radiopharmaceutical therapy (131I-MIBG in MIBG-avid disease, or 177Lu-DOTATATE in DOTATATE-avid disease), and systemic cytotoxic chemotherapy (a cyclophosphamide-dacarbazine-vincristine-based regimen, or a capecitabine-temozolomide combination). Any of these procedures again require adequate preoperative alpha-blockade.
- Supportive care includes ongoing alpha- (and if needed beta-) blockade for catecholamine-driven hypertension, and antiresorptive therapy for patients with bone metastases.
- Up to half of patients with SDHB-related paraganglioma develop metastatic disease, which is a materially higher rate than paraganglioma in general, so SDHB-mutation carriers warrant closer long-term surveillance for metastatic conversion.

## Monitoring

- Patients with a confirmed SDHx germline mutation need lifelong surveillance for both tumor types (recurrent or new GIST, and new or recurrent paraganglioma/pheochromocytoma), since the syndrome predisposes to metachronous and multifocal lesions over time.
- Biochemical follow-up (plasma free metanephrines and normetanephrines, or 24-hour urine catecholamines/metanephrines) is the primary surveillance tool for paraganglioma once a patient's secretory profile is known; imaging surveillance is reserved for those with a non-secretory or previously unknown biochemical profile.
- For SDH-deficient GIST, surveillance imaging intervals should reflect the generally indolent behavior of the disease, but multifocality means the whole at-risk field (typically the stomach) needs periodic reassessment rather than a single resection site.
- At-risk first-degree relatives should be offered genetic counseling and SDHx testing, since Carney-Stratakis syndrome is autosomal dominant.

## When to refer

- Refer to a sarcoma-experienced surgical/medical oncology team for any GIST that is KIT/PDGFRA wild-type or SDHB-negative, and to an endocrine oncology or endocrine surgery team for any diagnosed or suspected paraganglioma, given the specialized preoperative blockade and perioperative management these tumors require.
- Refer for genetic counseling and germline SDHx testing at the time of diagnosis of either tumor type in a patient with a personal or family history suggestive of the syndrome.
- Refer to a center with specific experience in rare hereditary tumor syndromes when multifocal or recurrent disease raises complex surgical decision-making, or when systemic therapy for advanced GIST or metastatic paraganglioma is being considered, given the limited and TKI-class-dependent evidence base in this population.
