# Chromophobe renal cell carcinoma (chRCC) - management

## Localised disease (the great majority at presentation)

Chromophobe RCC is usually found incidentally as a well circumscribed solid renal
mass and is managed with the same stage/size-based surgical framework used for
localised renal cell carcinoma generally, individualised to tumour size, complexity,
and the patient's renal function and comorbidity. Options, chosen with a urologist
experienced in RCC:

- Partial (nephron-sparing) nephrectomy for small to medium tumours where
  technically feasible, favoured because it preserves renal function compared with
  removing the whole kidney.
- Radical (total) nephrectomy for larger, more complex, or locally advanced tumours,
  or where nephron-sparing surgery is not achievable. Routine ipsilateral
  adrenalectomy is no longer performed unless there is direct invasion or a
  suspicious adrenal lesion on imaging.
- Thermal ablation for small masses, as a kidney-sparing alternative to surgery,
  particularly when operative risk is high; it carries a comparatively higher rate
  of local failure and can make histological confirmation and follow-up imaging
  interpretation harder.
- Active surveillance for small, indolent-appearing masses, especially in older or
  comorbid patients, with imaging follow-up to detect growth or a change in
  behaviour.

There is no reliable trial evidence specific to chromophobe histology showing a
survival benefit for adjuvant systemic therapy after resection. Several randomised
adjuvant trials in resected RCC (evaluating VEGF-pathway tyrosine kinase inhibitors
and, in one, an mTOR inhibitor) enrolled non-clear-cell histologies including
chromophobe, but on the whole did not demonstrate a consistent disease-free or
overall survival benefit; the one adjuvant trial that did show a modest disease-free
survival benefit for a VEGF tyrosine kinase inhibitor enrolled clear-cell-predominant
disease only, so it does not generalise to chromophobe RCC. Outside of a clinical
trial, routine adjuvant systemic therapy is not established practice for resected
chromophobe RCC.

## Advanced or metastatic disease

Chromophobe RCC is grouped with the "non-clear-cell" renal cancers for systemic
treatment purposes and is generally less responsive to therapy than clear-cell
carcinoma, so referral to medical oncology and consideration of clinical trial
enrolment (given how uncommon this histology is) should happen early. From what
is grounded in the source material:

- An mTOR inhibitor (everolimus) has activity that appears to be maintained across
  RCC histologies, and within the non-clear-cell group it seems to have relatively
  stronger activity specifically in chromophobe tumours than in other non-clear-cell
  subtypes.
- In RCC more broadly (predominantly studied in clear-cell disease), the treatment
  landscape has moved toward immune-checkpoint-inhibitor-based combination therapy
  as initial treatment for advanced disease, with single-agent VEGF-pathway
  tyrosine kinase inhibitor therapy used in the refractory setting; a VEGF-pathway
  multi-target tyrosine kinase inhibitor plus an anti-PD1 checkpoint inhibitor is
  an example of such a combination. The advance of checkpoint inhibitor and
  tyrosine-kinase-inhibitor-based options has pushed the mTOR inhibitor into third
  line and beyond in general RCC practice, and there is signal that combining the
  mTOR inhibitor with a VEGF-pathway tyrosine kinase inhibitor may keep it useful
  earlier, in the second line.
- None of this trial evidence isolates chromophobe RCC as its own study population;
  it is extrapolated from RCC trials that enrolled non-clear-cell (and sometimes
  clear-cell-predominant) cohorts, so treatment selection should default to
  standard non-clear-cell RCC guideline pathways (an oncologist applying current
  NCCN-consistent non-clear-cell RCC recommendations) with a strong bias toward
  offering a clinical trial where available.
- Sarcomatoid dedifferentiation, when present, denotes markedly worse behaviour and
  should prompt more aggressive systemic treatment intent and closer monitoring.

## Hereditary work-up

Because chromophobe histology (and hybrid oncocytic tumours) can be a
manifestation of Birt-Hogg-Dube syndrome, an autosomal-dominant condition also
causing skin fibrofolliculomas and pulmonary cysts, patients with multiple or
bilateral chromophobe/oncocytic tumours, early age of onset, or a suggestive family
or personal history should be referred for genetic counselling and germline
testing, with screening for pulmonary cysts and skin lesions in confirmed cases and
counselling of at-risk relatives.

## Monitoring and follow-up

After treatment of localised disease, follow-up should be risk-stratified (as for
RCC generally, using stage/grade-based risk categories) with surveillance imaging
extending out to around five years; a meaningful proportion of recurrences in RCC
occur after that point, so vigilance should not stop abruptly at five years,
particularly in higher-risk disease. Baseline and follow-up renal function should
be checked, especially after nephron-sparing procedures or in patients left with a
single kidney. Any suspicion of sarcomatoid change, rapid mass growth, or new
symptoms should prompt prompt re-staging.

## When to refer

- To urology (with RCC-specific experience) at diagnosis, for surgical planning of
  any localised mass.
- To medical oncology for any locally advanced disease not amenable to complete
  resection, or any metastatic disease, and to discuss clinical trial availability.
- To clinical genetics when Birt-Hogg-Dube syndrome is suspected (multiple/bilateral
  tumours, oncocytic hybrid histology, early onset, or a compatible family or
  personal history).
- To a uro-oncology multidisciplinary team for cases with sarcomatoid features,
  equivocal oncocytoma-versus-chromophobe distinction, or atypical/aggressive
  behaviour.
