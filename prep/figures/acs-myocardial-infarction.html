What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How an acute coronary syndrome develops. A lipid-rich atherosclerotic plaque (foam cells, lipid core, thin fibrous cap) ruptures or erodes. Exposed material activates platelets, which stick and clump, and the clotting cascade lays down fibrin, forming a thrombus. A partial block with a still-open vessel gives unstable angina or NSTEMI (often ST depression); a complete block gives STEMI (ST elevation, transmural injury). Muscle starved of blood becomes ischaemic, then infarcts, and troponin leaks out. This is standard teaching in medicine and cardiology texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Treatment ideas shown. Antiplatelet drugs stop platelet clumping, an anticoagulant blocks fibrin formation, reperfusion (PCI with a stent, or a clot-dissolving drug) reopens the vessel, and a statin, beta-blocker and ACE inhibitor reduce further events and remodelling. These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. Plaque erosion versus rupture, vasospasm, microvascular disease, time-dependence of salvage, reperfusion injury, arrhythmias, mechanical complications, contraindications, drug choice and all doses are left out. The ECG is a single schematic lead and the heart is a simplified silhouette with one surface artery shown. The figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
