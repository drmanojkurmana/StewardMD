What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How portal hypertension develops. In cirrhosis, scarring and regenerating nodules narrow the liver's sinusoids and the liver's blood vessels also constrict, so resistance to portal flow rises. Widening of the splanchnic arteries then increases inflow, which keeps portal pressure high. Blood is diverted through portosystemic collaterals (for example gastro-oesophageal varices) that bypass the liver. Consequences shown: variceal bleeding, ascites (high pressure in the gut capillaries, low albumin, and renal sodium and water retention), splenic congestion with enlargement and low platelets (hypersplenism), and hepatic encephalopathy as ammonia and other gut-derived toxins reach the brain without being cleared. This is standard teaching in hepatology and medicine texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Treatment ideas shown. A non-selective beta-blocker lowers portal inflow and so portal pressure, and is used to prevent first bleeding or rebleeding. Endoscopic band ligation strangles the varices but does not lower portal pressure. Diuretics with salt restriction clear ascites without treating the pressure itself. Lactulose reduces the gut's ammonia load and treats or prevents encephalopathy. A TIPS (transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt) is a stent from portal to hepatic vein that lowers portal pressure and treats refractory ascites or bleeding that other measures do not control, but it sends more ammonia past the liver and can worsen encephalopathy. The figure shows the direction of each effect, not how much to give, when, or who qualifies.
