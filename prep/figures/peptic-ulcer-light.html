What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How a peptic ulcer forms. The stomach and duodenum are normally protected by a mucus and bicarbonate layer, mucosal blood flow and prostaglandin-supported repair. Helicobacter pylori inflames the mucosa and disturbs acid control, while NSAIDs block prostaglandin production and thin that defence. Acid and pepsin then erode a crater through the mucosa. A crater that reaches a vessel can bleed, and one that passes through the whole wall can perforate. Gastric ulcers lean more on weakened defence, duodenal ulcers more on Helicobacter pylori and acid. This is standard teaching in medicine and gastroenterology texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Treatment ideas shown. Acid suppression lowers acid and pepsin activity so the crater can heal, eradication removes Helicobacter pylori so ulcers are less likely to return, and stopping the NSAID lets prostaglandin-supported defence recover. These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. Gastrin and acid hypersecretion syndromes, gastric cancer in a non-healing gastric ulcer, stress ulceration, other drugs and alcohol, endoscopy and surgery, transfusion, and all doses and durations are left out. Gastric, duodenal and complicated ulcers are shown on one shared wall cross-section, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
