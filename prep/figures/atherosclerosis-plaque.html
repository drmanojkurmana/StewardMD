What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How an atherosclerotic plaque develops. Endothelial injury and retained low-density lipoprotein (LDL) in the intima start the process. LDL becomes oxidised, monocytes enter and become macrophages that take up oxidised LDL and turn into foam cells, forming a fatty streak. Smooth muscle cells migrate into the intima and make a fibrous cap. Foam cells die and leave a necrotic lipid core. A stable plaque has a thick cap and a smaller core; a vulnerable plaque has a thin cap, a large core and more inflammation. Cap rupture exposes core material and triggers platelet clotting and thrombosis. This is standard teaching in pathology and cardiology texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Treatment ideas shown. A statin lowers LDL entry and plaque lipid and helps stabilise the cap. An antiplatelet drug reduces platelet clotting after rupture but does not shrink the plaque. Risk factor control (for example not smoking, and controlling blood pressure, blood sugar and lipids) reduces endothelial injury and oxidation. These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. Calcification, plaque erosion, healing and remodelling, collateral vessels, individual lipid and blood pressure targets, drug choice, and all doses are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any person should be treated.
