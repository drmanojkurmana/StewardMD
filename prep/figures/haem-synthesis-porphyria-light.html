What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How haem is made. The first step is in the mitochondrion: glycine and succinyl-CoA are joined by ALA synthase to make aminolaevulinic acid (ALA). In the cytosol, ALA dehydratase joins ALA molecules to make porphobilinogen (PBG), and PBG deaminase plus later cytosol steps build porphyrin intermediates. Back in the mitochondrion the last steps run, and ferrochelatase inserts iron into protoporphyrin to make haem. Haem feeds back to restrain ALA synthase. This is standard teaching in biochemistry and haematology texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Lead and acute intermittent porphyria. Lead inhibits ALA dehydratase and ferrochelatase, so ALA and porphyrin intermediates build up while haem falls. Acute intermittent porphyria is a partial defect of PBG deaminase; when ALA synthase is driven up, ALA and PBG build up ahead of the faulty step and attacks bring abdominal pain and neuropathy. Haem and glucose restrain ALA synthase, chelation removes lead, and avoiding triggers reduces attacks. These are teaching toggles only.
