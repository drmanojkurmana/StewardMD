What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • The dopamine hypothesis in brief. Excess dopamine signalling in the mesolimbic pathway (ventral tegmental area to nucleus accumbens and related limbic areas) is linked to positive symptoms such as delusions and hallucinations. Too little dopamine signalling in the mesocortical pathway (ventral tegmental area to prefrontal cortex) is linked to negative symptoms (blunted affect, avolition, social withdrawal) and cognitive symptoms. The nigrostriatal pathway (substantia nigra to striatum) controls movement, and the tuberoinfundibular pathway (arcuate nucleus of the hypothalamus to the pituitary) normally restrains prolactin release. This is standard teaching in psychiatry and psychopharmacology and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • What D2 blockade does. Antipsychotics block dopamine D2 receptors. Blocking the mesolimbic pathway reduces positive symptoms, but D2 receptors cannot be blocked in one pathway alone. Nigrostriatal blockade causes extrapyramidal side effects (acute dystonia, parkinsonism, akathisia and, with time, tardive dyskinesia). Tuberoinfundibular blockade removes dopamine restraint on the pituitary, so prolactin rises. Cortical dopamine deficit is not corrected by D2 blockade, so negative and cognitive symptoms often persist. This is flagged for review.
  • Typical compared with atypical. In this teaching simplification, a typical (first generation) antipsychotic blocks D2 receptors strongly across all pathways, so movement effects and prolactin rise are prominent at high blockade. An atypical (second generation) antipsychotic is drawn as achieving similar mesolimbic blockade with relatively less nigrostriatal and tuberoinfundibular blockade, so movement effects and prolactin rise are smaller for the same treated state. Individual agents differ a lot, and some atypical agents raise prolactin clearly; the figure does not rank real medicines. These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. Glutamate, serotonin, neurodevelopmental and genetic accounts of schizophrenia, positive symptom circuitry beyond the mesolimbic pathway, subtypes and time course of extrapyramidal effects, galactorrhoea and bone effects of raised prolactin, metabolic and other adverse effects, agent by agent differences within each class, clozapine and treatment resistance, psychological and social treatment, and all doses and drug choices are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
