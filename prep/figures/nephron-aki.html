What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How the kidney filters. Blood enters the glomerulus through the afferent arteriole. The filtration barrier (capillary lining, basement membrane and podocytes) lets water and small solutes into Bowman space while keeping blood cells and most protein in the blood. The filtrate is then reworked along the tubule (the proximal tubule reclaims most of it) and leaves as urine through the ureter into the bladder. This is standard physiology teaching and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Three broad groups of acute kidney injury. Prerenal: too little blood reaches the glomerulus (low volume, poor pump function, drugs that change glomerular blood flow), so filtration falls while the kidney itself is intact; if it goes on, tubule cells can die. Intrinsic: damage inside the kidney, most often acute tubular necrosis from poor blood flow or a toxin (dead tubule cells shed and form casts), or disease of the glomerulus or interstitium. Postrenal: obstruction to drainage (for example a stone or an enlarged prostate) raises pressure back toward the kidney and lowers filtration. Flagged for review.
  • Nephritic versus nephrotic. A nephritic picture is an inflamed glomerulus: red cells in the urine, red cell casts, often high blood pressure and swelling, and reduced filtration. A nephrotic picture is a leaky filter: heavy protein loss in the urine with low blood albumin and swelling, while filtration may stay near normal early on. Some immune-driven forms respond to steroids or other immune treatment, but many causes need different management. Flagged for review.
  • Treatment ideas shown. Fluids for low volume, relieving a blockage, a steroid for an immune-driven glomerular disease, and stopping a kidney-damaging toxin. These are teaching toggles only; each helps only when it matches the cause, which is the point of the figure.
  • Organ art. Organ art is NIH BioArt Source, public domain.

What was simplified. Real cases overlap (for example prolonged low blood flow becomes tubule injury, and obstruction can follow other injury), and the interstitial and vascular causes, fluid overload, electrolyte and acid-base effects, the need for dialysis, the many causes of each glomerular disease, and all doses and targets are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
