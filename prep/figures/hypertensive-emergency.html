What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How a hypertensive emergency develops. When pressure rises, small arteries narrow to protect the tissue beyond them (autoregulation). If pressure rises too far or too fast, that protection is overwhelmed: the vessel is forced open, flow and pressure break through to fragile capillaries, the endothelium is injured, plasma proteins and fibrin enter the wall (fibrinoid necrosis), and the wall weakens and leaks. Target organs then fail: brain swelling (encephalopathy), acute kidney injury, heart strain and failure, a torn aorta, and retinal damage with a swollen optic disc (papilloedema). Malignant hypertension is the form with that eye picture. This is standard teaching in medicine and cardiology texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Treatment ideas shown. In an emergency the pressure is lowered in a controlled, gradual way so organ perfusion is not lost, alongside therapy aimed at the damaged organ. Urgency without organ damage is usually managed with oral therapy and close follow up. These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. Secondary causes of hypertension, chronic autoregulatory resetting, drug choice, infusion agents, exact reduction targets and timing, pregnancy, and organ-specific procedures are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
