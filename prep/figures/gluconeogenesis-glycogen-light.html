What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • Fed and fasting glucose control. After a meal, insulin promotes glucose uptake and glycogen synthesis (glycogenesis) in liver and muscle and promotes glycolysis, while suppressing glycogen breakdown and new glucose production. As fasting begins, falling insulin and rising glucagon activate glycogen breakdown (glycogenolysis); glucose-6-phosphate is converted to free glucose by glucose-6-phosphatase in the liver and released into blood. With prolonged fasting glycogen runs out and gluconeogenesis takes over, making glucose from lactate, glucogenic amino acids and glycerol. Phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (PEPCK) is a key regulated step, and glucose-6-phosphatase is the shared final step for both glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis. This is standard teaching in biochemistry and medicine texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • The storage defect and treatments shown. In glucose-6-phosphatase deficiency (von Gierke disease, glycogen storage disease type I), glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis cannot release free glucose, so fasting hypoglycaemia occurs with glycogen accumulation and diversion of glucose-6-phosphate to lactate. Frequent feeds supply glucose from outside and so bypass the blocked step; glucagon cannot help when the final step is blocked or when glycogen is exhausted. These are teaching toggles only and show direction of effect, not how any person should be treated.

What was simplified. Muscle glycogen (which cannot release free glucose), the kidney contribution in very prolonged fasting, fatty-acid oxidation and ketones as alternative fuels, individual transport steps, and hormonal detail beyond insulin and glucagon are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
