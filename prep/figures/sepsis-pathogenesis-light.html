What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How sepsis develops. Microbial molecules (for example bacterial endotoxin) bind pattern-recognition receptors such as Toll-like receptors on macrophages and other cells. That switches on inflammatory genes (NF-kB) and releases cytokines (TNF, IL-1, IL-6). On blood-vessel lining cells these cause nitric-oxide-driven vasodilation, a leaky capillary wall, and activation of clotting (tissue factor, microthrombi). The result is low blood pressure, poor organ perfusion and a rising lactate. This is standard teaching in medicine and critical-care texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Treatment ideas shown. Treating the source (antibiotics, drainage), restoring circulating volume with fluids, and tightening vessels with a vasopressor. These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. The anti-inflammatory and immune-paralysis phase, organ-specific damage, mitochondrial dysfunction, resistant organisms, steroid and other adjuncts, source control by drainage or surgery, and all doses and targets are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
