What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How sickle cell disease develops. A single point change in the beta globin gene swaps one amino acid in the beta chain, making haemoglobin S (HbS). Oxygenated HbS behaves much like normal haemoglobin, but deoxygenated HbS is sticky and joins into long polymers. The polymers stiffen and bend the red cell into a sickle. Sickled cells jam in small vessels (vaso-occlusion), causing pain and organ injury, and they survive a shorter time in the circulation, so haemolysis causes anaemia and jaundice. A person with one copy (trait) makes mostly normal haemoglobin and sickles little. This is standard teaching in medicine and haematology texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Treatment ideas shown. Hydroxycarbamide raises fetal haemoglobin (HbF), which does not join the polymer and so reduces sickling. Hydration and analgesia support flow and ease pain during a crisis without changing the haemoglobin. Transfusion adds red cells with normal haemoglobin and dilutes the sickled cells. These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. Oxygen affinity, HbF distribution between cells, adhesion to the vessel wall, inflammation, nitric oxide loss, stroke and leg ulcer and priapism detail, chronic organ screening, gene therapy and transplantation, drug choice and all doses are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
