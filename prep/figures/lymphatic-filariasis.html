What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How lymphatic filariasis develops. Infective larvae enter through the bite of a mosquito such as Culex and travel to the lymphatics, where they mature into adult worms. Adults live in lymph vessels and nodes and release microfilariae that appear in the blood at night, ready to be taken up by another mosquito. Living and dying worms and the host response injure lymph vessels, dilate them and impair valves, so lymph drains poorly. Early infection is often asymptomatic despite microfilaraemia. Acute attacks bring painful lymph vessel and node inflammation, often with secondary bacterial entry through damaged skin. Longstanding obstruction brings lymphoedema and elephantiasis of a limb and fluid around the testis (hydrocele). In tropical pulmonary eosinophilia the microfilariae are cleared in the lungs, blood microfilariae are absent, and eosinophils and cough or wheeze dominate. This is standard teaching in medicine, parasitology and tropical-medicine texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Treatment ideas shown. Diethylcarbamazine with albendazole clears microfilariae and affects adult worms, hygiene and careful limb care reduce acute attacks and help lymphoedema, and surgery can help selected hydrocele or severe swelling. Established lymphatic damage and swelling may persist despite clearing parasites. These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. The other filarial species and vectors in detail, larval development stages inside the mosquito, Wolbachia and doxycycline, mapping and mass treatment programmes, diagnostic tests and timing of blood samples, grading of lymphoedema, surgical techniques, and all doses and targets are left out. Tropical pulmonary eosinophilia is shown only as a raised eosinophil signal. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
