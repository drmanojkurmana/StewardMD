What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • Glycolysis in the cytosol. Glucose is trapped and split in a run of enzyme steps. Hexokinase is slowed by its own product, glucose-6-phosphate. PFK-1 is the main control point: it is sped up when the cell is short of energy (AMP and fructose-2,6-bisphosphate) and slowed by plenty of ATP and by citrate arriving from the mitochondrion. Pyruvate kinase, near the end of the chain, is sped up by an earlier intermediate and slowed when energy is plentiful. The chain gives a small net gain of ATP and makes pyruvate. This is standard teaching in biochemistry and physiology texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • The aerobic route. When oxygen and mitochondria are working, pyruvate is carried into the mitochondrion and pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) turns it into acetyl-CoA, releasing carbon dioxide. PDH needs thiamine, in its thiamine pyrophosphate form, as one of its cofactors, so thiamine deficiency can hold this step back and push pyruvate toward lactate. Acetyl-CoA then enters the TCA cycle (also called the Krebs or tricarboxylic acid cycle), which releases carbon dioxide and loads carriers as NADH and FADH2. Those carriers feed the electron transport chain, where oxygen is the final acceptor and most ATP is made. Giving thiamine only helps when thiamine is lacking; this scene shows it as a teaching toggle for a cofactor-limited PDH step. Without oxygen the cycle stalls even when thiamine is present, because the carriers cannot unload.
  • The anaerobic route. Without enough oxygen, the electron transport chain stalls, NADH cannot be unloaded, and the TCA cycle slows. Pyruvate is instead turned into lactate by lactate dehydrogenase, which frees the carrier needed to keep glycolysis running. The cell gains only the small glycolytic ATP yield, works less efficiently, and lactate builds up. Restoring oxygen lets the mitochondrial route take over again. These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. Most individual intermediates, the electron transport chain itself, the malate-aspartate and glycerol phosphate shuttles, fatty acid and amino acid entry into the cycle, glycogen breakdown, gluconeogenesis, tissue differences such as red cells having no mitochondria, and all doses and targets are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
