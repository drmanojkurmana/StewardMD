What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • Hodgkin lymphoma. The malignant Reed-Sternberg cells (large, often with two nuclei like owl eyes, CD30 positive and usually CD15 positive, typically without CD20) are a minority scattered in a reactive mix of lymphocytes, eosinophils, plasma cells and histiocytes. It tends to spread in an orderly way to neighbouring node groups. This is standard teaching in pathology and haematology texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma. A clonal sheet of B cells (CD20 positive) grows monotonously and effaces the normal follicles and zones of the node. Spread is less orderly and organs or bone marrow may be involved early. Subtypes, grading and molecular groups are not shown.
  • Stage and B symptoms. Limited stage means disease confined to one side of the diaphragm in a small number of node regions; advanced stage means both sides of the diaphragm, or spread to organs such as liver or bone marrow (the Ann Arbor idea, shown in words rather than numerals). The B symptoms are fever, drenching night sweats and weight loss, and they make the outlook and the treatment plan more urgent.
  • Treatment ideas shown. Chemotherapy acts throughout the body in both kinds. An anti-CD20 antibody (such as rituximab) binds CD20, so it helps in B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma and not in classic Hodgkin lymphoma. Radiotherapy is a local field, strongest for limited disease and unable on its own to reach widespread disease. These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. Lymphoma subtypes and grading, PET and other imaging, biopsy markers beyond the few named, bone marrow biopsy detail, prognosis scores, relapsed disease, stem cell transplant, targeted and immune treatments other than anti-CD20, and all doses and schedules are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
