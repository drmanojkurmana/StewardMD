What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • What shock is. Shock is a circulation that cannot deliver enough oxygen to the tissues. Blood pressure is roughly output times vessel tone, so each type fails in a different place: too little blood (hypovolaemic), a failing pump (cardiogenic), vessels that have lost their tone (distributive, for example septic, anaphylactic or neurogenic), or a block to flow (obstructive, for example tamponade, pulmonary embolism or tension pneumothorax). The classic bedside pattern of output, filling pressure and vessel tone differs by type. This is standard teaching in medicine and critical-care texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Treatment ideas shown. Fluids or blood fit a low volume, an inotrope fits a weak pump, a vasopressor fits lost vessel tone, and relieving the obstruction fits a block. Treating the cause matters most in every type. A wrong choice gives little benefit or harm (for example fluids in a failing pump worsen lung congestion). These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. Mixed and evolving shock, compensatory stages, specific agents, monitoring, targets, doses and the many causes within each type are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
