What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How incretins and their agonists act. Gut enteroendocrine cells release incretins after meals: L cells make glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) and K cells make glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP). In the pancreas, both stimulate beta-cell insulin secretion in a glucose-dependent manner (low intrinsic hypoglycaemia risk when used alone), and GLP-1 suppresses inappropriate glucagon secretion from alpha cells. In the stomach, GLP-1 slows gastric emptying, blunting postprandial glucose spikes. In the hypothalamus and brainstem, GLP-1 and GIP signal satiety and reduce hunger cravings. Endogenous GLP-1 and GIP are rapidly cleaved within minutes by dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4). Semaglutide (a GLP-1 receptor agonist) and tirzepatide (a dual GIP and GLP-1 receptor agonist) are engineered with fatty diacid side chains that bind albumin and resist DPP-4, extending their half-lives to about one week.
  • Dual incretin agonism. Tirzepatide engages both GIP and GLP-1 receptors. Synergy between GIP and GLP-1 receptor pathways produces potent glucose-dependent insulin release, prominent central appetite suppression and marked weight reduction, often with slightly less acute gastric slowing than selective GLP-1 agonism.
  • What follows from the action (flagged for clinician review). Common adverse effects reflect the pharmacology: gastrointestinal symptoms including nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea or constipation (from slowed gastrointestinal transit and vagal signaling), reduced appetite, gallbladder events (such as cholelithiasis from rapid weight loss and gallbladder stasis), and rare pancreatitis. Hypoglycaemia risk is low with monotherapy because insulin secretion is glucose-dependent, but increases when combined with insulin or sulfonylureas. Caution is advised with personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma or multiple endocrine neoplasia syndrome type 2 (MEN2), and in severe gastrointestinal disease.
