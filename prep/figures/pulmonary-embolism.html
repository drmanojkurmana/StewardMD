What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How pulmonary embolism develops. A deep vein thrombosis, usually in the calf or thigh, sheds pieces that travel with venous blood through the right side of the heart into the pulmonary arteries, where they lodge as emboli. The blocked region keeps being ventilated but is no longer perfused, which is alveolar dead space: it wastes ventilation and lowers the oxygen in blood leaving the lungs. The right ventricle must pump against the sudden blockage, so its pressure rises, the septum bows toward the left, output falls and blood pressure can drop. An anticoagulant stops the clot growing and new clot forming, a clot-dissolving drug breaks the embolus up, and oxygen with careful fluids supports the patient while the body clears the clot. This is standard teaching in medicine and respiratory texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Treatment ideas shown. An anticoagulant to stop growth and further clots, a clot-dissolving drug to break up a large blocking embolus, and oxygen with careful fluids to support oxygen levels and output. These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. Risk factors and prevention, clot in the heart or already in the lungs, saddle and chronic emboli, pulmonary infarction, diagnosis by scan and blood tests, filters, surgery to remove clot, bleeding risk, fluid harm in a failing right ventricle, and all doses and targets are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
