What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How oxygen binding works. Haemoglobin has four subunits. Binding of the first oxygen makes the next ones easier to bind, and release is likewise cooperative, which gives the sigmoid dissociation curve. P50 is the oxygen partial pressure at half saturation: a higher P50 means lower affinity and easier release, a lower P50 means higher affinity and tighter holding. In the lungs, where oxygen partial pressure is high, haemoglobin loads; in the tissues, where it is lower, haemoglobin unloads. This is standard teaching in physiology texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Shifts and the Bohr effect. Raised carbon dioxide and hydrogen ions (the Bohr effect), raised temperature and raised 2,3-BPG shift the curve right and favour unloading to active tissues. Fetal haemoglobin, carbon monoxide and alkalosis shift it left and favour holding oxygen. Acid-base correction is shown as a teaching toggle that moves an acid or alkali driven shift back toward the normal position; it does not correct fetal haemoglobin, carbon monoxide, temperature or 2,3-BPG effects in full.

What was simplified. Myoglobin, methaemoglobin, detailed allosteric structure, the chloride shift, carbamino carriage, altitude adaptation, transfusion and all clinical thresholds and values are left out. The lung and tissue panels are schematic compartments, not anatomy. The figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
