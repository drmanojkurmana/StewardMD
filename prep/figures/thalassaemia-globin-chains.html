What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How thalassaemia develops. Alpha thalassaemia usually reflects loss of one or more of the four alpha globin genes; beta thalassaemia reflects reduced or absent beta chain production from the beta globin genes. Either way the two chains of adult haemoglobin are made in unequal amounts. The unpaired excess chains precipitate inside developing red cells, damaging membranes and killing many precursors in the marrow (ineffective erythropoiesis). Surviving red cells are microcytic and hypochromic and are removed early, chiefly in the spleen (haemolysis). Anaemia drives marrow expansion, which reshapes growing bones, and it suppresses hepcidin, so more iron is absorbed from the gut; regular transfusion adds further iron, which can load the liver, heart and endocrine organs. Trait is usually mild, intermedia is intermediate, and major presents early with severe anaemia. This is standard teaching in haematology texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Treatment ideas shown. Transfusion restores haemoglobin and suppresses the ineffective marrow but adds iron. Chelation binds excess iron so it can be removed, without fixing the chain defect. Transplant replaces the marrow with donor marrow that makes balanced chains. These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. The separate genetics and naming of alpha and beta subtypes, HbH and Hb Bart hydrops detail, fetal haemoglobin switching, splenectomy, growth and endocrine monitoring, gene therapy, drug choice and all doses are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
