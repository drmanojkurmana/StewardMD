What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • The chromosome change and the clone. Chronic myeloid leukaemia is a clonal myeloid neoplasm of a haematopoietic stem cell. A reciprocal swap between chromosomes 9 and 22 creates the Philadelphia chromosome and joins BCR on chromosome 22 to ABL on chromosome 9. The fusion gene makes a tyrosine kinase that stays switched on without its usual controls, so signalling for growth and survival never stops and granulocyte production expands. The clone gradually takes over haematopoiesis in the marrow. This is standard teaching in haematology texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Phases, blood and spleen. In chronic phase the excess cells still mature, so granulocytes and sometimes platelets rise, red cell production is often fairly preserved at first, and the enlarged spleen may be the main finding. Further genetic aberrations and mutations can occur as the clone evolves. In accelerated phase maturation falters, blasts and basophils rise and anaemia deepens. In blast phase immature blasts dominate like an acute leukaemia, normal marrow fails, and anaemia, infection and bleeding follow. Germline predisposition is uncommon in this disease; radiation is a recognised risk factor in classic teaching. Classification follows the phase and the fusion, in line with World Health Organization style grouping of myeloid neoplasms. This is standard teaching and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Treatment ideas shown. A tyrosine kinase inhibitor blocks the BCR-ABL kinase and is highly effective in chronic phase, less so once blast phase and resistant variants develop. Chemotherapy reduces the dividing clone less selectively. Stem cell transplant can replace the clonal stem cell population and remains a potentially curative option for selected patients, previously the main curative route before kinase inhibitors. These are teaching toggles only and the likely response varies by phase.

What was simplified. Other chromosome aberrations and kinase domain variants, basophil and eosinophil detail, risk scores, monitoring of the fusion transcript, second generation inhibitors, transplant conditioning and graft effects, and all drug choices and doses are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
