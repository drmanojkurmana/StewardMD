What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How a pneumothorax develops. The pleural space is normally a thin sealed space that couples the lung to the chest wall. Air entering it, through a chest wall injury or from a ruptured bleb (a small air blister on the lung surface), breaks that coupling. Intrapleural pressure rises toward atmospheric pressure, the lung recoils and collapses, and breath sounds and oxygen uptake fall on that side. In a tension pneumothorax the entry acts as a one-way valve: air enters as the patient breathes in but cannot leave, pressure keeps rising, the mediastinum and trachea are pushed away from that side, the great veins are compressed, venous return and cardiac output fall, and obstructive shock follows. This is standard teaching in medicine and respiratory texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Treatment ideas shown. A small pneumothorax in a stable patient may be observed. Needle decompression lets trapped air out in tension, followed by a chest drain that keeps removing air so the lung can re-expand. Oxygen can support oxygen levels and may help pleural air be absorbed, but it does not remove trapped air or relieve tension. These are teaching toggles only.
