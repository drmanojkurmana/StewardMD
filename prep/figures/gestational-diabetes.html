What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How gestational diabetes develops. As the placenta grows it releases hormones including human placental lactogen and progesterone, with cortisol also contributing. These raise insulin resistance in maternal muscle, fat and liver, so glucose is taken up less readily and the liver releases more. The pancreatic beta cell normally compensates by making more insulin. When it cannot compensate enough, maternal glucose rises. This is standard teaching in medicine, obstetrics and endocrinology texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Effects on the fetus. Maternal glucose crosses the placenta, but maternal insulin does not cross in the same way. The fetus responds with its own extra insulin (fetal hyperinsulinism). Fetal insulin is a growth signal, so extra growth and fat follow (macrosomia). After birth the maternal glucose supply stops at once while fetal insulin remains high for a time, so the newborn can develop low glucose (neonatal hypoglycaemia). Flagged for review.
  • Treatment ideas shown. Diet and exercise lower the glucose load and improve uptake. Metformin improves insulin sensitivity and reduces liver glucose release. Insulin replaces the shortfall in insulin action and lowers maternal glucose, which in turn lowers glucose crossing to the fetus. These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. Screening and diagnosis, glucose targets, fetal monitoring, delivery timing, other medicines, maternal and fetal complications beyond extra growth and newborn low glucose, and all doses are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any person should be treated.
