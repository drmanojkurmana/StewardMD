What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How syphilis develops. Treponema pallidum is usually sexually transmitted and enters through mucosa or broken skin. After an incubation period it causes a usually painless, nontender primary chancre at the entry site, with local lymphadenopathy; the lesion is infectious and full of organisms, then heals even without treatment. Weeks later, secondary disease follows blood-borne spread: widespread lesions including a rash, condylomata lata and mucous patches, generalised lymphadenopathy, and sometimes ocular involvement. Macrophages and the immune response partly control organisms. Serologic tests become reactive: nontreponemal tests such as VDRL track activity, while treponemal tests usually stay reactive. A latent stage with no lesions may follow. In some untreated persons, late tertiary disease develops years later: gumma lesions, cardiovascular involvement such as aortitis, and neurosyphilis. Infection in pregnancy can cross the placenta and cause congenital syphilis. This is standard teaching in medicine and infectious-disease texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Treatment ideas shown. Penicillin clears organisms and heals lesions, and a regimen that reaches the nervous system is used when neurosyphilis is present. Follow-up serology only tracks the nontreponemal response; on its own it does not clear organisms. Existing late damage may persist even after organisms are cleared. These are teaching toggles only.
