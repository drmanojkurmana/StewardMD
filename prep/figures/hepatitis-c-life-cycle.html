What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How hepatitis C infects a hepatocyte. Hepatitis C virus (HCV) is an enveloped, positive-sense RNA virus. It enters hepatocytes through a set of surface receptors, releases its single RNA genome into the cytoplasm (it does not need the nucleus or integrate into host DNA), and the host ribosome translates that RNA into one long polyprotein on the endoplasmic reticulum. Viral and host proteases cleave the polyprotein: the NS3 protease is a key viral cutter. The NS5B RNA-dependent RNA polymerase, organised with NS5A and other non-structural proteins in a membrane replication complex, copies the RNA. New genomes and structural proteins assemble at lipid droplets and the endoplasmic reticulum, and virions leave the cell. This is standard teaching in medicine, virology and hepatology texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • From infection to cirrhosis and cancer, and the treatment ideas shown. Many infections become chronic. Years of immune inflammation and hepatocyte injury and repair lay down scar tissue (fibrosis), which can progress to cirrhosis and raise the risk of liver failure and hepatocellular carcinoma (liver cancer). Direct-acting antivirals target viral proteins: NS3 protease inhibitors stop polyprotein cleavage, NS5B polymerase inhibitors stop RNA copying, and NS5A inhibitors disrupt the replication complex and assembly; modern treatment combines such agents. These are teaching toggles only, not a regimen.

What was simplified. Entry-receptor detail, genotypes and resistance, lipid and lipoprotein handling, acute clearance rates, biopsy and elastography, transplant, cancer surveillance and treatment, extrahepatic effects, and all doses, regimens and cure-rate numbers are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
