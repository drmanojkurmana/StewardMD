What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How water balance is controlled. Antidiuretic hormone (ADH, also called vasopressin) is made in the hypothalamus and released from the posterior pituitary. It acts on V2 receptors in the collecting duct to insert aquaporin-2 water channels, so water is reabsorbed and urine becomes small and concentrated. Without that signal, or when the duct cannot respond, aquaporin-2 stays out of the membrane, water is lost as large volumes of dilute urine, blood sodium rises and thirst is driven. This is standard teaching in medicine and physiology texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • The disorders and treatments shown. SIADH is excess ADH effect with water retention, dilutional hyponatraemia and concentrated urine: limiting fluid intake is the teaching toggle shown. Central diabetes insipidus is ADH deficiency and responds to desmopressin, a synthetic ADH analogue. Nephrogenic diabetes insipidus is duct resistance to ADH, so desmopressin does not correct it; a thiazide can paradoxically reduce urine volume by causing mild volume depletion and more proximal reabsorption. The figure shows direction of effect only.

What was simplified. Osmoreceptor and baroreceptor control loops, causes of each disorder, copeptin and diagnostic testing, urine and plasma osmolality values, correction rates and osmotic demyelination, other treatments, and all doses and targets are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
