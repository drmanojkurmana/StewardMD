What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • Entamoeba histolytica. Infection follows swallowing mature cysts in food or water contaminated with feces, or by fecal oral contact. Cysts excyst in the small bowel and trophozoites reach the colon. Many infections stay in the lumen without symptoms, and stool tests including antigen and nucleic acid tests (NAATs) help tell invasive species apart. When trophozoites attach to and digest the colonic lining they invade the epithelium, recruit inflammation and undermine the surface, making flask shaped ulcers. That gives abdominal pain and bloody, frequent small volume stool (amoebic dysentery), which can become severe with perforation in vulnerable persons. Trophozoites can also enter portal blood and reach the liver, where a usually single abscess forms. Its centre is acellular necrotic material likened to anchovy paste, with trophozoites at the rim rather than in the centre. This is standard teaching in medicine, microbiology and infectious disease texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Giardia. Giardia cysts are swallowed in contaminated water or food or by fecal oral spread, excyst in the small bowel, and trophozoites attach to the duodenal lining with a ventral sucking disc. They do not invade the epithelium. Coating of the surface, villus blunting and disturbed fat absorption give bloating, cramps and bulky, greasy, offensive stool (steatorrhoea) with malabsorption, although many infected persons have no symptoms. Stool antigen and NAATs are commonly used because cysts and trophozoites are shed unevenly. Other intestinal protozoa such as Cryptosporidium, whose oocysts resist chlorination and can cause severe watery diarrhoea in immunocompromised persons, are mentioned here only to place the group and are not drawn. This summary was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Treatment ideas shown. Metronidazole is shown as a tissue agent that kills invasive trophozoites in the colon wall and liver and also acts against Giardia. A luminal agent is shown clearing parasites and cysts that remain in the bowel lumen after tissue treatment, which matters in amoebiasis to stop relapse and spread. Drainage of a liver abscess is shown as rarely needed, reserved for selected large, left sided, complicated or poorly responding abscesses alongside drug treatment. These are teaching toggles only.
