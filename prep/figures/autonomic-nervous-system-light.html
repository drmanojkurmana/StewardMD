What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • Two outflows. Sympathetic fibres leave the thoracolumbar cord, relay in the paravertebral chain or prevertebral ganglia, and travel far to targets. Parasympathetic fibres leave the craniosacral outflow, mostly in the vagus nerve, plus sacral fibres to pelvic organs, and relay in ganglia close to or inside the target organ. In both systems the preganglionic fibre releases acetylcholine onto nicotinic receptors. Sympathetic postganglionic fibres mostly release noradrenaline onto alpha and beta adrenoceptors, parasympathetic postganglionic fibres release acetylcholine onto muscarinic receptors. This is standard teaching in physiology and pharmacology texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Opposite effects, with exceptions. Sympathetic activity speeds the heart, widens the pupil and airways, slows gut and holds urine while tightening vessels. Parasympathetic activity slows the heart, narrows the pupil and airways, moves the gut and empties the bladder. Sweat glands and most vessels have little parasympathetic supply, and sympathetic fibres to sweat glands release acetylcholine; those exceptions are noted in the text but simplified in the drawing.
  • Treatment ideas shown. A beta blocker blunts beta receptor effects at the heart and airways, a muscarinic antagonist blunts muscarinic effects and so mimics some sympathetic signs, and an alpha agonist tightens vessels and widens the pupil. These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. Prevertebral ganglia, the adrenal medulla, enteric plexuses, baroreflex feedback, sweat gland cholinergic sympathetic fibres, receptor subtypes beyond alpha, beta and muscarinic, and every drug and dose are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
