What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How adenosine acts. Adenosine binds A1 receptors on AV nodal and sinoatrial cells. The receptor is Gi coupled: potassium channels open, the cell hyperpolarises, and calcium current falls. Conduction through the AV node slows and then blocks briefly, while the SA node also slows. Because many re-entrant supraventricular tachycardias need the AV node as part of the circuit, a brief AV block can terminate the circuit and restore sinus rhythm. Adenosine is broken down very fast, so the block and the pause are transient, sometimes with a short period of asystole before sinus rhythm returns. This is standard teaching in pharmacology and cardiology texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Unwanted effects and interactions shown. Flushing, chest tightness, breathlessness from bronchospasm and a transient pause or asystole can occur. Caffeine and theophylline are adenosine receptor antagonists and can blunt the effect. Dipyridamole blocks adenosine uptake and potentiates it. These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. AVNRT versus AVRT subtypes, accessory pathways, vagal manoeuvres, other rate control drugs, pacing and ablation, atrial fibrillation, dosing, contraindications in detail, and all numeric rates and targets are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
