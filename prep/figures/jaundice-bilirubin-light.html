What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How bilirubin is handled. Old or damaged red cells are broken down, mainly in the spleen. Haem from haemoglobin becomes unconjugated bilirubin, which is not water soluble, travels in blood bound to albumin, and cannot appear in urine. Hepatocytes take it up and conjugate it (glucuronidation), making water soluble conjugated bilirubin that is excreted in bile. In the gut, bacteria convert it to urobilinogen; most becomes brown stercobilin in stool and a little is reabsorbed and excreted by the kidney. This is standard textbook teaching in physiology and medicine and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • The three patterns. Pre-hepatic jaundice (for example excess haemolysis): mainly unconjugated bilirubin, no bilirubin in urine, more urobilinogen and normal to dark stool. Hepatic jaundice (liver cell injury or impaired conjugation): a mixed rise, bilirubin may appear in urine. Post-hepatic jaundice (blocked bile duct): mainly conjugated bilirubin, dark urine with bilirubin, pale clay coloured stool and little urobilinogen. This is standard teaching and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Treatment ideas shown. Phototherapy uses light on the skin to break down unconjugated bilirubin, relieving an obstruction restores bile flow, and treating the underlying cause tackles haemolysis or liver injury at its source. These are teaching toggles only.
