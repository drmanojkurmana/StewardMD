What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • What DIC is. Disseminated intravascular coagulation is not a disease in itself but a syndrome triggered by an underlying cause: sepsis, major trauma or burns, malignancy and obstetric emergencies are classic triggers. Tissue factor (with activated endothelium and monocytes) switches the clotting system on throughout the circulation instead of at one injury. Widespread microthrombi form in small vessels and impair organ perfusion, while platelets, clotting factors and fibrinogen are consumed faster than they can be replaced. Secondary fibrinolysis dissolves fibrin and raises fibrin degradation products such as D-dimer. The same patient therefore clots in small vessels and bleeds from depleted, weak clotting: oozing, purpura and organ dysfunction together. This is standard teaching in haematology and critical-care texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Treatment ideas shown. Treating the underlying cause is the key step and is the only toggle that switches the drive off. Replacing platelets, plasma or fibrinogen concentrate refills what was used up but does not stop ongoing consumption, so on its own it only partly and temporarily helps. These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. Scoring systems and laboratory thresholds, the balance with natural anticoagulant pathways, organ-specific patterns, transfusion triggers and targets, heparin debates, and all doses are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
