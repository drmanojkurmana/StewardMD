What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How pericarditis develops. The pericardium has an inner visceral layer on the heart and an outer parietal layer. Infection, most often viral, or another cause inflames both layers. The inflamed surfaces roughen with fibrin and rub as the heart beats, giving pleuritic chest pain that is worse lying flat and on breathing in, and eased by sitting up and leaning forward, plus a scratchy friction rub. Inflammation across the heart surface gives widespread ST elevation and PR depression on the ECG rather than a change in one territory. This is standard teaching in medicine and cardiology texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Effusion, tamponade and constriction. Inflammation can weep fluid into the pericardial space as an effusion, which separates the layers so pain and the rub may fade. A large or fast effusion can squeeze the heart and progress toward tamponade. Healing can instead leave a thick, fibrotic and sometimes calcified shell: constrictive pericarditis, where the stiff pericardium stops the heart filling freely. This is standard teaching in medicine and cardiology texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Treatment ideas shown. Anti-inflammatory treatment calms the inflamed layers and eases pain. The colchicine concept is shown as a second way to dampen pericardial inflammation and lower the chance of recurrence. Drainage removes effusion fluid and relieves squeezing, but does not by itself calm acute inflammation or soften a constrictive shell. These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. Specific causes and their tests, pericardial pain referral in detail, ECG evolution through stages, echocardiographic and imaging signs, tamponade physiology in full, recurrence patterns, drug choice, combinations and all doses are left out. Sizes, speeds and levels are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
