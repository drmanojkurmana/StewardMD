What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How digoxin works. Digoxin inhibits the membrane sodium potassium ATPase pump. Sodium then accumulates inside the heart muscle cell, the sodium calcium exchanger removes less calcium, intracellular calcium rises and contraction becomes stronger (positive inotropy). Digoxin also increases vagal tone, slowing conduction through the AV node and slowing the pulse. This is standard teaching in pharmacology and cardiology texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Toxicity. Excess pump blockade gives nausea and vomiting, visual disturbance often described as yellow tinged vision or halos, confusion, and cardiac arrhythmias with AV block; acute severe poisoning raises extracellular potassium (hyperkalaemia) because the pump can no longer move potassium into cells. Low potassium (hypokalaemia) lets digoxin bind the pump more strongly and worsens toxicity, which is why correcting potassium matters. Teaching toggles only.
  • Treatment ideas shown. Stopping the drug, correcting potassium, and digoxin specific antibody fragments (Fab) that bind free digoxin in the blood so it can no longer block the pump. Severe cases need urgent hospital and specialist care.

What was simplified. Renal clearance and drug interactions, digoxin blood level testing and its timing, electrolyte correction details, pacing and other rhythm treatments, chronic versus acute poisoning differences, and all doses are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
