What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How cardiac arrest develops. In cardiac arrest the heart stops pumping effectively, so circulation and cardiac output are lost and the brain and other organs become ischaemic very quickly. Ventricular fibrillation and pulseless ventricular tachycardia are shockable rhythms, where a defibrillation shock may restore an organised rhythm. Asystole and pulseless electrical activity are non-shockable rhythms, where a shock does not help and the focus is on compressions, adrenaline and finding a reversible cause. This is standard teaching in resuscitation and cardiovascular texts, including the Harrison chapter on cardiovascular collapse, cardiac arrest and sudden cardiac death, and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Treatment ideas shown. The chain of survival: early recognition and call for help, high quality chest compressions with minimal interruption, early defibrillation for a shockable rhythm, adrenaline, treatment of reversible causes (the Hs and Ts: hypoxia, hypovolaemia, electrolyte disturbance, hypothermia, tamponade, toxins, tension pneumothorax and thrombosis), and post-arrest care after return of spontaneous circulation. These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. Airway and breathing detail, compression depth and rate targets, drug doses and timing, rhythm checks, airway devices, mechanical CPR, extracorporeal CPR, individual reversible cause treatments, targeted temperature and other post-arrest care detail, and all prognostic scoring are left out. The ECG is a single schematic lead and the heart, brain and kidney are simple symbols. The figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
