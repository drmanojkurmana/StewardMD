What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How organophosphate poisoning works. Organophosphates (some insecticides and nerve agents) bind and inhibit acetylcholinesterase, the enzyme in synaptic clefts that splits acetylcholine. Acetylcholine then accumulates and overstimulates muscarinic receptors (salivation, lacrimation, urination, defecation, gut cramps, miosis, bronchospasm and slow heart rate, grouped as SLUDGE signs) and nicotinic receptors (muscle twitching and fasciculation, then weakness and paralysis, including the breathing muscles). This is standard teaching in pharmacology and toxicology texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Treatment ideas shown. Atropine blocks muscarinic receptors, so it eases SLUDGE signs but does not restore the enzyme or fix paralysis. An oxime can reactivate the blocked enzyme before the bond ages, lowering acetylcholine at both receptor types. Supportive care (airway and breathing support) keeps oxygen going while the poison wears off. These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. Ageing of the enzyme bond, intermediate and delayed syndromes, atropine and oxime choice, seizures and their treatment, decontamination and staff safety, and all doses and targets are left out. One generic synapse stands in for gland, gut, heart and skeletal muscle junctions. The figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
