What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How the urea cycle works. Protein and amino acid breakdown releases ammonia, which is toxic, especially to the brain. In the liver, ammonia enters the urea cycle to be made into urea, which the kidney excretes. The first steps are inside mitochondria: carbamoyl phosphate synthetase makes carbamoyl phosphate, and ornithine transcarbamylase combines it with ornithine to make citrulline. Citrulline leaves the mitochondrion and, in the cytosol, is converted through argininosuccinate to arginine. Arginase then splits arginine into urea and ornithine, and ornithine returns to the mitochondrion to start again. This is standard teaching in biochemistry and medicine texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • What goes wrong in a defect. When an enzyme of the cycle is partly or completely defective, for example ornithine transcarbamylase deficiency, the cycle cannot keep up with ammonia production. Ammonia accumulates in blood (hyperammonaemia), reaches the brain and can cause vomiting, drowsiness, confusion and coma, while urea production falls. Flagged for review.
  • Treatment ideas shown. Restricting protein reduces the ammonia load. A nitrogen scavenger provides an alternative route for nitrogen to leave the body, bypassing the blocked cycle. Dialysis directly clears ammonia from the blood in a severe crisis. These are teaching toggles only, shown as concepts without any dose or product name.

What was simplified. Aspartate as the second nitrogen donor, fumarate and the link to the citric acid cycle, N-acetylglutamate activation of the first enzyme, other cycle defects and carriers, newborn screening, and all doses, thresholds and targets are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
