What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How ARDS develops. Acute respiratory distress syndrome follows direct pulmonary injury (for example pneumonia or aspiration) or indirect vascular injury from a systemic illness. In the early exudative phase the alveolar epithelial and capillary endothelial barrier is injured, vascular leak lets protein rich edema fluid flood the interstitial space and alveoli, hyaline membranes form on the alveolar wall, surfactant is lost and neutrophils enter. Flooded and collapsed alveoli still receive blood, so that blood bypasses gas exchange as a shunt and oxygen stays low despite added oxygen (refractory hypoxaemia). The chest shows widespread shadowing and the lung is stiff, so breathing or ventilation needs more pressure. A proliferative phase follows, with type II alveolar cell growth and early repair, and in some patients a fibrotic phase with interstitial fibrosis. This is standard teaching in critical care and respiratory medicine and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Treatment ideas shown. Lung-protective ventilation limits further injury from overstretch while the lung is stiff and uneven. Prone positioning makes ventilation and blood flow match more evenly and reduces shunt, so oxygen often rises. Treating the underlying cause removes the driver of epithelial and endothelial injury and lets leak, oedema and neutrophil influx settle. These are teaching toggles only and show direction of effect, not how any patient should be treated.

What was simplified. Diagnostic criteria and timing, oxygen thresholds and ratios, ventilator settings and pressures, fluid strategy, steroids and other adjuncts, extracorporeal support, the separate roles of each mediator, and right heart and vascular effects are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
