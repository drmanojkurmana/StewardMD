What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • Left to right shunts. In an atrial septal defect, ventricular septal defect and patent ductus arteriosus, higher pressure on the left side drives oxygen rich blood back into the right heart or pulmonary artery. Pulmonary blood flow rises, the lungs and left heart are volume loaded, and over time pulmonary vascular pressure can rise. This is standard teaching in paediatrics and cardiology texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Reversal and tetralogy. In Eisenmenger physiology pulmonary vascular resistance exceeds systemic resistance, the shunt reverses to right to left, and oxygen poor blood reaches the body, causing cyanosis. Closing the defect then removes a pressure relief route and can harm. In tetralogy of Fallot a ventricular septal defect with a narrowed right ventricular outlet drives a right to left shunt and reduced pulmonary flow; a duct kept open by prostaglandin can add pulmonary flow as a temporary teaching concept. Definitive care is specialist and surgical, and this figure does not describe any patient care.
  • Treatment ideas shown. Closing the defect for a simple left to right shunt, and prostaglandin only as the concept of keeping the ductus arteriosus open. No drug name beyond the class, dose or timing is shown.

What was simplified. Defect size and location, pressure measurements, pulmonary vascular resistance testing, associated lesions, endocarditis, heart failure staging, surgical and catheter techniques, prostaglandin indications in detail, and all doses are left out. Colours, counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
