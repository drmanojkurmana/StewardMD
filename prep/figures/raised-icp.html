What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • The Monro-Kellie doctrine and intracranial compliance. The rigid cranium contains brain tissue, blood and cerebrospinal fluid in fixed total volume. When an expanding mass or cerebral oedema develops, compensation initially displaces venous blood into jugular veins and CSF into the spinal subarachnoid space. Once these compensatory buffers are exhausted, intracranial compliance drops to zero, and small additional volume increments cause steep exponential spikes in intracranial pressure (ICP). This is standard teaching in neurology, neurosurgery and neurocritical care texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Cerebral perfusion and herniation syndromes. Cerebral perfusion pressure equals mean arterial pressure minus intracranial pressure. As ICP climbs, perfusion pressure collapses, precipitating cerebral ischaemia. Pressure gradients between compartments drive brain herniation: subfalcine under the falx cerebri, transtentorial uncal herniation compressing the midbrain and third cranial nerve (causing pupillary dilatation), and tonsillar herniation through the foramen magnum compressing the medulla oblongata. Medullary ischaemia triggers the Cushing response: severe hypertension, reflex bradycardia and respiratory depression.
  • Treatment mechanisms shown. Head elevation promotes jugular venous drainage without lowering arterial pressure; osmotic agents draw free water from brain parenchyma across an intact blood-brain barrier into the circulation; an external ventricular drain directly vents CSF; and decompressive craniectomy removes the rigid bone boundary to eliminate spatial constraint and relieve brainstem compression.
