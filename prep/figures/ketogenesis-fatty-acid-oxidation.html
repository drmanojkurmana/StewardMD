What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How fat becomes ketones. When insulin is low and energy is needed, hormone-sensitive lipase in adipose tissue breaks stored triglyceride into free fatty acids and glycerol. Fatty acids travel in blood (bound to albumin) to the liver, are activated to fatty acyl-CoA in the cytosol, and enter mitochondria through the carnitine shuttle (CPT1 on the outer membrane, a translocase, and CPT2 inside), a step held back in the fed state by malonyl-CoA. Inside, beta-oxidation repeatedly shortens the chain, releasing acetyl-CoA. When oxaloacetate is scarce because it is being used to make glucose, acetyl-CoA cannot all enter the energy cycle (citric acid cycle) and is instead joined in liver mitochondria through HMG-CoA to make the ketone bodies acetoacetate and beta-hydroxybutyrate. These leave the liver, travel in blood, and are used as fuel by brain, heart and muscle, which convert them back to acetyl-CoA. In prolonged fasting this spares glucose and muscle protein. This is standard teaching in biochemistry and medicine texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Defects and excess. In diabetic ketoacidosis, absent insulin drives uncontrolled lipolysis and ketone production, and the ketone acids cause metabolic acidosis. By contrast, defects in the carnitine shuttle or in beta-oxidation enzymes such as medium-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase (MCAD) block fat oxidation: during fasting, fatty acids are released but ketones stay inappropriately low, glucose falls, and energy fails, which is dangerous in infants and children. Glucose given by mouth or vein suppresses lipolysis and provides fuel that does not need fat oxidation; carnitine can help when the problem is carnitine shortage or transport. These are teaching toggles only.
