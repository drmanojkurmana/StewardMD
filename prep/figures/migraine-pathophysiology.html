What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How a migraine attack unfolds. Migraine is a primary headache disorder, often episodic and sometimes familial. Many attacks have a premonitory or prodrome phase with yawning, food craving, mood change and neck stiffness, linked to the hypothalamus and to brainstem nuclei including the dorsal raphe nucleus and the locus ceruleus, with dopamine and other transmitters involved. A typical aura is thought to reflect cortical spreading depression, a slow wave of neuronal and glial depolarisation followed by suppression, which explains gradually spreading visual, sensory or speech symptoms. Familial hemiplegic migraine shows that neuronal ion-channel dysfunction can lower the threshold for this wave. This is standard neurological teaching and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Trigeminovascular activation and headache. The wave and brainstem activity activate the trigeminovascular system. Signals pass through the trigeminal nucleus caudalis in the brainstem and the trigeminal ganglion to meningeal vessels. Nerve endings release calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) and other peptides. CGRP acts at its receptor on the vessel and nerve, the meningeal vessel dilates, plasma proteins leak, and neurogenic inflammation sensitises the endings. Pain signals then relay through the thalamus to cortex. Sensitisation gives throbbing pain that worsens with movement, plus photophobia, phonophobia, nausea and vomiting. Vertigo, sensory change and brainstem symptoms can occur in some subtypes. This figure separates migraine from cluster headache, tension-type headache and paroxysmal headache disorders only by mechanism; it does not diagnose probable migraine or any other headache in a person.
  • Treatment ideas shown. A triptan acts at serotonin receptors to constrict the meningeal vessel and reduce CGRP release. An NSAID reduces prostaglandin-driven inflammation and sensitisation. A CGRP-based treatment blocks CGRP or its receptor. Preventive treatment, used regularly, lowers neuronal excitability and how often attacks start, rather than stopping one attack at once. What is effective varies between people. These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. Aura without headache, chronic migraine, hemiplegic and brainstem subtypes in detail, central sensitisation and allodynia, the full transmitter list, genetics beyond the familial point, triggers, differential diagnosis against cluster, tension-type and paroxysmal disorders, secondary headache causes, and all doses and drug choices are left out.
