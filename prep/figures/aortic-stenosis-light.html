What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How aortic stenosis develops. The aortic valve narrows, most often from calcific degeneration of a three-leaflet valve or from a congenital bicuspid valve that wears early. The left ventricle must generate much higher pressure to eject through the small opening, so a pressure gradient forms across the valve. The wall thickens in parallel (concentric hypertrophy), which keeps wall stress down at first but makes the chamber stiff and hungry for oxygen. Output is maintained at rest for years, but it cannot rise on effort. This is standard teaching in medicine and cardiology texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Symptoms and treatment ideas shown. Angina (supply cannot meet the thick wall's demand, even with open coronary arteries), syncope on effort (output cannot rise while muscle vessels dilate), and breathlessness (a stiff ventricle raises filling pressures). Only replacing the valve (surgical or transcatheter) relieves the obstruction; no drug opens the valve. A vasodilator can be harmful here: peripheral resistance falls but the fixed valve cannot let output rise to compensate, so blood pressure and coronary perfusion fall. These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. Coronary artery disease as a separate cause of angina, low-flow states, grading by measured valve area or velocity, bicuspid aortopathy, endocarditis, surgical versus transcatheter choice, anticoagulation, and all doses and targets are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
