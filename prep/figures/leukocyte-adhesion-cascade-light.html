What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How leukocytes leave the blood. At an inflamed site the vessel lining (endothelium) is activated and displays selectins, which catch passing leukocytes and make them roll slowly along the wall. Chemokines shown on the wall switch on leukocyte integrins. Those integrins bind ICAM on the endothelium and hold the cell firmly. The cell then squeezes between endothelial cells (transmigration, or diapedesis) and follows chemokine signals through the tissue (chemotaxis) to the site. This is standard teaching in immunology and pathology texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • What the choices show. An anti-integrin blocker leaves rolling largely intact but prevents firm sticking, so cells roll on and are carried away. An adhesion deficiency defect (as in leukocyte adhesion deficiency) weakens rolling and firm adhesion, so leukocytes stay in the blood and few reach the tissue even though the signals are present. These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. The separate selectin, integrin and ICAM family members, inside-out integrin signalling in detail, junction molecules used during crossing, other leukocyte types, and all drug names, doses and clinical management are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
