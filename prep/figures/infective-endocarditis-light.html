What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How endocarditis develops. A valve leaflet that is already damaged or abnormal loses its smooth lining, and platelets and fibrin settle on the raw surface. Bacteria circulating in the blood (bacteraemia, for example from teeth, skin, a line or injecting) stick to that deposit and multiply inside it, forming a friable mass called a vegetation. Acute disease, classically with Staphylococcus aureus, destroys the valve fast, perforates or tears a leaflet and causes severe regurgitation and heart failure. Subacute disease, classically with viridans streptococci on a previously abnormal valve, runs slowly over weeks, so immune phenomena have time to appear. This is standard teaching in medicine and cardiology texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Emboli and immune complexes. Fragments of vegetation break off into the circulation and lodge downstream: in the brain (stroke, abscess), the kidney (infarct, blood in the urine) and the skin and eyes (small haemorrhagic spots and lesions), and in the spleen and elsewhere. Separately, prolonged bacteraemia drives circulating immune complexes that deposit in small vessels and glomeruli, causing vasculitic skin lesions and glomerulonephritis. Which pattern dominates depends on how fast and how long the infection runs, which is what the slider shows.
  • Treatment ideas shown. A prolonged course of an antibiotic that kills the bacteria inside the vegetation, and valve surgery to remove infected tissue and repair or replace a destroyed, leaking valve. Antibiotics alone cannot fix a mechanically destroyed valve, and surgery alone cannot sterilise the blood, so severe disease often needs both. These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. Which valve and which organism in detail, prosthetic valve and device infection, culture negative disease, blood cultures and echocardiography, the full Duke criteria, abscess and heart block, every embolic site, antibiotic choice and duration, surgical indications and timing, and every dose are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
