What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How osteomyelitis develops in a child. Bacteria carried in the blood (haematogenous seeding) lodge in the slow looping vessels of the metaphysis, the growing end of a long bone. Staphylococcus aureus is the usual organism; streptococci and other pyogenic bacteria also occur, and in sickle cell disease Salmonella is a classic alternative. Pus forms under pressure inside rigid bone, pressure cuts off the local blood supply, and bone dies. Dead bone is a sequestrum, new bone laid down under a lifted and reactive periosteum is an involucrum (periosteal reaction), and a chronic sinus may drain pus to skin. Fever, pain and loss of use are typical acute signs. This is standard teaching in medicine, paediatric and orthopaedic texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Ideas the slider compresses. Acute disease is mainly pus, pressure and systemic signs. Subacute disease is more contained and may form a local cavity. Chronic disease is defined by dead bone, involucrum and a draining sinus, so antibiotics alone penetrate poorly and healing is slow. Vertebral and spinal disease, implant-associated infection, infection spreading from endocarditis, and disease in a joint or with neurologic signs follow related principles but different anatomy, so they are mentioned here rather than drawn. Where cultures are negative or the course is atypical, biopsy for cultures and histology should be considered, and the antibiotic spectrum, an alternative diagnosis and complications should be reconsidered with specialists. This is a teaching summary only, without any numbers.
  • Treatment ideas shown. A prolonged antibiotic course, surgical debridement to drain pus and remove dead bone, and immobilisation to rest and protect the bone. Therapy usually needs weeks rather than days and often combines approaches. These are teaching toggles only.
