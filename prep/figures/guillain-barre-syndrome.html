What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How Guillain-Barre syndrome develops. Guillain-Barre syndrome is an acute immune polyradiculoneuropathy and one of the acute flaccid neuropathies. It usually follows an infection. Molecular mimicry is central: microbial sugars can resemble gangliosides on peripheral nerve axons and myelin, so antibodies made against the microbe cross react with nerve. Antibody binding at nodes of Ranvier, paranodal proteins such as NF155 (neurofascin 155), myelin and axolemma activates complement. Complement and antibody recruit a macrophage that strips myelin in the demyelinating form, giving a demyelinating neuropathy with slowed conduction and conduction block. In the acute motor axonal neuropathy form (AMAN) and the acute motor and sensory axonal neuropathy form (AMSAN), attack at nodes and axons causes axonal degeneration, which recovers slowly. Early disease can look mild, then weakness ascends from the legs, reflexes are lost (areflexia), and sensory symptoms, autonomic instability and, when severe, diaphragmatic weakness with respiratory failure can follow. This is standard teaching in neurology texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Spinal fluid and nerve tests. Cerebrospinal fluid classically shows albuminocytological dissociation: raised protein with little or no pleocytosis, meaning few white cells, though protein can be normal early. Nerve conduction studies can show demyelinating slowing and block, or reduced motor and sensory amplitudes when axonal loss dominates. The level of protein and the conduction pattern help classify the neuropathy but do not by themselves set treatment.
  • Treatment ideas shown. IVIG is pooled polyclonal immunoglobulin (not a monoclonal antibody); it can neutralise pathogenic antibodies, block Fc receptors and damp complement. Plasma exchange physically removes circulating antibodies and complement factors. Given early, either can shorten the plateau and hasten recovery; they are not usually combined in this teaching figure. Ventilatory support does not change the immune attack, myelin loss or axonal degeneration: it supports breathing and buys time while the nerve repairs, by remyelination in demyelinating disease and by slower axonal regrowth after axonal injury.
