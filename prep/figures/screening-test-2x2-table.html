What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How the table is built. Every tested person sits in one cell by two facts: whether the disease is truly present or absent, and whether the test reads positive or negative. True positives and false negatives fill the disease present row; false positives and true negatives fill the disease absent row. Sensitivity is the share of the disease present row that tests positive. Specificity is the share of the disease absent row that tests negative. This is standard teaching in clinical epidemiology and evidence based medicine texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Why prevalence and the cut-off matter. A positive predictive value asks, of everyone who tests positive, how many truly have the disease, so it mixes both rows and rises as the disease becomes more common, even for the same test. A negative predictive value behaves in the opposite direction. Test results in the two groups overlap, so moving the cut-off trades one error for another: a lower cut-off catches more disease and creates more false alarms, a raised cut-off does the reverse, and a second confirmatory test applied to positive results removes many false positives at a small cost to sensitivity. These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. The two distributions are drawn as smooth overlapping bells with a single cut-off, real tests can be skewed, multi-level or combined in panels, and a confirmatory test is rarely fully independent of the first. Spectrum effects, verification bias, indeterminate results, costs and harms of testing, and all numeric thresholds are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any person should be tested or treated.
