What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How chronic inflammation develops. Chronic inflammation follows a trigger that persists or a response that fails to resolve. Neutrophils give way to macrophages and lymphocytes. Th1 cells release interferon gamma and Th17 cells release interleukin 17, while activated macrophages release tumour necrosis factor. Together these cytokines keep macrophages activated, so enzymes and reactive products damage tissue. New blood vessels grow in (angiogenesis) and fibroblasts lay down collagen, giving fibrosis. When macrophages cannot clear the trigger they become epithelioid cells and multinucleated giant cells and wall it off as a granuloma, with a cuff of lymphocytes around it. This is standard textbook teaching in pathology and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Treatment ideas shown. Removing the trigger lets the infiltrate and cytokines settle, though laid down fibrosis fades only partly. An anti-inflammatory broadly lowers cytokines, infiltration and damage while the trigger may persist. An anti-cytokine biologic strongly lowers cytokine signalling and macrophage activation, so damage falls while cells and trigger may remain. These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. The many causes of granuloma, macrophage polarisation states, individual cytokine pathways, scarring in specific organs, infection specific patterns, and all doses and targets are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
