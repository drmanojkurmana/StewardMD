What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • CSF circulation. Cerebrospinal fluid is made mainly by the choroid plexus in the ventricles. It flows from the lateral ventricles to the third ventricle, through the cerebral aqueduct to the fourth ventricle, then out into the subarachnoid space around the brain and spinal cord, and is absorbed through arachnoid villi into the venous sinuses. This is standard teaching in neuroanatomy and neurology texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Obstructive and communicating patterns. In obstructive (non-communicating) hydrocephalus, flow is blocked inside the ventricular system, classically at the narrow cerebral aqueduct, so the ventricles before the block enlarge and pressure rises. In communicating hydrocephalus, CSF reaches the subarachnoid space but absorption fails, for example after bleeding or meningitis, so all the ventricles enlarge. Normal pressure hydrocephalus is a chronic communicating pattern in older adults with the triad of gait disturbance, cognitive slowing and urinary urgency, often with near normal CSF pressure.
  • Treatment ideas shown. A shunt drains CSF from a ventricle, usually to the peritoneum. Endoscopic third ventriculostomy makes a hole in the floor of the third ventricle to bypass an aqueduct block, so it suits obstructive rather than communicating patterns. Treating the cause acts on the block or on the failed absorption itself. These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. The spinal CSF circulation in detail, CSF production outside the choroid plexus, the openings of the fourth ventricle by name, causes such as tumours and congenital aqueduct stenosis by name, infant signs such as an enlarging head, shunt complications and valve settings, and all pressures, volumes and doses are left out.
