What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How a stone forms. Urine carries calcium, oxalate, uric acid, cystine and other solutes. When urine is concentrated, or solute load is high, it becomes supersaturated and crystals can nucleate, often on a small surface deposit in the renal pelvis, then grow and stick together into a stone. Calcium oxalate is the commonest type; uric acid stones favour acidic urine; struvite stones grow with urease splitting infection and can form a staghorn; cystine stones are an uncommon inherited type. A stone still in the kidney may be silent. Once it enters the ureter, the ureter squeezes against it and urine is held back: that gives the severe, waving loin to groin pain of ureteric colic, obstruction upstream, swelling of the pelvis and calyces (hydronephrosis), and a raised risk of infection in the held back urine, which can be dangerous. This is standard teaching in urology and renal texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Treatment ideas shown. Fluids dilute the urine and analgesia eases colic pain, which may let a small stone pass. Lithotripsy focuses shock waves to fragment a stone into pieces that can pass. Ureteroscopy passes a fine scope up the ureter to see, fragment and remove the stone and relieve the blockage. These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. Metabolic work up, stone prevention diets and medicines, pregnancy, single kidney and transplant kidneys, emergency drainage with a stent or nephrostomy for infected obstruction, open surgery, and all doses and size cut offs are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
