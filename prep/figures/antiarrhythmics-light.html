What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • The classification. The Vaughan Williams scheme sorts antiarrhythmics by their main mechanism: class I sodium channel blockers (Ia moderate and prolonging repolarisation, Ib weak and shortening it, Ic strong with little effect on duration), class II beta-blockers, class III potassium channel blockers that prolong the action potential, and class IV non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers; several drugs have properties of more than one class (Antiarrhythmic medications, StatPearls).
  • Use dependence. Class I drugs block more at fast rates, because they bind sodium channels in the open and inactivated states; lidocaine type drugs act mostly in depolarised tissue such as ischaemic myocardium. This is standard teaching in the pharmacology texts the PrepNucleus lessons cite (Katzung; KD Tripathi) and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Shapes of the curves. The phases, channels and effects follow standard cardiac electrophysiology teaching; the curves are idealised drawings, not recordings.

What was simplified. Real curves differ by tissue (atrium, ventricle, Purkinje, endocardium and epicardium) and species; the effects on the ECG (PR, QRS, QT) are described only in words in the explanations, not drawn; amiodarone and sotalol have effects of several classes (amiodarone also blocks sodium and calcium channels and beta receptors); class Ia drugs also have anticholinergic effects; adenosine, digoxin, magnesium and ivabradine are not drawn; pro-arrhythmia (torsades with prolonged repolarisation, ventricular tachycardia with class Ic in structural heart disease) is not drawn. Use dependence is shown by a simple rate factor. Levels and the size of each effect are illustrative. The drug choices are teaching toggles of a class: no named drug, dose or schedule is shown or implied, and the figure does not say who should or should not take any of them.
