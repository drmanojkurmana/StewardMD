What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How acute inflammation develops. Injury or infection creates danger and microbial signals. Resident mast cells release histamine and macrophages release cytokines, while prostaglandin is made locally. Histamine, prostaglandin and cytokines widen the vessel (vasodilation) and open gaps in its wall (increased permeability). Protein rich fluid leaves as exudate and collects as oedema. Neutrophils slow and stick at the wall (margination), squeeze out (emigration), follow chemical signals and phagocytose microbes and debris. The cardinal signs are redness, heat, swelling, pain and loss of function. In resolution neutrophils die and macrophages clear them; if the trigger persists the response can become chronic with more macrophages and lymphocytes. This is standard textbook teaching in pathology and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Treatment ideas shown. An NSAID blocks prostaglandin making, so widening, leak and pain signals fall while histamine, cytokines and phagocytosis are largely kept. A steroid broadly suppresses mediator making, neutrophil emigration and phagocytosis, so signs fall but clearing is slower and a trigger can persist. These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. Complement, clotting and kinin pathways, selectins and integrins by name, fever and systemic responses, chronic granuloma and fibrosis detail, and all doses and targets are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
