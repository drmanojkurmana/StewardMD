What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How cord lesions are localised. A spinal cord lesion is placed by its level and by which tracts it interrupts. Ascending dorsal columns carry proprioception, vibration and fine touch from a sensory nerve on the same side up through cord segments, crossing only in the medulla, so a cord lesion usually loses these senses ipsilaterally below the lesion. Spinothalamic fibres from pain and temperature nerves cross in the anterior white commissure within a segment or two of entry and then ascend contralaterally, so pain and temperature loss is usually on the opposite side and starts a little below the level. Descending corticospinal fibres mostly cross at the medullary pyramids and run in the lateral cord, so a cord lesion gives upper motor neuron signs: weakness with spasticity, brisk reflexes and an upgoing plantar response below the lesion, with lower motor neuron signs only at the level where anterior horn cells or the nerve root are damaged. Vertebral bodies and the cord segment do not align exactly, and cervical segments sit higher than the same numbered vertebral bodies. This is standard teaching in clinical neurology texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • The patterns shown. Complete transection interrupts every tract, with paralysis and loss of sensory and autonomic function below the level, first flaccid in spinal shock and later spastic. Brown-Sequard hemisection gives ipsilateral motor and proprioception loss with contralateral pain and temperature loss. Anterior cord syndrome from anterior spinal artery or compression damage spares the dorsal columns while motor and pain and temperature are lost below. Central cord injury and syringomyelia, usually idiopathic or linked to a Chiari malformation, expand around the central canal, interrupt crossing pain and temperature fibres first and give a cape like suspended sensory loss over the shoulders and arms, with upper limbs often weaker than lower limbs. Extrinsic compression from vertebral, disc, tumour, abscess or inflammation can mimic these patterns and is a surgical urgency. This is standard teaching and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Treatment ideas shown. Decompression relieves extrinsic compression of the cord. A steroid concept stands for calming secondary inflammation and swelling after acute injury, shown only as a concept with no agent or dose implied. Rehabilitation trains spared pathways, strength, transfers and bladder and bowel routines and prevents complications. These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. Exact dermatomes and myotomes, bladder, bowel and sexual dysfunction in detail, spinal shock timing, autonomic dysreflexia, every cause of myelopathy including metabolic and infectious causes, imaging, surgical indications and all drug doses are left out. The lesion level is shown as cervical for illustration. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
