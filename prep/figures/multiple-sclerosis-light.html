What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How multiple sclerosis injures the brain. For reasons not fully understood, autoreactive T cells and B cells that recognise myelin antigens are activated outside the brain. They cross the blood-brain barrier, whose endothelial lining normally limits entry to the brain and spinal cord. Inside, T cells, B cells, antibodies, microglia and macrophages attack the myelin sheath made by oligodendrocytes. The stripped axon segment forms a plaque: saltatory conduction fails there, so the impulse slows or blocks and symptoms appear. This is standard teaching in neurology and pathology texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Relapse, remission and progression. A relapse is a burst of new inflammation and demyelination. In remission inflammation settles and some thin remyelination can occur, so conduction partly recovers. Over time axons themselves are lost, repair fails and disability builds even between relapses: progression. The course slider compresses that long story into three stages.
  • Treatment ideas shown. A steroid for relapse calms the acute inflammation and barrier leak quickly, so a relapse settles faster, but it does not repair myelin or prevent later damage. Disease-modifying therapy lowers the autoreactive attack and the rate of new plaques over time, so it protects conduction in the long run, but it does not reverse fixed myelin or axon loss. These are teaching toggles only and name no individual drug or dose.

What was simplified. Genetic and environmental triggers, Epstein-Barr virus and vitamin D ideas, individual lesion sites and symptoms, optic nerve and spinal cord lesions, imaging and spinal fluid tests, individual disease-modifying drugs and their risks, and all doses and targets are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
