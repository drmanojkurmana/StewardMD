What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How the baroreceptor reflex works. Stretch receptors in the carotid sinus and aortic arch sense arterial pressure. Afferent signals travel in the glossopharyngeal nerve from the carotid sinus and in the vagus nerve from the aortic arch to the medulla, where the vasomotor centre sets sympathetic and parasympathetic outflow. A fall in pressure reduces sensor firing, so sympathetic outflow to the heart and arterioles rises and vagus outflow to the heart falls. Heart rate and force rise and vessels tighten, restoring pressure. A rise in pressure does the opposite. This negative feedback is standard teaching in physiology and medicine texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Standing, haemorrhage and treatment ideas shown. Standing up lets blood pool in the leg veins, so less blood returns to the heart and pressure tends to fall until the reflex compensates. Haemorrhage loses circulating volume, giving a larger and more sustained fall. A vasoconstrictor tightens arterioles and fluids replace volume. These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. Slower control by the kidney, hormones such as the renin-angiotensin system and antidiuretic hormone, chemoreceptor input, individual variation, fainting when compensation fails, and all doses and targets are left out. The response is shown at one settled moment rather than second by second, and counts and speeds are illustrative. The figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
