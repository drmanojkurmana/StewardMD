What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How skeletal muscle contracts. An action potential in a motor neuron opens calcium channels in the nerve terminal, acetylcholine vesicles fuse and acetylcholine crosses the neuromuscular junction to nicotinic receptors. The end-plate potential depolarises the sarcolemma and the signal travels down T tubules, opening calcium release channels in the sarcoplasmic reticulum. Calcium binds troponin, tropomyosin shifts to expose actin binding sites, and myosin heads form cross-bridges and cycle using ATP. When the signal stops, calcium is pumped back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum and the fibre relaxes. In rigor there is no ATP, so cross-bridges cannot detach. This is standard teaching in physiology texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Treatment ideas shown. A neuromuscular blocker occupies the acetylcholine receptors so the sarcolemma is not depolarised even though acetylcholine is released. A cholinesterase inhibitor slows acetylcholine breakdown in the cleft, so the signal is stronger and longer. These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. Voltage-gated channel subtypes, quantal release, end-plate folding detail, calcium buffering, the full cross-bridge kinetic cycle, motor unit recruitment, fibre types, and all drug names, doses and clinical uses are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
