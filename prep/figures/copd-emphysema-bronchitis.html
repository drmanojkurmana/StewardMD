What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How COPD develops. Long term inhaled smoke and other irritants inflame the small airways and lung tissue. Chronic bronchitis is the pattern of a thickened, inflamed airway wall with more and larger goblet cells and submucosal glands making too much mucus, so the airway narrows and cough brings up sputum. Emphysema is the pattern of destroyed walls between air sacs (alveoli): sacs merge into larger, floppy spaces, elastic recoil and the tethering that holds small airways open are lost, and air is trapped when breathing out. Both patterns often occur together and both limit airflow, most clearly on breathing out. A flare up, called an exacerbation and often set off by infection, makes inflammation, mucus and narrowing suddenly worse. This is standard teaching in respiratory medicine and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Treatment ideas shown. A bronchodilator relaxes airway smooth muscle and eases airflow without undoing wall damage. An inhaled steroid calms airway inflammation and mucus and lowers flare up risk in selected patterns, but does not rebuild alveolar walls. Oxygen supports blood oxygen when it is low but does not open airways or repair sacs. Stopping smoking removes the ongoing injury and slows further damage, though destroyed alveolar walls do not regrow in this schematic. These are teaching toggles only.
