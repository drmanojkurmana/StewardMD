What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How pulmonary hypertension develops. Pressure in the pulmonary artery rises when the small pulmonary vessels narrow and stiffen. Endothelial dysfunction tips the balance toward vasoconstriction and growth of the vascular muscle layer, so the wall remodels and resistance climbs. The same pressure rise follows left heart disease (pressure passed back from the left side), lung disease with low oxygen (hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction), and chronic thromboembolic disease (old clot that never cleared). This is standard teaching in medicine and cardiology texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • What the pressure does, and treatment ideas shown. The right ventricle faces a pressure overload: its wall hypertrophies to cope, then the chamber dilates, cardiac output can no longer rise on exercise, and right heart failure follows with raised jugular venous pressure, oedema and renal congestion from venous back pressure and low output. Oxygen relieves hypoxic vasoconstriction and a diuretic unloads congestion; a pulmonary vasodilator concept lowers vascular resistance (with a systemic pressure cost if it is not selective); treating the cause (the left heart, the lung disease, or the clot) helps most before remodelling is fixed. These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. Classification into groups, measured pressures and resistance values, right heart catheter assessment, vasoreactivity testing, specific pulmonary vasodilator drug classes, anticoagulation and surgical choices, transplant, and all doses and targets are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
