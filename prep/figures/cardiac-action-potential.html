What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How the cardiac action potential works. In a ventricular myocyte, opening of fast voltage-gated sodium channels drives the rapid phase 0 upstroke. Brief potassium efflux gives the phase 1 notch. In phase 2, calcium entry through L-type calcium channels balances potassium efflux and holds the plateau that triggers contraction. Phase 3 follows when calcium channels close and potassium efflux repolarises the cell to the phase 4 resting potential, held by inward-rectifier potassium channels. Pacemaker cells in the sinoatrial node have no stable resting potential: the funny current, then calcium currents, give a slow phase 4 rise to threshold and a slower, calcium-driven upstroke. The surface ECG sums these events: the QRS reflects ventricular depolarisation and the T wave ventricular repolarisation, so a longer action potential gives a longer QT. High extracellular potassium makes the resting potential less negative, inactivates sodium channels and speeds repolarisation. Low potassium reduces potassium conductance, prolongs repolarisation and flattens the T wave. This is standard teaching in physiology and cardiology texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Treatment ideas shown. A sodium channel blocker slows the phase 0 upstroke. A potassium channel blocker slows phase 3 and prolongs the action potential and QT. A calcium channel blocker shortens the plateau and slows the pacemaker rise. These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. Single-cell currents, channel subunit detail, autonomic effects, conduction through the AV node and His-Purkinje system, other potassium and calcium currents, arrhythmia mechanisms in detail, drug classes and their cautions, and all doses, voltages and intervals are left out.
