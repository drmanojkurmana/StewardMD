What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • Binding and response. A drug binds reversibly to a receptor. More concentration means more receptors occupied (binding, or occupancy) until binding saturates. The response also rises and then levels at a ceiling (the maximum effect, called efficacy). The concentration position of the curve is potency: a curve further left needs less drug for the same effect. Response can reach its ceiling before every receptor is bound, because the cell amplifies the signal: these unneeded receptors are called spare receptors. This is standard teaching in pharmacology texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Agonists and antagonists shown. A full agonist binds and fully switches the receptor on. A partial agonist binds but only partly switches it on, so its ceiling is lower however much is given. A competitive antagonist binds the same site reversibly without switching the receptor on: more agonist can out-compete it, so the curve shifts right with the same ceiling (surmountable). A non-competitive antagonist binds a separate site or disables the receptor, so the ceiling itself falls and more agonist cannot restore it. An inverse agonist (explained in the figure, not a selectable drug) binds and lowers the resting activity that some receptors show on their own. These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. Binding curves measured separately from responses, receptor subtypes and signalling pathways, desensitisation and tolerance, allosteric modulators that only change the agonist effect, irreversible antagonists in detail, and every named drug and dose are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
