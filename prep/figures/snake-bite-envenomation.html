What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How snake venom acts. Elapid venoms rich in neurotoxins block the neuromuscular junction, at the nerve ending or at the acetylcholine receptor, so signals fail and weakness descends from the eyes and bulbar muscles to the breathing muscles. Viper venoms rich in procoagulant enzymes switch on clotting, fibrin forms and factors and platelets are consumed, so blood can then fail to clot and bleeding follows. Many venoms also injure tissue at the bite, causing swelling, blistering and necrosis. This is standard teaching in toxinology and emergency medicine and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Treatment ideas shown. Immobilising the limb and reassuring the patient slows venom spread. Anti-snake venom neutralises circulating venom but does not undo established tissue necrosis or all established paralysis. Ventilation supports breathing while paralysis recovers, and blood products replace consumed clotting factors and platelets. These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. Venom variation between species, dry bites, first aid pressure bandaging detail, wound care and surgery, kidney and muscle breakdown complications, repeat dosing and product choice, and all doses and targets are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
