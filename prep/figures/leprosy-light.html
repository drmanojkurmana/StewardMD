What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • The organism and the spectrum. Leprosy (Hansen disease) is caused by Mycobacterium leprae, a slow growing bacillus that favours cooler skin and peripheral nerves and invades Schwann cells. Where a person sits on the spectrum is set by the cell mediated immune response. Tuberculoid disease: a strong Th1 response with interferon gamma, a well formed granuloma, very few bacilli, a single well defined skin patch with loss of feeling, and early nerve damage from the immune attack itself. Lepromatous disease: a weak cell mediated response, foamy macrophages packed with bacilli, many widespread skin lesions, and later widespread nerve involvement. Borderline disease sits between the two and is unstable. This is standard teaching in infectious disease and pathology texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Reactions and nerve damage. Reactions are sudden immune flares that can injure nerves quickly and cause deformity if untreated. A type 1 (reversal) reaction is a rising Th1 response, mostly in borderline disease, with inflamed patches and painful swollen nerves. Erythema nodosum leprosum is an immune complex flare in lepromatous disease with tender skin nodules and fever. Loss of feeling and weakness lead to unnoticed injury, claw hand and other deformity. This summary is flagged for review.
  • Treatment ideas shown. Multidrug therapy kills the bacilli and lowers the load in skin and nerve; it does not by itself calm a reaction. A steroid calms the reaction flare and protects the nerve during a reaction, but does not kill bacilli. They are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. The full Ridley and Jopling classification, borderline subtypes, pure neuritic disease, eye and nasal involvement, the details of each reaction, drug resistance, rehabilitation and prevention, and all drug doses and durations are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
