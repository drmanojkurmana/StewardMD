What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • V/Q mismatch. Ventilation (V) is air reaching the alveoli and perfusion (Q) is blood reaching their capillaries. A shunt is perfusion without ventilation (collapse, fluid, mucus plugging): the blood stays unoxygenated and adding oxygen helps little. Dead space is ventilation without perfusion (for example pulmonary embolism): air is wasted and carbon dioxide is not cleared unless total breathing rises. Units with low but not absent ventilation behave partly like a shunt and do respond to oxygen. This is standard respiratory physiology teaching and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Blood gas and acid-base. Low oxygen drives faster breathing, which blows off carbon dioxide and can give respiratory alkalosis. When breathing fails or tires, carbon dioxide rises and pH falls (respiratory acidosis). Over days the kidney retains bicarbonate in chronic acidosis, or loses it in chronic alkalosis, which brings pH back toward normal (metabolic compensation). Oxygen can reduce hypoxic drive and worsen carbon dioxide retention in some patients, so it is titrated to a target. Ventilation support sets minute ventilation and can overshoot into alkalosis. The figure shows the direction of each effect, not how much to give.

What was simplified. Many things are left out: the oxygen-haemoglobin curve, the Haldane effect, diffusion limitation, hypoventilation as a separate cause of hypoxaemia, the alveolar-arterial gradient, mixed disorders, metabolic acid-base causes, anion gap, lactate, PEEP and lung-protective settings, noninvasive versus invasive ventilation, and all doses, targets and numbers. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
