What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How aqueous humour flows. The ciliary body makes aqueous humour into the posterior chamber. It flows forward through the pupil into the anterior chamber, then drains at the iridocorneal angle through the trabecular meshwork into the canal of Schlemm and episcleral veins. A smaller share leaves by the uveoscleral route. This is standard teaching in ophthalmology texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Open and closed angle glaucoma. In open angle glaucoma the angle looks open but trabecular drainage works poorly, so pressure rises slowly and the optic disc cups with gradual visual field loss. In closed angle glaucoma the iris blocks the angle; in acute closure the iris bows forward, the chamber shallows, drainage stops and pressure rises fast with pain and blurred vision. Raised pressure damages retinal ganglion cell axons at the optic disc, enlarging the cup. This is standard teaching and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Treatment ideas shown. Drops that reduce aqueous production, drops that increase trabecular or uveoscleral outflow, and laser peripheral iridotomy, which makes a small hole in the peripheral iris so aqueous can bypass a blocked pupil and the iris falls back. These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. Uveoscleral drainage detail, episcleral venous pressure, diurnal pressure variation, visual field patterns, retinal nerve fibre layer imaging, normal pressure glaucoma, secondary causes, surgery such as trabeculectomy, and all doses and pressure targets are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
