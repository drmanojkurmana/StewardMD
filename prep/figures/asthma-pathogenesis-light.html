What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How asthma develops. In allergic asthma an inhaled allergen is taken up by dendritic cells in the airway lining and presented to helper T cells, which take a Th2 pattern. Th2 cytokines help B cells switch to making IgE (IL-4), call in and sustain eosinophils (IL-5), and drive goblet cell mucus and twitchy smooth muscle (IL-13). IgE sits on mast cells; when allergen cross-links it the mast cell degranulates, releasing histamine and other mediators that tighten smooth muscle, swell the wall and increase mucus. Eosinophil granule proteins injure the lining and keep inflammation going. Repeated inflammation remodels the wall, with a thicker basement membrane, more and larger smooth muscle and enlarged submucosal glands, so airflow limitation becomes less fully reversible. Airway hyperresponsiveness means the muscle tightens too easily and too much to triggers such as cold air, exercise, smoke and viral infection. This is standard teaching in respiratory medicine and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Treatment ideas shown. A reliever bronchodilator relaxes smooth muscle and opens the airway quickly but does not treat the inflammation underneath. An inhaled corticosteroid switches off many inflammatory genes across lining, immune and mast cells, reducing eosinophils, mucus and hyperresponsiveness over time and slowing remodelling. Used together they give quick relief plus control of the cause. An anti-IgE biologic mops up IgE so mast cells are less well armed, which suits allergic disease. These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. Non-allergic and neutrophilic asthma, aspirin-exacerbated disease, the late phase reaction in detail, neural control, small airway disease, infection and pollution triggers in detail, biomarker selection for biologics, inhaler technique, adherence, side effects, exacerbation management and all doses are left out.
