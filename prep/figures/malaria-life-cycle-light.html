What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • The life cycle. An infected female Anopheles mosquito injects sporozoites. They reach the liver within minutes and enter liver cells. There they multiply as a liver schizont (no symptoms), which bursts and releases merozoites into the blood. In P. vivax and P. ovale some liver forms stay dormant as hypnozoites and can reactivate weeks or months later, causing relapse. In the blood, merozoites invade red cells and pass through ring, trophozoite and schizont stages. The red cell bursts, releasing merozoites that infect new cells, and the pigment and cytokines released cause the periodic fever paroxysm. Some parasites become male and female gametocytes, which a mosquito takes up so the sexual cycle can continue. This is standard teaching in microbiology, medicine and tropical-medicine texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Severe falciparum malaria. Infected red cells stick to the lining of small vessels (cytoadherence and sequestration), including in the brain, and haemolysis causes anaemia. Treatment ideas shown are by class only: blood schizonticides clear the blood stage, primaquine-type drugs clear hypnozoites and gametocytes, and chemoprophylaxis covers the blood stage. These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. Species other than vivax and falciparum, drug resistance, G6PD testing before primaquine-type drugs, pregnancy and child dosing, severe-malaria management, vector control and vaccines, and all doses and schedules are left out. Counts, timing and gauge levels are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
