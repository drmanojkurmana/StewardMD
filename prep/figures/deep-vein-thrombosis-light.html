What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How deep vein thrombosis develops. The Virchow triad groups the influences that favour a venous clot: slow or stagnant blood flow (stasis, for example during immobility), injury to the vein wall lining (endothelial injury), and blood that clots more readily (hypercoagulability). A clot often starts in the pocket around a calf vein valve, where flow is slowest. Platelets and fibrin build it up and it propagates along the vein toward the heart. The blocked vein raises pressure behind the clot, so fluid leaves the vessels and the calf swells and aches. A piece that breaks off is an embolus: it travels with venous blood to the lung and can block lung flow as a pulmonary embolism. This is standard teaching in medicine, haematology and pathology texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Treatment ideas shown. Compression and mobilisation squeeze the calf and speed venous flow, which eases swelling and stasis. An anticoagulant stops the clot growing and stops new clots forming while the body clears it. A filter placed in the large vein to the heart catches travelling pieces, and a clot dissolving treatment breaks clot up; both are reserved for selected situations. These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. Risk factor detail, inherited and acquired clotting disorders, diagnosis by scan and blood tests, clot above the knee versus in the calf, post thrombotic syndrome, bleeding risk, filter placement detail, surgery, prevention in hospital, and all doses and targets are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
