What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • The shared picture. In renal tubular acidosis the glomeruli filter fairly normally, but the tubule cannot keep blood bicarbonate up, so blood becomes acidic while chloride rises to balance it. That is a normal anion gap, hyperchloraemic acidosis. This is standard teaching in medicine and nephrology texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Proximal type 2 and distal type 1. In proximal (type 2) disease the proximal tubule fails to reclaim filtered bicarbonate, so bicarbonate is wasted in the urine and blood bicarbonate falls. In distal (type 1) disease alpha-intercalated cells in the distal tubule and collecting duct fail to secrete hydrogen ions, so the urine cannot be acidified, urine pH stays high, and calcium stones and nephrocalcinosis are favoured, often with low potassium. Flagged for review.
  • Type 4 and treatment ideas shown. In type 4 disease aldosterone is lacking or resisted, so potassium excretion fails and potassium rises, with a milder acidosis because less ammonium is made. Bicarbonate (alkali) replaces what is lost or not regenerated, potassium replaces a low potassium where present, and a mineralocorticoid replaces missing aldosterone action in type 4. These are teaching toggles only, and potassium given when potassium is already high would be harmful in real care.

What was simplified. Causes of each type, Fanconi syndrome features in proximal disease, bone disease, growth effects, ammonium handling in detail, urine anion gap testing, stone composition, and all doses and targets are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
