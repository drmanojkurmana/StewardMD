What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • Superantigen mechanism. Some staphylococcal and streptococcal toxins act as superantigens. Instead of sitting in the antigen groove after processing, they bind the outside of MHC class II on a presenting cell and the T cell receptor at the same time. That cross-links many T cells at once, regardless of their normal antigen specificity, and releases a large burst of cytokines. The result can be fever, rash, low blood pressure and toxic shock. This is standard textbook teaching and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Necrotising fasciitis. Infection spreads rapidly along the fascial plane, aided by bacterial toxins and enzymes. Clues taught include pain that seems out of proportion to what is visible, rapid spread, dusky or dead tissue, and gas in the tissues that can feel like crepitus. The fascia and dead tissue are poorly reached by blood flow, which is why urgent surgical removal of dead tissue (source control) is central in teaching, alongside antibiotics. This is standard textbook teaching and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Treatment ideas shown. Antibiotics kill bacteria, and clindamycin is taught as also switching off new toxin production. Surgery removes the infected and dead source tissue. IVIG is shown only as a concept: pooled antibodies that may bind and neutralise circulating toxin. These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. Which organism, toxin names, antibiotic choices, scoring systems, surgical technique, IVIG evidence detail, intensive care support, and all doses and targets are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
