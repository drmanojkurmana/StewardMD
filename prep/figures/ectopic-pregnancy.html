What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How an ectopic pregnancy develops. A fertilised egg normally travels down the fallopian tube and implants in the uterine cavity. If the tube is damaged, for example after infection or surgery, transport fails and the egg implants in the tube itself, most often in its wider ampullary part; implantation can also occur elsewhere outside the cavity. Trophoblast, the outer layer of the early pregnancy, invades the thin tube wall and its vessels. The tube cannot stretch like the uterus, so it leaks or ruptures, bleeding into the peritoneal cavity (haemoperitoneum) and causing abdominal pain and shock. Typical features are a missed period (amenorrhoea), pain and vaginal bleeding, with hCG rising more slowly than in a uterine pregnancy. This is standard teaching in obstetrics and gynaecology texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Treatment ideas shown. Methotrexate stops trophoblast growing and can resolve an unruptured ectopic pregnancy. Surgery removes the ectopic pregnancy and stops bleeding, and is needed when the tube has ruptured. Expectant care, watching with repeat hCG checks, suits only a small ectopic pregnancy whose hCG is already falling. These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. Implantation sites outside the tube, diagnostic cut-offs and scan criteria, choice between removing the tube or opening it, drug eligibility rules and dosing, fertility after treatment, and care of shock are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
