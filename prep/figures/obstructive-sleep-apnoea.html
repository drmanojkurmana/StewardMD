What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How obstructive sleep apnoea develops. During sleep the neuromuscular tone that keeps the upper airway open falls. If the pharyngeal airway is already crowded, by obesity and fat around the neck, a large tongue, a long soft palate, or inherited airway shape, the negative pressure of breathing in pulls it shut. Episodes of apnoea occur many times a night in adults, more often as severity rises. Each episode lowers ventilation, so oxygen falls and carbon dioxide rises, until arousal briefly restores muscle tone and breathing. Repeated arousals fragment sleep and cause daytime sleepiness. Sympathetic surges with each arousal raise pressure in the chest and circulation and are linked to hypertension, arrhythmia and pulmonary hypertension. Prevalence is higher with obesity and the condition is under recognised, including in women. This is standard respiratory and sleep teaching and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Treatment ideas shown. Weight loss and positional therapy (sleeping on the side, which helps when collapse is worse lying flat), CPAP which uses pressure to splint the airway open, and surgery or a mandibular advancement device which moves the tongue and jaw forward. These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. Central sleep apnoea, detailed sleep staging, airway imaging and endoscopy, exact diagnostic thresholds, device pressures and surgical procedures, and associated conditions beyond the main pathway are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
