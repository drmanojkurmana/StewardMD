What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How this state develops. In type 2 diabetes there is usually enough insulin to suppress fat breakdown and ketone production, so ketones and acidosis are not the main problem, unlike diabetic ketoacidosis. Insulin is still too little, or works too poorly, to control glucose. Very high glucose passes into the urine and drags water and electrolytes with it (osmotic diuresis). Over days this causes profound dehydration and hyperosmolality: the blood becomes concentrated, water is pulled out of cells including brain cells, and alertness falls from drowsiness toward coma. Slow, concentrated blood and immobility raise the risk of clots. Illness, infection, missed treatment or new diabetes are common triggers. This is standard teaching in medicine and endocrine texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Treatment ideas shown. Fluids first to restore circulating volume and slowly lower osmolality, then insulin to lower glucose once fluids are running; replacing electrolytes lost in urine, with monitoring; and preventing clots while the blood is concentrated and flow is slow. Insulin without fluids is shown as incomplete because dehydration and hyperosmolality persist and glucose and water can shift quickly. The figure shows direction only, not how much to give or when.
  • Contrast with diabetic ketoacidosis. Ketoacidosis is driven by near absent insulin, ketone acids and acidosis, often over hours. This state is driven by dehydration and hyperosmolality with ketones low, often over days, usually in type 2 diabetes.

What was simplified. Triggers in detail, sodium correction and safe rates of osmolality change, potassium thresholds, cerebral oedema, insulin regimens, clot prevention choices, and all doses, rates and targets are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
