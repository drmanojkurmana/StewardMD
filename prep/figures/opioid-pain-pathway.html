What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How pain reaches the brain. Tissue injury releases prostaglandins and other mediators that sensitise nociceptors, the free nerve endings that detect harmful stimuli. Their fibres enter the spinal cord through the dorsal root and synapse in the dorsal horn (notably the substantia gelatinosa). Second order neurons cross to the opposite side and ascend in the spinothalamic tract to the thalamus, which relays to the somatosensory cortex where pain is localised and felt. A descending pathway from the periaqueductal grey and rostral ventromedial medulla returns to the dorsal horn and can inhibit the relay, a form of gate control. This is standard teaching in neuroanatomy, physiology and pharmacology texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Where the treatments act. NSAIDs block cyclo-oxygenase, so fewer prostaglandins are made and nociceptors are less sensitised at the injury site. Opioids act mainly at mu opioid receptors: in the dorsal horn they reduce transmitter release from the incoming fibre and damp the second order neuron, and in the brainstem and higher centres they strengthen descending inhibition and reduce the felt intensity of pain. Local anaesthetics block voltage gated sodium channels in the peripheral nerve, so action potentials cannot pass the blocked segment and the signal never reaches the cord. These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. Fast and slow pain fibres, wide dynamic range neurons, peripheral and central sensitisation over time, neuropathic pain, emotion and attention circuits, other opioid receptor types and side effects, adjuvant drugs, and all doses and routes are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
