What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How dengue develops. Dengue virus is carried by Aedes mosquitoes and enters at the bite. It infects and copies itself in immune cells, then circulates in the blood. The early febrile phase has fever and a rising virus level. Around defervescence some patients enter a critical phase: mediators loosen the capillary wall so plasma leaks out of vessels, the remaining blood becomes concentrated (rising haematocrit), platelets fall, and loss of circulating volume can lead to shock. In recovery the leak stops, leaked fluid is reabsorbed, haematocrit settles and platelets rise again. Warning features and bleeding can occur, and platelet support is reserved for bleeding rather than used to chase a count alone. This is standard teaching in medicine, infectious-disease and tropical-medicine texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Treatment ideas shown. Careful fluids with close monitoring to replace leaked plasma and support circulation, and platelet support only if there is bleeding. Fluids do not seal the leak or clear the virus, and platelet support alone does not restore circulating volume. These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. Dengue serotypes and secondary-infection antibody enhancement in detail, warning-sign checklists, bleeding and organ involvement, fluid types and rates, transfusion triggers, pregnancy and comorbidity, and all doses, thresholds and targets are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
