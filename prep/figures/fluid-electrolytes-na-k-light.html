What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • Water sets sodium concentration. Blood sodium concentration reflects water relative to salt, not total salt alone. Extra water without salt (for example from excess intake with poor kidney clearance, or excess vasopressin effect) dilutes sodium and water moves into brain cells, which can swell. Losing more water than salt (for example poor intake with ongoing losses, or diabetes insipidus) concentrates sodium and water leaves brain cells, which shrink. The kidney adjusts by passing more or less water and holding or losing salt. This is standard teaching in medicine and physiology texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Potassium sets the heartbeat. Most body potassium sits inside cells. A low blood level (for example from gut or kidney losses) flattens the ECG trace and adds a small extra wave; a high level (for example kidney failure, or potassium leaving cells in acidosis) first peaks the trace and then widens and slows it, which can stop the heart. Replacement corrects a low level, while shifting potassium into cells and removing it from the body corrects a high level. This is standard teaching in medicine and cardiology texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.

What was simplified. Osmotic demyelination from too fast correction, exact fluid choices and rates, vasopressin and aldosterone control loops, acid base links, calcium and magnesium effects, diuretics, dialysis, and all doses, volumes, rates and targets are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
