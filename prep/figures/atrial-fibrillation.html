What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How atrial fibrillation starts and is sustained. Rapid ectopic firing, most often from sleeves of atrial muscle around the pulmonary veins, can trigger atrial fibrillation. Once started, it is sustained by many wandering re-entrant wavelets and areas of fibrosis that break the wavefront into chaotic activity, so the atria quiver and do not contract in an organised way. The atrioventricular node is bombarded and conducts only some impulses, at irregular intervals, so the ventricular response is irregularly irregular and often fast. Loss of organised atrial contraction and slow, swirling flow in the left atrial appendage favour stasis and clot formation; a clot fragment can embolise to the brain and cause a stroke. Paroxysmal episodes come and go, persistent episodes last and usually need cardioversion to stop, and permanent atrial fibrillation is accepted and no longer chased. This is standard teaching in medicine and cardiology texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Treatment ideas shown. Rate control slows conduction through the AV node so the ventricles slow, but the atria keep fibrillating. Rhythm control with an antiarrhythmic drug or cardioversion tries to restore and hold sinus rhythm. Anticoagulation lowers the chance that a clot forms and embolises, so it lowers stroke risk without changing the rhythm itself. The combination pairs rhythm or rate control with anticoagulation. These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. Atrial flutter and other supraventricular rhythms, structural heart disease, triggers outside the pulmonary veins, ablation detail, left atrial appendage closure, rate versus rhythm trial evidence, anticoagulant choice, bleeding risk and risk scores, cardioversion safety steps, and all doses and targets are left out.
