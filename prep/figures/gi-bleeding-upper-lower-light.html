What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How gastrointestinal bleeding presents. Upper gastrointestinal bleeding, from oesophageal varices, a peptic ulcer, or a Mallory-Weiss tear torn by repeated vomiting, usually presents with haematemesis (vomited blood) and melaena (black, tarry stool from digested blood). Lower gastrointestinal bleeding, from diverticula, angiodysplasia, colitis, cancer or other colonic sources, usually presents with haematochezia (bright red blood per rectum), although a brisk upper bleed can do the same. Risk clues in the history include aspirin and other NSAIDs, anticoagulants, alcohol, liver disease and being elderly. A slow occult bleed may show only as anaemia, unintentional weight loss, anorexia, nausea or a change in bowel habit, which should prompt further testing and, where indicated, screening or endoscopy for cancer rather than reassurance. This is standard teaching in medicine and gastroenterology texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Assessment and treatment ideas shown. Assessment comes first: airway, breathing, circulation, pulse and blood pressure, signs of shock, and urgent tests including blood count, grouping and cross matching, clotting and liver tests. Clear fluids or nil by mouth, hospitalize decisions and how active the bleed looks all follow that assessment and local protocol; the figure does not set them. Resuscitation with fluids and blood restores circulating volume but does not stop the source. Endoscopic therapy at endoscopy (bands for varices, clips, heat or injection for ulcers and tears, and selected treatment for angiodysplasia or colitis) treats the source in the lumen. Surgery or embolisation is the fallback when bleeding cannot be controlled, is massive, or returns. Given the stakes, a patient who should be admitted and observed is not sent home on the strength of a single quiet moment. These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. Scoring systems, transfusion triggers, vasoactive and acid suppression drugs, variceal antibiotics, timing of endoscopy, interventional radiology details, obscure and small bowel bleeding, aortoenteric fistula, and all doses, targets and thresholds are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
