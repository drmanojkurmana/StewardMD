What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How pulmonary oedema develops. Net fluid movement across the pulmonary capillary follows Starling forces: hydrostatic pressure in the capillary pushes fluid out, plasma oncotic pressure holds fluid in, and the leakiness of the wall and the interstitial pressures set how easily fluid crosses. In cardiogenic pulmonary oedema the usual driver is elevated left sided filling pressure from left heart failure. Standard cardiac causes stressed in textbooks include myocardial infarction and coronary artery disease with ventricular dysfunction, severe hypertension, mitral valve disease and other mechanical problems of the heart; the failing left ventricle raises pulmonary vascular pressure until fluid leaves the capillary. In non-cardiogenic pulmonary oedema the barrier itself is injured and permeability rises, as in acute respiratory distress syndrome, so protein rich fluid leaks even without a raised hydrostatic pressure. Either way fluid collects first in the interstitium, then floods alveoli, usually beginning in dependent lung. Flooded alveoli are perfused but not ventilated, so gas exchange fails, oxygen falls, the lung stiffens and breathing becomes laboured; in extremis patients are deteriorating fast with evidence of systemic hypoperfusion when cardiogenic shock complicates the cardiac cause. This is standard teaching in medicine, cardiology and critical care texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Treatment ideas shown. A diuretic removes fluid and a nitrate, a nitric oxide donor, dilates veins and lowers preload, so capillary hydrostatic pressure falls. Positive pressure support recruits flooded alveoli, pushes fluid back toward the interstitium, and improves oxygen while the cause is treated. Treating the underlying cause means treating the cardiac cause in cardiogenic oedema or the illness driving increased permeability in non-cardiogenic oedema. These are teaching toggles only and show direction of effect, not how any patient should be treated.

What was simplified. Cardiogenic shock and other shock states, cardiac tamponade and detailed hemodynamic monitoring, lymphatic drainage, alveolar fluid clearance pumps, oxygen and ventilator settings, fluid strategy, and all doses and targets are left out.
