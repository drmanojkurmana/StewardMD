What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • What the gap means. Blood is electrically neutral: measured cations (mainly sodium) exceed the measured anions (chloride plus bicarbonate) by a small amount, the anion gap, which is mostly unmeasured anions such as albumin and phosphate. When an acid is added (lactate in shock, ketones in diabetic ketoacidosis, acids retained in kidney failure, or toxins such as methanol and salicylate), its hydrogen ions are buffered by bicarbonate, which falls, while its unmeasured anion remains, so the gap widens: a high anion gap acidosis. When bicarbonate is lost instead (diarrhoea, renal tubular acidosis, or other gut or kidney losses), chloride rises to keep neutrality, so the gap stays normal: a normal anion gap, or hyperchloraemic, acidosis. Mixed patterns occur when both happen. The lungs compensate with deep, faster breathing that blows off carbon dioxide. This is standard teaching in medicine, renal and critical-care texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Treatment ideas shown. Treating the cause (restoring perfusion, insulin for ketoacidosis, stopping a toxin, treating diarrhoea), giving bicarbonate as a buffer in selected cases, and dialysis to clear retained acids and toxins and restore bicarbonate. They are teaching toggles that show which step each acts on, not advice for any patient.

What was simplified. Albumin and phosphate as the usual gap, the delta ratio and other mixed-disorder calculations, urine anion and osmolal gaps, respiratory and renal compensation over days, specific toxin antidotes, and all doses, targets and thresholds are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
