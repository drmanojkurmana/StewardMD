What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How the cardiac action potential works. In a ventricular myocyte, opening of fast voltage-gated sodium channels drives the rapid upstroke. Brief potassium efflux gives an early notch. In the plateau, calcium entry through L-type calcium channels balances potassium efflux and holds the trace up while the cell contracts. Then calcium channels close and potassium efflux brings the cell back to rest. Pacemaker cells in the sinoatrial node have no stable rest: a slow inward current gives a rising slope to threshold and a slower, calcium-driven rise. The surface ECG sums these events: the spike reflects ventricular firing and the hump reflects resetting, so a longer action potential gives a longer QT. High outside potassium lifts the resting level, weakens sodium entry and speeds resetting. Low potassium slows potassium outflow, lengthens resetting and flattens the hump. This is standard teaching in physiology and cardiology texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Treatment ideas shown. A sodium channel blocker plugs the sodium channel and weakens the upstroke. A potassium channel blocker plugs the potassium channel, traps potassium inside, and lengthens the action potential and the QT. A calcium channel blocker plugs the calcium channel, shortens the plateau, and slows the pacemaker climb, which also calms a long QT. These are teaching toggles only.
