What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How a subarachnoid haemorrhage develops. Most spontaneous bleeds follow rupture of a berry (saccular) aneurysm at a branching point of the circle of Willis, for example on the anterior communicating, posterior communicating, middle cerebral or basilar artery. Blood under arterial pressure floods the subarachnoid space, giving a sudden thunderclap headache, vomiting, collapse and raised intracranial pressure. Early dangers are rerupture of the unsecured aneurysm and hydrocephalus when blood blocks cerebrospinal fluid circulation and absorption. Over the following days, classically from day 4 to about day 10 and sometimes into the next two weeks, blood breakdown products around cerebral arteries provoke vasospasm; narrowed arteries can cause delayed cerebral ischemia and focal neurologic deficits, including motor weakness, which may progress to infarction. Severity is graded clinically (for example the World Federation of Neurosurgical Societies grade, based on the Glasgow Coma Scale score and focal deficits) and by the amount of blood on imaging. This is standard teaching in medicine and neurology texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Investigation and treatment ideas shown. The aneurysm is located with CT angiography or catheter angiography. Securing it early, either with a surgical clip across its neck or with endovascular coiling techniques through the artery, prevents rerupture; neither removes blood already in the subarachnoid space. Nimodipine is given to improve outcome after aneurysmal bleeding and lessen delayed ischaemia; it does not seal the aneurysm. Hydrocephalus is relieved by draining cerebrospinal fluid, shown here as an external drain. These are teaching toggles only, and major accompanying care such as blood pressure control, reversal of coagulopathy and critical care is not shown.

What was simplified. Non-aneurysmal causes, aneurysm formation and growth, sentinel leaks, seizures, hyponatraemia and other systemic complications, transcranial Doppler monitoring, induced hypertension and other vasospasm rescue techniques, permanent shunting, drug choice and all doses and targets are left out.
