What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How adrenal insufficiency develops. In primary adrenal insufficiency the adrenal cortex itself fails, most often from autoimmune adrenalitis. Cortisol and aldosterone are both lost. Low cortisol removes feedback on the pituitary, so ACTH rises, and the shared precursor pathway also raises melanocyte stimulating activity, which darkens the skin (pigmentation). In secondary adrenal insufficiency the pituitary makes too little ACTH, so cortisol falls but aldosterone is largely preserved because aldosterone is controlled mainly by the renin angiotensin system and potassium, not ACTH. Loss of cortisol and, in primary disease, aldosterone gives low blood pressure, low sodium, high potassium and low glucose, and an intercurrent stress can tip the patient into an adrenal crisis. This is standard teaching in medicine and endocrinology texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Treatment ideas shown. Hydrocortisone replaces cortisol and fludrocortisone replaces aldosterone for ongoing replacement in primary disease. In a crisis, IV fluids restore circulating volume alongside hydrocortisone. A stress dose is the teaching concept of extra hydrocortisone cover when illness or stress raises the body need for cortisol. These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. Tertiary (hypothalamic) disease, the full renin angiotensin detail, ACTH and cortisol blood tests and their cut-offs, other causes of gland failure such as infection, haemorrhage and infiltration, mineralocorticoid need in secondary disease, and all doses, timings and targets are left out. The two columns are a schematic contrast, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
