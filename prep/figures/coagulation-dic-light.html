What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How a clot forms. When the vessel lining breaks, collagen and tissue factor are exposed. Platelets stick through von Willebrand factor (primary haemostasis), activate and clump through fibrinogen bridges (GPIIb/IIIa) into a plug. At the same time the clotting cascade runs: tissue factor with factor VIIa (extrinsic arm, tested by PT), the contact factors XII, XI, IX with VIII (intrinsic arm, tested by aPTT), both feeding factor X with V (common arm) to make thrombin. Thrombin turns fibrinogen into fibrin and factor XIII cross-links it. Plasmin later dissolves the clot (fibrinolysis), releasing D-dimer. This is standard teaching in medicine, haematology and pathology texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • DIC. A massive trigger (for example severe sepsis or an obstetric catastrophe) switches clotting on everywhere. Micro-clots block small vessels, and platelets and clotting factors are consumed faster than they are made, so the same patient can clot and bleed. Treating the cause matters most.
  • Treatment ideas shown. Heparin acts through antithrombin on Xa and thrombin, warfarin lowers the vitamin K factors II, VII, IX and X, direct oral anticoagulants block Xa or thrombin, antiplatelet drugs block platelet activation or aggregation, and replacement restores what was used up. These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. The cell-based model of coagulation, natural anticoagulants (protein C, protein S, antithrombin), contact-activation detail, inherited bleeding and clotting disorders, laboratory interpretation, reversal agents, monitoring, targets and all doses are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
