What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • Beta-blockers and selectivity (well established). Beta-blockers are competitive antagonists at beta-adrenoceptors. Non-selective agents such as propranolol block both beta-1 and beta-2 receptors; cardioselective agents such as metoprolol act mainly on beta-1 receptors, which sit mainly in the heart, whereas beta-2 receptors are spread across many organs and relax smooth muscle. The negative chronotropic and inotropic effects lower the heart's oxygen demand (Beta Blockers, StatPearls).
  • Selectivity is relative, not absolute. In binding studies on human receptors expressed in one cell line, clinically used antagonists ranged from modest beta-1 selectivity to modest beta-2 selectivity and most showed little selectivity, so “beta-1 selective” describes a preference that can be lost at higher exposure (Baker 2005, Br J Pharmacol). The figure therefore leaves a small residual block of beta-2 under the beta-1 selective setting and shows no affinity numbers.
  • Receptor and G protein. Beta-adrenoceptors are seven-transmembrane G protein-coupled receptors; the beta-2 and beta-1 receptor structures were among the first solved for ligand-activated GPCRs (Rosenbaum, Rasmussen & Kobilka 2009, Nature). Activation makes Gαs swap GDP for GTP, Gαs·GTP stimulates adenylyl cyclase, and the GTPase of Gα ends the signal.
  • Heart downstream. Sympathetic stimulation raises cardiac L-type calcium current through beta-adrenergic signalling, cAMP and PKA phosphorylation of the channel; calcium entry triggers release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum (Bers 2002, Nature; PKA raises the open probability of the channel, Ginsburg & Bers 2004).
  • Molecule. Noradrenaline (norepinephrine), 4-(2-amino-1-hydroxyethyl)benzene-1,2-diol, C8H11NO3 (PubChem CID 439260).

What was simplified. Only beta-1 in the heart and beta-2 in the airway are drawn. Beta-1 receptors also sit in the kidney and beta-2 receptors also in the heart, vessels, liver and muscle; beta-3 receptors, intrinsic sympathomimetic activity, vasodilating beta-blockers (carvedilol, labetalol), inverse agonism, beta-arrestin signalling, phosphodiesterases, Gi coupling of beta-2 in the heart, receptor desensitisation and the reflex rise in sympathetic drive are all left out. The two cells are drawn side by side only to compare receptor types. Activation levels, the size of the block and timing are illustrative; protein shapes are cartoons, not structures. “Non-selective” and “β1-selective” are drug classes shown as teaching toggles: no named drug, dose or schedule is shown or implied, and the figure does not say who should or should not take a beta-blocker (for example in asthma or heart failure).
