What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How acute mesenteric ischaemia develops. The superior mesenteric artery may be blocked suddenly by an embolus, or by thrombosis on a narrowed artery, or flow may fall without a block (nonocclusive low flow), or the mesenteric vein may clot. The mucosa, the inner lining, is the most sensitive layer and is injured first; injury then spreads outward to become transmural. Typical teaching is severe pain out of proportion to the signs found on examination early on. With infarction the bowel may perforate, spilling bowel contents and causing sepsis, while lactate rises as tissue is starved of oxygen. This is standard teaching in surgery and medicine texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Treatment ideas shown. Resuscitation and anticoagulation to support circulation and stop clot spreading, revascularisation to restore flow to salvageable bowel, and resection to remove infarcted bowel. These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. Collateral supply, chronic mesenteric ischaemia, imaging and endovascular versus open techniques, vasopressors and other adjuncts, second-look surgery, short bowel after large resections, and all doses and targets are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
