What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • Absorption and first pass. After an oral dose, drug crosses the gut wall into portal blood and reaches the liver before the systemic circulation, so gut wall and liver enzymes can remove part of the dose before it acts (first-pass metabolism) and bioavailability is less than complete. An intravenous dose enters the systemic blood directly, bypasses absorption and first pass, and reaches a higher, faster peak. This is standard pharmacology teaching and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Distribution, binding and half-life. In plasma, drug exists in a dynamic balance between protein-bound drug (a circulating store that does not leave the blood) and free drug (the form that crosses into tissues, acts, and is cleared). Distribution into tissues and back, together with total clearance by metabolism and excretion, sets the half-life: the time for the plasma concentration to fall by half. Reduced clearance prolongs half-life and raises exposure. Flagged for review.
  • Metabolism, excretion, interactions and steady state. The liver metabolises drug (with a smaller biliary route into the gut), and the kidney excretes drug and metabolites in urine. Enzyme inducers speed liver metabolism and lower exposure; enzyme inhibitors slow it and raise exposure and half-life. With repeated dosing at a regular interval, drug accumulates until the amount cleared between doses balances the amount given, reaching a steady state; when kidney function is poor, adjusting the regimen (for example spacing doses) brings exposure back toward usual. These are teaching toggles only. Flagged for review.

What was simplified. Transporters, enterohepatic recycling detail, tissue binding, volume of distribution as a measured quantity, active metabolites, renal tubular secretion and reabsorption detail, saturable kinetics, and all doses, intervals and targets are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
