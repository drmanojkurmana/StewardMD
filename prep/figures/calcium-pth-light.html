What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • Calcium and PTH physiology. Extracellular calcium is sensed by the calcium sensing receptor on parathyroid chief cells. When calcium falls, the gland releases parathyroid hormone (PTH). PTH stimulates osteoclasts to resorb bone, tells the kidney to reclaim calcium and waste phosphate while switching on active vitamin D, and active vitamin D drives gut calcium uptake. Calcium and vitamin D feed back to quiet the gland. This is standard teaching in endocrinology and physiology texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Low versus high calcium states. In hypoparathyroidism the failed glands make too little PTH, so calcium falls and phosphate rises, and nerves and muscle become excitable with cramps and spasms. In vitamin D deficiency the glands work overtime to compensate. In primary hyperparathyroidism, usually a small benign adenoma, autonomous PTH drives calcium high with stones, bone loss, weakness and fatigue. Flagged for review.
  • Conceptual treatments shown. Calcium with active vitamin D to top up low calcium, saline fluids to help the kidney flush out high calcium, bisphosphonates to coat bone and quiet osteoclasts, calcimimetics to sensitize the gland sensor so less PTH is released, and parathyroidectomy to remove the overactive tissue. These are teaching toggles only, with no doses or drug regimens.
