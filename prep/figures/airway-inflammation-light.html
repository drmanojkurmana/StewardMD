What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • Type-2 pathway. Allergens and other triggers damage or stimulate the airway lining, which releases alarmins (TSLP, IL-33, IL-25). These wake ILC2 and Th2 cells, which make IL-4, IL-5 and IL-13. IL-5 recruits and sustains eosinophils. IL-4 drives B cells to make IgE, which sits on mast cells and releases histamine and leukotrienes when an allergen cross-links it. IL-13 makes goblet cells produce thick mucus and makes the smooth muscle twitchy. This is standard teaching in respiratory medicine and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Non-type-2 pathway. Smoke, infection and pollution drive Th17 cells and IL-17 and IL-8 release, which bring in neutrophils. This pattern responds poorly to type-2 targeted antibodies and often to inhaled steroid too.
  • Treatments shown. The mark on the diagram shows where each class acts: an inhaled steroid switches off many inflammatory genes across cells; anti-IgE blocks IgE on mast cells; anti-IL-5 removes IL-5 signalling to eosinophils; anti-IL-4 receptor alpha blocks IL-4 and IL-13 signalling; anti-TSLP blocks an upstream alarmin; a bronchodilator only relaxes the muscle. These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. Many other cell types and mediators (basophils, Th1, IL-9, IL-17 in type-2 disease, epithelial remodelling, infection triggers in detail), steroid-resistance mechanisms, biomarker use (blood eosinophils, FeNO, IgE), who qualifies for each drug, side effects, and all doses and schedules are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
