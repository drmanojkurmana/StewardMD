What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How chronic kidney disease develops. Nephrons are lost over time from causes such as diabetes, high blood pressure, glomerular disease and ageing. The nephrons that remain adapt by filtering more each (hyperfiltration and enlargement), which holds total filtration up for a while but adds strain. As loss continues, total filtration (GFR) falls and waste products that the kidney normally clears, including urea, potassium, hydrogen ions (acid) and phosphate, are retained. This is standard teaching in medicine and nephrology texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Blood, mineral and pressure effects. The healthy kidney makes erythropoietin, the hormone that tells the bone marrow to make red cells; when it falls, anaemia follows. Retained phosphate, together with disturbed vitamin D and calcium, drives the parathyroid glands to release more parathyroid hormone (secondary hyperparathyroidism), which draws calcium from bone and thins it. Retained salt and water, with activation of pressure-raising systems, causes hypertension, which in turn speeds further nephron loss. Flagged for review.
  • Treatment ideas shown. Blood pressure control protects the remaining nephrons and the circulation. A phosphate binder in the gut plus active vitamin D lowers phosphate and parathyroid hormone. Erythropoietin replaces the missing hormone and raises haemoglobin. Dialysis is an artificial filter that clears retained urea, potassium, acid and phosphate when the kidney can no longer do so. These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. The many causes and stages of chronic kidney disease, the exact stage thresholds, protein in the urine as an early marker, cardiovascular disease, drug dose adjustment, transplant, and all doses and targets are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
