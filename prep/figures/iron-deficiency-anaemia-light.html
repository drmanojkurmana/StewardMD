What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How iron normally moves. Iron from food is absorbed mainly in the duodenum. In blood it is carried by the transport protein transferrin to the bone marrow, where it is built into haem for haemoglobin in new red cells. Spare iron is held in the storage protein ferritin, mostly in the liver and marrow. This is standard teaching in medicine and haematology texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • How deficiency develops. Iron is lost or never taken in faster than it is absorbed: chronic blood loss (for example from the gut or heavy periods), poor dietary intake, and malabsorption (for example coeliac disease or after gut surgery) are the classic causes. The body has no active way to excrete iron, so balance is set at absorption. First the ferritin stores empty while haemoglobin is still normal, then the marrow makes red cells short of iron (iron-deficient erythropoiesis), and only then does haemoglobin fall (anaemia). Flagged for review.
  • What the anaemia looks and feels like. Red cells made without enough iron are small (microcytic) and pale (hypochromic), with less haemoglobin in each cell. Symptoms follow the low haemoglobin and the empty stores: tiredness, pallor, breathlessness on effort, and features such as brittle nails, hair loss and pica in some people. Flagged for review.
  • Treatment ideas shown. Oral iron is absorbed through the duodenum and slowly refills stores. IV iron bypasses the gut and refills stores faster, useful when absorption fails or losses continue. Treating the cause stops the leak at its source but on its own does not quickly replace iron already lost, so it is often combined with iron. These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. Hepcidin control of absorption, iron recycling from old red cells by macrophages, the laboratory pattern (ferritin, transferrin saturation, blood film), other causes of microcytic anaemia such as thalassaemia, pregnancy and childhood demand, transfusion, and all doses and targets are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any person should be treated.
