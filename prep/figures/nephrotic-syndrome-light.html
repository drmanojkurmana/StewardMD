What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • What nephrotic syndrome is. Heavy protein in the urine (heavy proteinuria) from a leaky glomerular filter, low blood albumin (hypoalbuminaemia), oedema from fluid leaving the circulation, and raised blood lipids (hyperlipidaemia) made by the liver in response to the protein loss. Loss of regulatory and defence proteins in the urine also raises the risk of blood clots (thrombosis) and of infection. This is standard teaching in medicine and nephrology texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • The three lesions on the slider. In minimal change disease the glomerulus looks near normal by light microscopy but podocyte foot processes are spread flat and fused (effaced) when seen closely, and it usually responds well to a steroid. In focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS) some glomeruli have a scarred segment of the tuft with podocyte loss, and the response to a steroid is often only partial. In membranous nephropathy immune deposits collect under the podocytes (subepithelial deposits), the basement membrane thickens around them, a steroid alone usually helps little, and broader immunosuppression is the teaching toggle that helps most. Flagged for review.
  • Treatment ideas shown. A steroid calms the podocyte injury, best in minimal change disease. An ACE inhibitor lowers pressure inside the glomerulus and cuts protein leak, and a diuretic removes oedema fluid, without repairing the filter itself. Immunosuppression dampens the immune injury, shown strongest in membranous disease. These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. The many other causes of nephrotic syndrome, biopsy appearances in detail, complement and antibody testing, salt restriction, anticoagulation decisions, lipid treatment, relapse patterns, and all doses and targets are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
