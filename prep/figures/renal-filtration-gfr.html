What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How filtration is set. The glomerulus is a tuft of capillaries between two arterioles. Filtration across its barrier (capillary endothelium, basement membrane and podocytes) is driven by the balance of pressures: glomerular capillary hydrostatic pressure favours filtration, while Bowman capsule pressure and the oncotic pressure of plasma proteins oppose it. The net filtration pressure, the area and permeability of the barrier, and the plasma flow together set the glomerular filtration rate (GFR). This is standard physiology teaching and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Autoregulation and tubuloglomerular feedback. Across a range of arterial pressures the kidney holds renal blood flow and GFR nearly steady: the afferent arteriole responds directly to stretch (the myogenic response), and the macula densa, a plaque of distal tubule cells at the vascular pole, senses sodium chloride delivery. When delivery rises it signals the afferent arteriole to tighten and filtration to fall; when delivery falls the afferent arteriole relaxes and renin release supports efferent tone. Prostaglandins help keep the afferent arteriole open when perfusion is threatened, and angiotensin II preferentially tightens the efferent arteriole to hold glomerular pressure up. Flagged for review.
  • Afferent versus efferent tightening, and the two drug classes. Afferent constriction lowers glomerular pressure, plasma flow and GFR. Efferent constriction raises glomerular pressure and GFR for a while but lowers plasma flow, so the filtration fraction rises. An NSAID blocks prostaglandin production and so removes afferent dilation, which can drop GFR sharply when perfusion already depends on it. An ACE inhibitor blocks angiotensin II formation and so releases efferent tightening, lowering glomerular pressure and GFR, an effect that protects an overloaded glomerulus but can drop filtration when afferent inflow is poor. These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. Changes in barrier area and permeability, glomerular disease, the full renin and angiotensin cascade, sympathetic and hormonal control, the differing effects along the length of the capillary as oncotic pressure rises, and all doses and targets are left out.
