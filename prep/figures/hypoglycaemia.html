What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How blood glucose falls, and how the body answers. Glucose stops coming in during fasting, and excess insulin (from treatment, an insulin secreting tumour or other causes) drives glucose into muscle and suppresses liver glucose production from glycogen breakdown and gluconeogenesis. As glucose falls, insulin secretion normally switches off and counter-regulatory hormones rise: glucagon and adrenaline act fast, cortisol and growth hormone act more slowly, all pushing the liver to release glucose. Adrenaline also causes the warning symptoms (sweating, shaking, racing heart, hunger). The brain depends on a steady glucose supply, so continued shortage causes neuroglycopenic symptoms (poor concentration, confusion, odd behaviour, drowsiness and, if severe, seizures or coma). This is standard teaching in medicine and endocrine texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Treatment ideas shown. Oral glucose only when the person is awake and can swallow safely, intravenous glucose when they cannot, and glucagon, which raises glucose by telling the liver to release its glycogen stores and so may work less well after prolonged fasting when stores are low. Finding and treating the cause matters as much as raising the glucose. These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. Sulfonylurea and other drug causes, alcohol, critical illness, hormone deficiencies, rechecking and follow on treatment, driving and safety rules, and all doses, thresholds and targets are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
