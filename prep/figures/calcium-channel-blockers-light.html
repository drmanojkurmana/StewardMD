What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • Two groups of calcium channel blockers. Dihydropyridines act mainly on vascular smooth muscle and dilate arterioles; verapamil and diltiazem are also vasodilators but have clearly negative chronotropic, inotropic and dromotropic effects, slowing the rate, force and AV conduction. The two groups bind separate sites on the L-type channel (Review of calcium channel blockers, PMC; British and Irish Hypertension Society, calcium channel blockers).
  • Calcium and contraction. In smooth muscle calcium raises contraction through calmodulin and myosin light chain kinase; in the heart calcium entry triggers further release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum. This is standard physiology, in the pharmacology texts the PrepNucleus lessons cite (Katzung; KD Tripathi) and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.

What was simplified. Reflex tachycardia after a fast-acting dihydropyridine, ankle oedema, other calcium channel types (T-type, N-type), differences between individual drugs (and drugs within a group), use dependence and voltage dependence of block, interactions (digoxin, ciclosporin, beta-blockers), and the effect in heart failure or heart block are left out. Levels, flow and the size of the block are illustrative. The drug choices are teaching toggles of a class: no named drug, dose or schedule is shown or implied, and the figure does not say who should or should not take any of them.
