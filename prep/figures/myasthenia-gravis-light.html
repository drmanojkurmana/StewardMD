What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How myasthenia gravis weakens muscle. In the common form, antibodies against the acetylcholine receptor at the neuromuscular junction block the receptor and attract complement. Complement damages the folded muscle membrane and receptors are lost, so each nerve impulse makes a smaller endplate signal. When that signal falls below what is needed, the fibre fails, and failure grows with repeated use: fatigable weakness. The thymus is often abnormal in this disease and helps drive the faulty antibody response, which is why removing it can help some people. Eye muscles are often first (drooping lid, double vision), then limb and bulbar muscles, and in crisis the breathing muscles. This is standard teaching in neurology texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Treatment ideas shown. A cholinesterase inhibitor slows breakdown of acetylcholine in the cleft, so what receptors remain get a stronger, longer signal, but it does not stop the antibody attack. Immunosuppression lowers antibody production over time. Thymectomy removes the thymus source and plasmapheresis removes circulating antibody, so receptor damage eases. These are teaching toggles only and name no individual drug or dose.

What was simplified. Other antibody types (for example MuSK and LRP4), congenital myasthenic syndromes, tests such as repetitive stimulation and single fibre studies, individual immunosuppressant drugs and their risks, who benefits from thymectomy, intensive care for crisis, and all doses and targets are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
