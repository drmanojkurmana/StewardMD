What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How a pleural effusion forms. The pleural space between the visceral and parietal pleura normally holds only a thin film of fluid. Fluid accumulates when formation beats absorption. A transudate follows raised hydrostatic pressure (for example in heart failure) or low serum oncotic pressure, and is low in protein. An exudate follows inflammation or malignancy: capillaries become leaky, so protein and cells escape. Comparing pleural fluid protein and LDH with serum values is the idea behind Light criteria for separating an exudate from a transudate, and a diagnostic pleural tap (thoracentesis) is how the fluid is sampled. This is standard teaching in medicine and respiratory texts (Harrison, Disorders of the Pleura) and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Effects and treatment ideas shown. A growing effusion compresses the lung, makes percussion dull over the fluid, and causes breathlessness. Treating the cause lowers formation, drainage by thoracentesis or a chest drain removes fluid and lets the lung re expand, and pleurodesis seals the pleural space so fluid cannot reaccumulate easily. These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. Pleural anatomy in detail, lymphatic absorption, empyema, haemothorax, chylothorax, imaging signs, the full Light criteria values, fluid chemistry, contraindications, and all doses and procedural technique are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
