What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • The axes. The hypothalamus releases TRH (thyroid axis) or CRH (adrenal axis). These make the anterior pituitary release TSH or ACTH, which drive the thyroid gland or the adrenal cortex to make thyroid hormone (T4, T3) or cortisol. The end hormone feeds back on both the pituitary and the hypothalamus (negative feedback), so the end hormone is held near a set level. TSH and ACTH also keep their gland grown. This is standard teaching in endocrinology and physiology texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Reading the fault. In primary failure the gland cannot respond, so the end hormone is low and the pituitary hormone is high. In secondary or tertiary failure the pituitary or hypothalamus fails, so the end hormone is low and the pituitary hormone is low or inappropriately normal (central hypothyroidism, secondary adrenal insufficiency). In primary excess the gland makes hormone on its own, so the pituitary hormone is suppressed. In secondary excess a pituitary tumour escapes feedback, so the pituitary hormone stays high and drives the gland (for example Cushing disease). Replacing an end hormone in a healthy axis, or blocking it in a failing one, pushes the axis the wrong way.
  • Treatment ideas shown. Replacing the missing end hormone, suppressing or blocking an excess one, and removing the source (the overactive gland or the pituitary tumour). These are teaching toggles only, with no agent names or doses.

What was simplified. Aldosterone and the renin system, thyroid hormone conversion and binding, immune and autoimmune causes, ectopic ACTH and other non-pituitary sources, tertiary excess, pituitary hormones other than TSH and ACTH, dynamic tests, the care needed when a source is removed or an axis is replaced (including stress cover for cortisol), and all doses and targets are left out. Gland size and hormone flow are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated or monitored.
