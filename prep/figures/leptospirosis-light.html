What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How leptospirosis develops. Leptospira bacteria live in the kidneys of animals and leave in urine, contaminating water and soil. After an incubation period they enter through cuts in skin or through mucosa, reach the blood (leptospiraemia) and spread to tissues. Outer membrane proteins help them attach and evade early immunity. The first phase brings fever, headache, muscle pain and conjunctival suffusion (red eyes without discharge). Antibodies then clear leptospires from blood, but they may persist in kidney tubules and appear in urine. In severe Weil disease, vasculitis and tissue injury cause jaundice from liver dysfunction, renal failure from tubular injury and interstitial nephritis, bleeding, and pulmonary haemorrhage, which drives mortality. Although many illnesses are anicteric and mild, severe disease can be fatal. This is standard textbook teaching, flagged as AI-assisted for clinician review.
  • Treatment ideas shown. Prophylaxis before exposure can reduce entry. Doxycycline or penicillin kills leptospires. Supportive care with dialysis supports failing kidneys and other organs but does not itself kill the bacteria. These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. Animal reservoirs in detail, serovar differences, laboratory diagnosis, meningitis, uveitis, myocarditis, exact immune mechanisms, and all doses and dialysis prescriptions are left out.
