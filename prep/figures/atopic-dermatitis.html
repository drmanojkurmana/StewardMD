What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How atopic dermatitis develops. In many people the skin barrier is weakened, in part through filaggrin, a protein that helps the outer skin bricks hold together and hold water. Gaps in that barrier increase water loss, so skin dries, and let allergens and microbes enter. Staphylococcus aureus often colonises the broken, inflamed skin and can worsen flares. Immune cells in the skin take a Th2 pattern and release IL-4 and IL-13, which further weaken the barrier, help B cells make IgE, and drive itch. Scratching for itch breaks the barrier again, so the itch and scratch cycle keeps the disease going. Long standing rubbing and inflammation thicken the skin, called lichenification. The typical sites change with age: face and trunk in infancy, the elbow and knee folds in childhood, and hands and folds in adults. This is standard teaching in dermatology and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Treatment ideas shown. An emollient fills gaps, reduces water loss and helps the barrier recover. A topical steroid calms the Th2 inflammation and itch in the skin. A topical immunomodulator or a biologic that blocks IL-4 and IL-13 signalling reduces Th2 drive, IgE help and itch. These are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. Other barrier genes, skin acidity and lipids in detail, food allergy testing, infection that needs separate treatment, eye and other complications, wet wrap and phototherapy, systemic treatments beyond the IL-4 and IL-13 idea, side effects, and all doses are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
