What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • Central and peripheral tolerance. Autoimmunity follows failure of tolerance to self antigens. In central tolerance, developing T cells in the thymus and B cells in the bone marrow that bind self strongly are deleted by cell death, edited or left unresponsive. In peripheral tolerance, regulatory T cells suppress escapees, and cells met by self antigen without costimulation become anergic. These layers normally keep immune responses to self low while preserving responses to microbes.
  • Why tolerance fails. Susceptibility is genetic and environmental in various combinations. Certain HLA genes and many non-HLA genes alter antigen presentation and lymphocyte signalling and enhance risk in susceptible individuals; epigenetic change and innate sensing can promote proinflammatory responses. Environmental triggers include infection, where microbial antigens mimic self (molecular mimicry), and tissue injury that releases hidden or sequestered self antigens and nucleic acids. Defective clearance of dying cells and nucleic acids, described in systemic disease, keeps self material available and sustains inflammation. Hormonal and other factors linked to sex are considered relevant because many systemic autoimmune diseases are more common in women. The kidney stands in here as one example target; organ-specific examples include Graves disease of the thyroid, while systemic examples spread across skin, joints, kidney and vessels. This is standard teaching in immunology texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Treatment ideas shown. Replacing lost function, for example a missing hormone, restores what the damaged organ no longer provides without itself stopping the immune attack. Anti-B cell therapy removes B cells and lowers autoantibodies while T cell injury may persist. Immunosuppression dampens autoreactive T and B cell activity broadly and so calms the whole attack. These are teaching toggles only.
