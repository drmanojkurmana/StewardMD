What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How DKA develops. Severe insulin deficiency, with raised glucagon and stress hormones, stops muscle and fat taking up glucose and lets the liver make glucose (glycogen breakdown, gluconeogenesis). Fat breaks down into free fatty acids, and the liver turns them into ketone bodies (beta-hydroxybutyrate, acetoacetate), which are acids and lower blood pH with a raised anion gap. Glucose above the kidney threshold causes an osmotic diuresis that loses water, sodium and potassium. The lungs compensate with deep, fast breathing (Kussmaul). Insulin deficiency and acidosis shift potassium out of cells, so the blood level can look normal or high while total body potassium is low. This is standard teaching in medicine and endocrine texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Treatment ideas shown. Fluids restore circulating volume; insulin switches off ketone production and drives glucose and potassium into cells; potassium replacement (with monitoring) prevents a dangerous fall once insulin starts; the cause (infection, missed insulin, new diabetes) is treated. The figure shows the direction of each effect, not how much to give or when.

What was simplified. The hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state is only suggested by the severe stop; cerebral oedema, phosphate and magnesium, bicarbonate use, the switch to subcutaneous insulin, euglycaemic DKA with SGLT2 inhibitors, and all doses, rates and targets are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
