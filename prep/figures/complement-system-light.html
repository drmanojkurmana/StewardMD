What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • How complement works. Three routes switch the system on. The classical pathway starts when antibody on a surface, with C1, is recognised. The lectin pathway starts when mannose-binding lectin or ficolins bind microbial sugars. The alternative pathway ticks over continuously and is amplified on microbial surfaces that lack host regulators. All three build a C3 convertase (classical and lectin C4b2a; alternative C3bBb), which splits C3 into C3a and C3b. C3b coats microbes (opsonisation) so phagocytes can eat them, and more C3b forms a C5 convertase that splits C5 into C5a and C5b. C3a and C5a are anaphylatoxins, and C5a is a strong neutrophil attractant. C5b starts the membrane attack complex (C5b, C6, C7, C8 and many C9) that punches pores in bacterial membranes and lyses the cell. Host cells are protected by regulators such as C1 inhibitor, factor H, factor I, CD55 and CD59. This is standard teaching in immunology texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Treatment ideas shown. A C5 blocker stops C5 cleavage, so C5a and the membrane attack complex fall while upstream C3b coating is preserved. Replacement of a missing component restores a step that was absent or low. They are teaching toggles only.

What was simplified. Individual complement proteins beyond the headline steps, the properdin stabilising loop in detail, complement receptors, immune-complex handling by red cells, genetic deficiency patterns, infection risk with terminal pathway blockade, and every drug, dose and test are left out. Counts and speeds are illustrative, and the figure does not say how any patient should be treated.
