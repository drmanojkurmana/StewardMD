What the figure shows, and how sure we are

  • Superficial infection. Dermatophyte fungi (species of Trichophyton, Microsporum and Epidermophyton) digest keratin in skin, hair and nails. They cause tinea: scaly, itchy, ring-like lesions that spread at the edge, scalp and hair-shaft infection, and onychomycosis, where the nail thickens and crumbles. Malassezia, a skin yeast, causes pityriasis versicolor. Superficial disease usually responds to topical therapy, but hair and nail infection needs oral therapy for months because the drug must grow out with the nail. This is standard teaching in dermatology and infectious-disease texts and was not checked against a linked source here, so it is flagged for review.
  • Candida and invasive disease. Candida is a normal mucosal organism that causes candidiasis (white plaques, soreness) when local or immune defences slip, and can enter the blood in very ill or immunosuppressed patients. Aspergillus spores are inhaled and can invade lung tissue and blood vessels when neutrophil defences are weak. Cryptococcus is an encapsulated yeast that can reach the meninges and cause meningitis, mainly in advanced immune suppression.
  • Cell targets and treatment ideas shown. The fungal wall is rich in glucan, and the membrane contains ergosterol instead of cholesterol. Azoles (topical, or systemic such as itraconazole) block ergosterol making; terbinafine acts on the same pathway. Polyenes bind ergosterol and punch holes in the membrane. Echinocandins block glucan making, so the wall weakens. Topical therapy only reaches the surface, so deep infection needs a systemic class. These are teaching toggles only.
