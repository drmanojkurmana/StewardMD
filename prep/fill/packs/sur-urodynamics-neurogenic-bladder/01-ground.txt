Filling cystometry separates bladder pressure from abdominal pressure. Detrusor pressure, Pdet, is calculated continuously by subtracting intra-abdominal pressure, Pabd measured through a rectal balloon, from total intravesical pressure, Pves. Without this subtraction a cough or a strain looks like a detrusor contraction, so every modern trace displays all three lines and the diagnosis is read from Pdet alone.

Compliance describes how quietly the bladder stores urine. It is defined as change in volume divided by change in detrusor pressure, Delta-V over Delta-Pdet, with values below 12 to 20 mL per cmH2O marking dangerously low compliance. A stiff low-compliance bladder transmits storage pressures straight to the upper tracts, so compliance is the number that decides whether the kidneys are safe during the filling phase.

Leak point pressure predicts renal damage. A detrusor leak point pressure above 40 cmH2O in neurogenic patients strongly predicts upper urinary tract deterioration with hydronephrosis and secondary renal damage. The threshold works because sustained storage pressure overwhelms ureteral peristalsis and refluxes infected urine upward, so crossing 40 commits the patient to pressure-lowering management.

Lesions above the pons overstimulate an intact cord. Suprapontine lesions such as stroke and Parkinson disease typically produce detrusor overactivity with coordinated sphincter relaxation, preserving normal voiding synergy. The bladder contracts too often but empties cleanly, so management targets urgency and frequency with antimuscarinics or beta-3 agonists rather than catheters.

Lesions between the pons and sacrum disconnect coordination. Suprasacral spinal injuries, including cervical and thoracic trauma above T6, cause detrusor-sphincter dyssynergia, in which involuntary detrusor contraction meets involuntary external sphincter closure. The bladder squeezes against a shut outlet, generating dangerous pressures with poor emptying, and the combination threatens both continence and the upper tracts.

High cord lesions add a cardiovascular emergency. Autonomic dysreflexia strikes spinal cord lesions at or above T6, classically triggered by bladder overdistension, and presents with paroxysmal life-threatening hypertension, severe headache, bradycardia, and diaphoresis above the lesion. Immediate bladder drainage aborts most episodes, and every patient with a high lesion carries a plan for rapid recognition and sitting upright with the bladder emptied.

Sacral lesions silence the bladder. Injuries of the conus medullaris and cauda equina, or pelvic plexus damage during rectal and gynaecological surgery, produce an acontractile detrusor with low compliance and no voluntary sphincter control. The bladder neither contracts nor holds pressure safely, so retention with overflow incontinence is the rule and the upper tracts need scheduled surveillance.

Emptying failure is managed with a catheter the patient passes. Clean intermittent catheterization, introduced by Lapides, remains the cornerstone gold standard for incomplete emptying and neuropathic retention. Done four to six times daily with adequate fluid discipline, it keeps pressures low, clears residual urine, and protects the kidneys better than any indwelling tube, with antimuscarinics added when overactivity persists between catheterizations.
