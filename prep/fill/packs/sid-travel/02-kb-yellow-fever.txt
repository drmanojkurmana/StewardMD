# Yellow fever
Yellow fever, Infectious / Tropical, Infectious Diseases.
Aliases: YF; yellow fever virus infection; yellow jack.
# Pathophysiology
Yellow fever virus, an RNA flavivirus, is inoculated by an infected mosquito and spreads via lymphatics and blood to target organs, with marked hepatotropism. Infection and death of hepatocytes (with characteristic midzonal necrosis and Councilman bodies) impair synthesis of clotting factors and cause jaundice, while direct renal injury produces acute kidney injury. The combination of coagulopathy, thrombocytopenia and vascular injury drives the haemorrhagic manifestations, and a dysregulated inflammatory response contributes to shock and multi-organ failure in the toxic phase.
# Prognosis
Many infections are mild or self-limiting and recover fully. Once the toxic phase develops, mortality is high, with death typically from hepatic and renal failure, bleeding and shock; survivors of the toxic phase generally recover without chronic sequelae.
# Clinical pearls
A mosquito-borne flaviviral haemorrhagic fever endemic to parts of sub-Saharan Africa and tropical South America, transmitted by Aedes and forest mosquitoes
India has no endemic yellow fever, but requires proof of vaccination from travellers arriving from endemic areas because the competent Aedes vector is present, posing an introduction risk
Illness is often biphasic: an initial febrile phase with headache and myalgia, sometimes followed after a brief remission by a toxic phase with jaundice, haemorrhage and organ failure
The toxic phase is characterised by hepatic and renal injury, coagulopathy and bleeding, and carries high mortality
A relative bradycardia despite high fever (Faget sign) is a described clinical clue
There is no specific antiviral treatment; care is supportive with management of bleeding, liver and kidney failure
A single dose of the live attenuated vaccine confers durable, likely lifelong immunity and is the cornerstone of prevention and international travel requirements
Diagnosis uses RT-PCR in early illness and serology (IgM), with care to distinguish from other flaviviruses and viral haemorrhagic fevers
# Additional differentials
Other viral haemorrhagic fevers (e.g. Ebola, Lassa, Marburg)
Severe malaria
Leptospirosis (Weil disease)
Viral hepatitis with fulminant course
Severe dengue
Rickettsial infection
Infection mimics: Fulminant viral hepatitis of other cause; Sepsis with disseminated intravascular coagulation; Other flaviviral illnesses; Drug-induced or toxic hepatic failure.
# Additional investigations
RT-PCR for yellow fever virus in early illness
Yellow fever IgM serology with confirmatory testing given flavivirus cross-reactivity
Liver function tests and coagulation profile to assess hepatic injury and coagulopathy
Renal function and urinalysis to detect kidney involvement
Concurrent testing to exclude malaria, leptospirosis and other haemorrhagic fevers
# Red flags
Entry into the toxic phase with jaundice, bleeding and organ dysfunction, a life-threatening emergency
Haemorrhagic manifestations with coagulopathy and shock
Acute kidney injury or fulminant hepatic failure
A febrile returning traveller from an endemic region, requiring isolation, notification and exclusion of other viral haemorrhagic fevers
# Pitfalls
Failing to elicit a travel history and consider yellow fever in a febrile traveller from an endemic area
Mistaking the early phase for uncomplicated dengue or influenza and missing progression to the toxic phase
Overlooking vaccination requirements and prevention counselling for travellers
Not instituting appropriate isolation and public-health notification pending exclusion of viral haemorrhagic fever
# Management
Provide supportive care as the mainstay, since there is no specific antiviral: fluid resuscitation, correction of coagulopathy and management of shock
Manage toxic-phase organ failure with supportive care of hepatic failure, transfusion of blood and clotting products for haemorrhage, and renal replacement therapy for acute kidney injury
Avoid aspirin and NSAIDs because of bleeding risk, and monitor for hypoglycaemia and secondary infection
Nurse under mosquito precautions during viraemia to prevent onward transmission
Prevent disease with yellow fever vaccination for travellers and residents of endemic areas and support vector control
Manage severe disease in a high-dependency or intensive care setting, escalating for shock, multi-organ failure and uncontrolled bleeding
Notify public health authorities and exclude co-existing malaria, leptospirosis and other haemorrhagic fevers
