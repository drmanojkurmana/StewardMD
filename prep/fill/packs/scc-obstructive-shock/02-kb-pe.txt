# Pulmonary embolism
Pulmonary embolism, Pulmonary / Vascular.
Red flags: Haemodynamic instability → consider thrombolysis.
# Investigations
CT pulmonary angiogram
D-dimer (if low probability)
ECG, troponin
# Pathophysiology
Virchow's triad of venous stasis, hypercoagulability, and endothelial injury, typically with an inflammatory trigger, recruits activated platelets that release microparticles and drive neutrophil extracellular trap formation, promoting thrombin generation; venous thrombi flourish in stasis, low oxygen tension, and proinflammatory gene upregulation. Detached lower-limb thrombi embolize through the vena cava and right heart to lodge in the pulmonary arteries (rarely paradoxically to the arterial circulation via a patent foramen ovale or atrial septal defect). The dominant gas-exchange defects are arterial hypoxemia and a widened alveolar-arterial oxygen gradient with increased dead space; additional mechanisms include raised pulmonary vascular resistance from obstruction and vasoconstrictors (e.g., serotonin), ventilation-perfusion mismatch remote from the embolus, reflex hyperventilation, increased airway resistance, and reduced compliance. Pulmonary-artery obstruction plus neurohumoral mediators raise pulmonary pressure and resistance; rising RV wall tension produces RV dilation, stretch and dysfunction (releasing BNP), septal bowing that impairs LV filling, and compression of the right coronary artery causing RV ischemia/microinfarction (releasing troponin), ultimately reducing LV output and systemic pressure, leading to circulatory collapse and death. Prothrombotic states include factor V Leiden, the prothrombin gene mutation, and deficiencies of antithrombin/protein C/protein S (rare); antiphospholipid syndrome is an acquired thrombophilia predisposing to both venous and arterial thrombosis.
# Prognosis
Low-risk PE (about 65-75% of cases) with normal blood pressure and normal RV carries an excellent prognosis; submassive (intermediate-risk) PE (~20-25%) has RV dysfunction despite normal pressure with raised cardiac biomarkers and a high risk of deterioration; massive (high-risk) PE (5-10%) presents with hypotension/shock and can lead to multisystem organ failure and death. A high-probability V/Q scan confers ~90% diagnostic certainty for PE. Roughly half of survivors report persistent dyspnea, fatigue, and reduced exercise capacity, and about one-quarter have persistent RV dysfunction on echo ('post-PE syndrome'); CTEPH is a less frequent late complication. Risk of recurrent VTE after stopping anticoagulation ranges from low (<3%/yr, e.g., major surgery/trauma) to intermediate (3-8%/yr) to high (>8%/yr, e.g., active cancer, antiphospholipid syndrome) (ESC 2019).
# Severity classification
PE is stratified by hemodynamics and RV status: massive (high-risk, 5-10%) with systemic hypotension and extensive thrombosis (>=half of the pulmonary vasculature), hallmarks dyspnea/syncope/hypotension/cyanosis and possible cardiogenic shock; submassive (intermediate-risk, ~20-25%) with RV dysfunction and raised troponin despite normal blood pressure; and low-risk (~65-75%) with normal pressure and normal RV. RV strain markers (troponin, BNP/NT-proBNP, echo RV dysfunction, CT RV enlargement with RV/LV diameter ratio >1) drive risk stratification.
# Clinical pearls
PE is the 'great masquerader' because its symptoms and signs are nonspecific; unexplained breathlessness is the most common symptom.
Suspect occult PE when a patient with congestive heart failure or pneumonia fails to improve as expected on standard treatment.
Pleuritic chest pain typically reflects a small, peripherally lodged embolus near pleural nerves, whereas massive PE can cause severe substernal pain mimicking MI from pulmonary-artery distention and RV strain.
Pulmonary infarction usually signals a small (peripheral) PE and is exquisitely painful.
May-Thurner syndrome (left iliac vein compressed by the right iliac artery) should be considered in young women with recurrent left thigh swelling and associated DVT.
A diffusely edematous leg makes ordinary DVT unlikely, with phlegmasia cerulea dolens being the exception.
Many patients with PE have no demonstrable DVT because the leg thrombus has already embolized to the lungs.
McConnell's sign on echocardiography (RV free-wall hypokinesis with preserved/hyperkinetic RV apex) is the best-known indirect echo sign of PE.

# Infection mimics
Pneumonia
Cellulitis (mimics DVT; fever and chills favor cellulitis over DVT)
Sepsis (elevates D-dimer and can confound)
# Non infectious mimics
Acute coronary syndrome (massive PE can give substernal pain mimicking MI)
Aortic dissection
Pericardial tamponade
Pneumothorax / rib fracture
Asthma / COPD exacerbation
Ruptured Baker's cyst, muscle strain, and postthrombotic syndrome/venous insufficiency (mimic DVT)
# Additional investigations
Age-adjusted D-dimer (age x 10 ng/mL upper limit) for patients >50 years with low/intermediate clinical probability of PE; does not apply to suspected acute DVT
Wells point-score clinical decision rule to stratify pretest probability (validated in ambulatory, not hospitalized, patients); low/low-to-moderate probability with normal D-dimer can avoid imaging
BNP / NT-proBNP as markers of RV myocardial stretch
ECG looking for S1Q3T3 (specific but insensitive) and T-wave inversion in V1-V4 from RV strain
Chest x-ray signs: focal oligemia (Westermark's sign), peripheral pleural-based density (Hampton's hump, from infarction), enlarged right descending pulmonary artery (Palla's sign); often normal
Echocardiography to assess RV dysfunction and to detect mimics (MI, tamponade, aortic dissection); usually normal in PE so not a reliable rule-out
Compression venous ultrasonography for DVT (loss of vein compressibility)
Ventilation/perfusion lung scan as second-line when IV contrast cannot be given
MR pulmonary angiography / MR venography when CT or ultrasound is unsuitable
RV-to-LV diameter ratio >1 on chest CT as a marker of RV enlargement
# Red flags
Systemic arterial hypotension / cardiogenic shock indicating massive (high-risk) PE
Syncope or cyanosis
RV dysfunction on echocardiography or RV enlargement on CT despite normal blood pressure (submassive/intermediate-risk PE)
Elevated troponin (from RV microinfarction) signaling high risk of clinical deterioration
Phlegmasia cerulea dolens (diffusely swollen, threatened limb)
# Pitfalls
D-dimer is sensitive (>95%) but not specific; it rises with MI, pneumonia, sepsis, cancer, postoperative state, and the 2nd/3rd trimester of pregnancy, so it rarely helps in hospitalized patients.
Wells criteria are validated in ambulatory patients, not in hospitalized patients.
A normal or near-normal chest x-ray and a normal ECG (or only sinus tachycardia) do not exclude PE.
Echocardiography is not a reliable rule-out test because most PE patients have normal echocardiograms.
Not all leg pain is DVT and not all dyspnea is PE; consider ruptured Baker's cyst, cellulitis, and muscle injury.
# Pulmonary embolism: treatment
Pleuritic pain/dyspnoea/tachycardia ± leg DVT; risk-stratify (Wells), D-dimer if low probability, CTPA to confirm, Admit; ICU if haemodynamically unstable.
Anticoagulation (DOAC or LMWH) on confirmed/high-probability PE
# Steps
Start anticoagulation on clinical suspicion if high probability (LMWH/DOAC).
Massive PE (shock): thrombolysis (or embolectomy) and resuscitation.
Confirm with CTPA (or V/Q); echo for RV strain in unstable patients.
Continue DOAC/LMWH→oral; duration ≥3 months (longer if unprovoked/recurrent).
Oxygen, analgesia; assess for cancer/thrombophilia if unprovoked.
Reperfusion / parenteral therapy for high-risk (massive) PE
Anticoagulate; thrombolyse if high-risk
