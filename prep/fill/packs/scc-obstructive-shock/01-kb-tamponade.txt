# Cardiac tamponade
Cardiac tamponade, Cardiology / Emergency.
Red flags: Obstructive shock — urgent pericardiocentesis.
# Investigations
Urgent echocardiogram
ECG (electrical alternans)
# Pathophysiology
Pericardial fluid accumulates between the visceral (epicardial) and parietal pericardial layers. Tamponade develops when intrapericardial pressure rises enough to exceed the filling pressures of the cardiac chambers, generally the right ventricle, impairing diastolic filling. Because the relatively non-distensible pericardial sac determines tolerance, the rate of fluid accumulation is more important than absolute volume: slow accumulation allows large effusions without compromise, while rapid accumulation (trauma, cardiac rupture, procedural perforation, aortic dissection into the pericardium) causes tamponade at small volumes. Inspiratory augmentation of right-heart filling at the expense of the left, mediated by exaggerated interventricular dependence within the fixed pericardial space, produces pulsus paradoxus.
# Prognosis
Outcome depends heavily on cause and timeliness of drainage. Untreated tuberculous pericarditis is usually fatal and even treated TB pericarditis carries case-fatality rates up to ~40% in some series, with risk of progression to constrictive pericarditis. Cancer patients whose pericardial fluid contains malignant cells on cytology have very poor survival. After percutaneous catheter drainage of malignant effusions the recurrence rate is ~20%, and a subset develop paradoxical hemodynamic worsening after drainage (post-drainage low cardiac output syndrome).
# Clinical pearls
It is the balance between intrapericardial pressure and ventricular (especially right ventricular) filling pressure that drives compression, not the absolute volume of fluid.
Rate of accumulation matters more than total volume: slowly forming effusions (e.g. malignant) can become large without classic tamponade physiology, whereas rapid accumulation (e.g. cardiac rupture, perforation, aortic dissection into the pericardium) can cause tamponade with only a small effusion.
Pulsus paradoxus is an inspiratory fall in systolic pressure of more than 10 mmHg and represents an exaggerated form of normal interventricular dependence.
Measure pulsus paradoxus by deflating the cuff slowly and noting the gap between the pressure where Korotkoff sounds are first heard (only in expiration) and the pressure where they are heard with every beat regardless of respiration; a difference >15 mmHg may even be palpable at the brachial or femoral pulse.
A pulsus paradoxus exceeding 12 mmHg in a patient with a large pericardial effusion is highly predictive of tamponade (reported sensitivity ~98%, specificity ~83%).
The ECG triad of sinus tachycardia, low QRS voltage, and total electrical alternans (beat-to-beat P-QRS-T alternation) strongly suggests pericardial effusion with tamponade physiology, though it is insensitive.
Total electrical alternans (whole-complex) is relatively specific for effusion/tamponade; in contrast, pure repolarization (ST-T/U-wave) alternans signals electrical instability and risk of ventricular arrhythmia, not tamponade.
Echocardiography is the initial test of choice for suspected effusion/tamponade and can usually be done rapidly at the bedside.
# Additional differentials
Constrictive pericarditis (also impairs cardiac filling via a thick, noncompliant pericardium; on the differential for dyspnea)
Acute right ventricular dysfunction (can mimic the elevated JVP / characteristic jugular waveform pattern)
# Infection mimics
Sepsis (can produce a paradoxical-type arterial waveform)
# Non infectious mimics
Massive pulmonary embolism (shares hypotension/tachycardia and pulsus paradoxus)
Tension pneumothorax (life-threatening cause of obstructive shock and pulsus paradoxus)
Hemorrhagic shock (cause of pulsus paradoxus)
Severe obstructive lung disease / asthma (cause of pulsus paradoxus)
Acute MI with cardiogenic shock (hypotension + tachycardia in chest-pain patient)
Acute aortic dissection (may extend into the pericardium and itself cause tamponade)
# Additional investigations
Pericardiocentesis under echocardiographic guidance, with pericardial fluid sent for biochemical, cytologic, and microbiologic analysis
Pericardial fluid cytology when malignancy is suspected (diagnostic in most malignant cases; fluid may be serous, serosanguineous, or hemorrhagic)
Pericardial/epicardial biopsy and pericardioscopy to distinguish neoplastic from benign disease
Chest CT, CMR, or echocardiography to assess effusion size and pericardial thickness
In suspected tuberculous etiology, pericardial fluid adenosine deaminase, lysozyme, and IFN-gamma, plus culture/biopsy (fluid is exudative, lymphocyte/monocyte-rich, often hemorrhagic)
# Red flags
Hypotension with tachycardia in a chest-pain patient mandates rapid evaluation for tamponade as one of the immediately life-threatening causes
Life-threatening hemodynamic instability with acute tamponade requires immediate fluid drainage
Hemorrhagic tamponade from cardiac rupture, procedural perforation, or aortic dissection into the pericardium can be catastrophic at small effusion volumes
# Pitfalls
Do not delay decompression in a hemodynamically unstable patient waiting for confirmatory tests; tamponade is a clinical/emergency diagnosis and instability requires immediate drainage.
Failing to appreciate the urgency of the situation is a recognized diagnostic error for tamponade (listed among can't-miss time-critical diagnoses).
Pulsus paradoxus can be hard to measure or unreliable in tachycardia, atrial fibrillation, or tachypnea.
A large slowly-accumulating effusion may lack classic tamponade signs, while a small rapidly-accumulating effusion may already be causing tamponade; judging severity by volume alone is misleading.
In malignant tamponade, relatively specific signs (paradoxical pulse, diminished heart sounds, pulsus alternans, friction rub) are LESS common than in nonmalignant disease, so their absence does not exclude tamponade.
ECG and chest radiograph are abnormal in ~90% of malignant pericardial cases but about half of those abnormalities are nonspecific.
# Confirm
Hypotension, raised JVP, muffled heart sounds (Beck's triad) with pulsus paradoxus; echo shows effusion with diastolic collapse.
ICU + pericardiocentesis.
Emergency pericardiocentesis (fluids as a bridge)
# Steps
EMERGENCY — urgent echo; cardiology/cardiothoracic input.
Echo-guided pericardiocentesis (or surgical drainage) to relieve tamponade.
IV fluids as a temporising measure to maintain filling.
Avoid positive-pressure ventilation if possible until drained.
Treat and investigate the underlying cause; send pericardial fluid for analysis.
Emergency pericardiocentesis
