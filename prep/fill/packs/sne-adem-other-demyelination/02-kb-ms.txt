# Multiple sclerosis (relapse)
Multiple sclerosis (relapse), Neurology.
Red flags: Exclude infection before high-dose steroids.
# Investigations
MRI brain/cord with contrast
CSF oligoclonal bands
Evoked potentials
# Pathophysiology
MS is an autoimmune CNS disease producing chronic inflammation, demyelination, gliosis (sclerotic plaques) and neuronal/axonal loss. New lesions begin with perivenular cuffing by T cells and macrophages and blood-brain-barrier (BBB) disruption with a preserved vessel wall (unlike vasculitis); cytotoxic CD8 cells line the leading edge, B cells infiltrate, myelin-specific autoantibodies coat degenerating myelin, and complement is activated. Sharply demarcated demyelination is the hallmark; axons are relatively spared early but can be destroyed in highly inflammatory lesions, and partial remyelination produces 'shadow plaques'. Cumulative axonal/neuronal loss drives irreversible disability (up to ~70% of corticospinal axons lost in MS paraplegia) via demyelination-related loss of trophic support, ion-channel redistribution, and dying-back axonopathy. Progressive MS features more diffuse microglial activation, chronic active (slowly expanding) plaques with microglia at a leading edge behind an intact BBB, cortical/subpial plaques poorly seen on MRI, and meningeal ectopic lymphoid follicles linked to subpial cortical demyelination. Physiologically, demyelination causes conduction block (hyperpolarization from exposed potassium channels) and conduction slowing; temperature and metabolic changes worsen block, explaining heat- and fever-related fluctuations. Susceptibility is polygenic with the strongest signal at HLA-DRB1*1501 (~10% of risk), and EBV seroconversion appears a near-universal prerequisite, plausibly via molecular mimicry between EBV peptides and myelin autoantigens.
# Prognosis
The clinical course is highly variable. RRMS accounts for ~90% of cases with attacks evolving over days to weeks and substantial early recovery; PPMS (~10%) declines steadily from onset, begins later (mean ~40 years), has a more even sex ratio, and accrues disability faster. In the pretreatment era, by 15 years after onset only ~20% had no functional limitation and one-third to one-half of relapsing patients had progressed to SPMS needing ambulatory assistance, with ~80% reaching that level by 25 years; the prior RRMS-to-SPMS conversion rate of ~3%/year has fallen to <1%/year in the high-efficacy-therapy era. Favorable prognostic features (pretreatment data): optic neuritis or sensory onset, fewer than two relapses in the first year, and minimal impairment at 5 years; adverse predictors: older onset age, male sex, greater disability, and motor signs in the first year. About <10% have a benign variant that never produces disability even untreated. Pregnancy: fewer attacks during gestation (especially the third trimester) but more in the first 3 months postpartum, with the overall pregnancy-year course unchanged.
# Severity classification
Course-based subtypes: relapsing-remitting MS (RRMS, ~90%), secondary progressive MS (SPMS, always evolves from RRMS), and primary progressive MS (PPMS, ~10%); 'relapsing MS (RMS)' covers all relapsing patients and 'active progressive MS' denotes SPMS/PPMS with ongoing relapses or new MRI lesions. Neurologic impairment is graded with the Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS), 0 (normal) to 10 (death), built from Functional Status (FS) subscores; EDSS <3.5 generally indicates normal walking and little disability, whereas EDSS >4.0 indicates gait-impaired progressive (SPMS/PPMS) disease. An EDSS >=4 plus a motor FS score >=2 supports an SPMS diagnosis. Fulminant clinical variants include Marburg's (acute fulminant) MS, tumefactive MS, and Balo's concentric sclerosis.
# Clinical pearls
MS is defined by lesions disseminated in both time and space: plaques arise at different times and in anatomically separate CNS sites.
Typical onset is age 20-40 (slightly later in men); women are affected roughly three times as often as men.
The most common presenting symptoms are sensory loss (~37%) and optic neuritis (~36%), followed by limb weakness (~35%) and paresthesias.
Optic neuritis usually causes monocular reduced acuity, dimness, and color desaturation, with periorbital pain worsened by eye movement and an afferent pupillary defect; uveitis is uncommon and should prompt thought of sarcoidosis or lymphoma.
A bilateral internuclear ophthalmoplegia (impaired adduction with nystagmus of the abducting eye, from a medial longitudinal fasciculus lesion) is particularly suggestive of MS; related gaze findings include horizontal gaze palsy and the 'one-and-a-half' syndrome.
Lhermitte's symptom is a neck-flexion-induced electric-shock sensation radiating down the spine/legs; it is not specific to MS and can occur with other cervical cord disease such as spondylosis.
Uhthoff's symptom (heat sensitivity) is transient worsening of neurologic symptoms with elevated core temperature (hot shower, exercise, fever) and reflects temperature-dependent conduction block rather than a true new lesion.
Exercise-induced weakness is characteristic; weakness is of upper-motor-neuron type with spasticity, hyperreflexia, and extensor plantar responses, though a tendon reflex can occasionally be lost if a plaque disrupts the afferent reflex arc.
Paroxysmal symptoms (tonic spasms, paroxysmal dysarthria/ataxia, sensory bursts) are brief (10 s-2 min), frequent (5-40/day), without altered consciousness, and self-limited; they arise from spontaneous discharges at the edges of demyelinated plaques.
Trigeminal neuralgia, hemifacial spasm, or glossopharyngeal neuralgia in a young patient, or with bilateral/atypical features or objective sensory loss, should raise suspicion of MS involving the cranial nerve root entry zone.
Fatigue is experienced by most patients and is the leading cause of work-related disability in MS; depression affects about half of patients.
On MRI, MS plaques are oriented perpendicular to the ventricular surface (Dawson's fingers) reflecting perivenous demyelination; lesions >6 mm in the corpus callosum, periventricular white matter, brainstem, cerebellum, or cord are especially helpful diagnostically.
A central vein sign within plaques on susceptibility-weighted (T2*) imaging supports an MS diagnosis.
Two or more discrete CSF oligoclonal bands not present in paired serum are found in >90% of MS patients; bands may be absent at onset and increase over time.
Evoked potential abnormalities occur in 80-90% of MS patients; a marked latency delay (rather than reduced amplitude or distorted waveform) is what suggests demyelination, and EPs are most informative when testing a clinically uninvolved pathway.
Most MRI-visible lesions and most early disease activity in MS are clinically silent—bursts of focal inflammation on serial MRI far exceed the relapse rate.
Even relapse-free RRMS patients undergo subtle 'silent progression,' and disability accumulation is now attributed mainly to progression independent of relapse activity (PIRA) rather than incomplete recovery from relapses (RAW), across RRMS, SPMS, and PPMS.
A radiologically isolated syndrome (RIS) is incidental MRI evidence of MS in an asymptomatic person; clinically isolated syndrome (CIS) is a first demyelinating event at high risk for MS.

# Non infectious mimics
NMOSD (anti-AQP4) and MOGAD - consider with bilateral/severe optic neuritis or severe transverse myelitis
ADEM - consider with hyperacute or postinfectious presentation
Tumefactive demyelination mimicking a primary or metastatic brain tumor
Sarcoidosis, Sjogren's syndrome, SLE, antiphospholipid syndrome, vasculitis
Vitamin B12 deficiency, hereditary leukodystrophies, CADASIL, MELAS
# Additional investigations
CSF analysis for intrathecal IgG synthesis: CSF IgG index and IgG synthesis rate, plus oligoclonal bands by agarose-gel electrophoresis (>90% positive in MS)
CSF cell count/profile: a mild mononuclear pleocytosis (>5 cells/uL) occurs in ~25% (young relapsing patients); pleocytosis >75 cells/uL, polymorphonuclear cells, or protein >1 g/L argues against MS
Gadolinium-enhanced MRI to detect active (BBB-disrupted) lesions; enhancement typically lasts <1 month
Susceptibility-weighted (T2*) MRI to look for the central vein sign
Quantitative brain and spinal cord atrophy / T2 lesion burden (correlates with disability; cord atrophy correlates more strongly)
T1 imaging for 'black holes' (markers of irreversible demyelination and axonal loss)
Visual, somatosensory, brainstem-auditory, and motor evoked potentials
Workup to exclude mimics in suspected MS: ESR, serum B12, antinuclear antibodies, and treponemal antibody
# Red flags
Distinguish a true relapse from a pseudoexacerbation driven by raised ambient temperature, fever, or infection - glucocorticoids are inappropriate for a pseudoexacerbation.
Features atypical for MS that should prompt an alternative diagnosis: symptoms confined to posterior fossa/craniocervical junction/spinal cord; onset <15 or >60 years; progressive course from onset; never any visual, sensory, or bladder symptoms; or atypical MRI/CSF/EP findings.
Symptoms rare/uncommon in MS that favor another diagnosis: aphasia, parkinsonism, chorea, isolated dementia, severe muscle atrophy, peripheral neuropathy, episodic loss of consciousness, fever, headache, seizures, or coma.
Bilateral and/or severe optic neuritis or severe transverse myelitis should raise NMOSD, MOGAD, or GFAP autoimmunity rather than MS.
Uveitis is uncommon in MS and should suggest sarcoidosis or lymphoma.
Intense inflammatory swelling can produce a mass lesion mimicking tumor (tumefactive MS), sometimes requiring brain biopsy.
# Pitfalls
Do not over-diagnose MS from a single isolated event or from MRI white-matter lesions alone - the diagnosis requires dissemination in space and time (clinical, MRI, or CSF evidence) per the McDonald criteria, and there is no single confirmatory test.
A pseudoexacerbation from heat/fever/infection (Uhthoff phenomenon) can mimic a relapse but reflects transient conduction block, not a new lesion; treating it as a relapse with steroids is inappropriate.
Failing to distinguish NMOSD, MOGAD, and GFAP autoimmunity from MS - clues are bilateral/severe optic neuritis, severe longitudinally extensive transverse myelitis (>=3 vertebral segments), brain MRI lesions that are non-destructive/cloud-like and can fully resolve, and CSF pleocytosis greater than in MS with neutrophils/eosinophils and oligoclonal bands in <20%.
ADEM should be considered for hyperacute or postinfectious demyelinating presentations rather than assuming a first MS attack.
MS-associated genetic variants (including HLA-DRB1*1501) lack the sensitivity and specificity to be used for diagnosis.
Highly restricted CNS oligoclonal antibodies are characteristic of MS but their specific antigenic targets differ between patients and their pathogenic role is uncertain - they aid diagnosis, not mechanistic certainty.
