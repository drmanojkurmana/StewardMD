# Tumefactive demyelination
Tumefactive demyelination, Nervous system / Neuroimmunology, Neurology / Neuroimmunology.
Aliases: tumefactive multiple sclerosis; tumefactive demyelinating lesion; demyelinating pseudotumour; tumour-like demyelination.
# Pathophysiology
Tumefactive demyelination is a focal region of intense inflammatory demyelination large enough to produce a space-occupying appearance. Macrophage-rich inflammation strips myelin while relatively sparing axons, and the advancing inflammatory edge produces the characteristic open-ring pattern of contrast enhancement. Because the pathology is inflammatory rather than neoplastic, restricted diffusion at the rim, relative lack of mass effect for size, and the open-ring sign help distinguish it from tumour, although overlap with the multiple sclerosis spectrum and with Balo and Marburg variants exists.
# Prognosis
Many patients recover well with corticosteroids, and lesions typically shrink over time; long-term outcome depends on whether the disorder declares itself as multiple sclerosis or remains a solitary event.
# Clinical pearls
A large (typically over 2 cm) demyelinating lesion that mimics a brain tumour clinically and radiologically
Presents with focal deficits, headache, cognitive change, or seizures depending on location and mass effect
Characteristic MRI features include an incomplete (open) ring of enhancement with the open edge facing the cortex or grey matter
Mass effect and surrounding oedema are usually less than expected for the lesion size, a helpful clue against neoplasm
May occur in isolation or as a presentation of multiple sclerosis, and can be triggered in the context of certain disease-modifying drugs
Corticosteroids are first-line, with plasma exchange for steroid-refractory disease
Biopsy is sometimes needed but carries the risk of misinterpretation, as active demyelination can resemble neoplasm histologically
Additional differentials: High-grade glioma; Primary central nervous system lymphoma; Cerebral abscess; Metastasis; Balo concentric sclerosis; Progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy; Neurosarcoidosis.
# Additional investigations
MRI of the brain with contrast, diffusion, perfusion, and spectroscopy to seek the open-ring sign and features favouring demyelination
MRI of the spinal cord and orbits to look for additional demyelinating lesions
Cerebrospinal fluid analysis including oligoclonal bands
Aquaporin-4 and MOG antibody testing
Brain biopsy in genuinely indeterminate cases
Serial imaging to demonstrate the expected evolution of a demyelinating lesion
# Red flags
Progressive mass effect with raised intracranial pressure
Rapid neurological deterioration
Imaging that cannot confidently exclude malignancy or infection
Steroid-refractory progression requiring escalation
# Pitfalls
Proceeding to resection or radiotherapy on the assumption of a tumour
Misreading a demyelinating biopsy as glioma
Overlooking associated demyelinating lesions elsewhere
Failing to consider a drug-associated trigger in treated multiple sclerosis
# Management
High-dose corticosteroids as first-line treatment of the acute demyelinating lesion
Plasma exchange for steroid-refractory disease
Careful exclusion of tumour and infection to avoid unnecessary resection, with brain biopsy reserved for genuinely indeterminate cases
Review of any disease-modifying drug that may have precipitated the lesion
Initiation of long-term disease-modifying therapy if the disorder declares itself as multiple sclerosis
Supportive management of mass effect and oedema, with symptomatic treatment of seizures or focal deficits and neurorehabilitation
Serial imaging follow-up to confirm the expected evolution, with neurology and neuroimmunology referral
