# Sarcoidosis
Sarcoidosis, Pulmonary / Multisystem.
Red flags: Exclude TB/lymphoma before steroids.
# Investigations
CXR (bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy)
Serum ACE, calcium
Biopsy (non-caseating granuloma)
# Pathophysiology
Sarcoidosis is thought to arise from a heightened, antigen-driven host immune response to an undetermined (likely inhaled bioaerosol or inorganic) environmental antigen in a genetically susceptible person; the inciting antigen may differ between individuals/populations. A poorly degradable material is presented to lymphocytes by dendritic cells/macrophages via MHC, triggering an innate cytokine/chemokine cascade and recruitment of adaptive immune cells. The immunopathologic hallmark is highly polarized CD4+ TH1 lymphocytes (less so CD8+) producing IFN-gamma, with upregulation of TH1-promoting cytokines/chemokines (IL-1, IL-2, IL-15, IL-18, CXCR3) that activate macrophages and drive formation of tightly organized nonnecrotizing (noncaseating) granulomas. Altered regulatory T-cell and TH17 responses are implicated in failure to resolve and in progression to fibrosis. Granulomas comprise macrophages, epithelioid cells, and multinucleated giant cells rimmed by lymphocytes/fibroblasts (CD4+ central, CD8+ and B cells peripheral). Mycobacterial proteins (mKatG, ESAT-6, superoxide dismutase A, antigen 85A, heat-shock proteins) and Cutibacterium acnes have been disproportionately implicated; the transmissibility seen with the Kveim-Siltzbach reagent and via organ transplantation supports an antigenic trigger.
# Prognosis
Disease resolves spontaneously in just over half of patients, but 30-40% develop chronic disease threatening vital organs or causing debilitating symptoms. Most patients show radiographic or spirometric improvement by 2 years (treated or untreated). Death attributable to sarcoidosis occurs in roughly 1-5%, most often from progressive respiratory insufficiency/pulmonary fibrosis (leading cause in the US/Northern Europe) or cardiac involvement (predominant cause of death in Japan). A robust acute initial immune response predicts a better outcome: Löfgren and Heerfordt syndromes remit spontaneously in up to ~80%, and immunosuppression is rarely needed. Adverse prognostic factors include lupus pernio, chronic uveitis, older age, chronic hypercalcemia, nephrocalcinosis, African ancestry, pulmonary hypertension, fibrotic pulmonary disease, sinus involvement, cystic bone lesions, neurosarcoidosis, and cardiac involvement. Relapses are common and associated with shorter treatment duration.
# Severity classification
Scadding chest x-ray stages describe the pulmonary pattern and correlate with spontaneous resolution rates (NOT with activity or prognosis in the individual): Stage 1 = bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy without parenchymal involvement (resolution ~55-90%); Stage 2 = bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy WITH parenchymal involvement (resolution ~40-70%); Stage 3 = parenchymal involvement without lymphadenopathy (resolution ~10-20%); Stage 4 = pulmonary fibrosis with architectural distortion and volume loss (resolution ~0%).
# Clinical pearls
Sarcoidosis is a systemic granulomatous disease of unknown cause; the lung is involved in over 90% of cases, but roughly half of patients have extrapulmonary disease and almost any organ can be affected.
Diagnosis rests on three criteria: a compatible clinical picture, nonnecrotizing granulomatous inflammation on tissue, AND exclusion of other causes of granulomas — it is never made with absolute certainty, so new signs during follow-up should be re-evaluated.
Löfgren syndrome (fever, erythema nodosum, ankle arthralgias, bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy) occurs in up to ~10% of cases and, like Heerfordt syndrome, carries an excellent prognosis with frequent spontaneous resolution.
Heerfordt syndrome = facial nerve palsy, fever, anterior uveitis, and bilateral parotid gland enlargement.
In very compelling presentations (classic Löfgren, Heerfordt, lupus pernio, or asymptomatic symmetrical bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy), biopsy may be deferred, but close clinical follow-up is still required.
A BAL CD4:CD8 lymphocyte ratio >3.5 supports the diagnosis when combined with consistent clinical/radiographic findings, but a normal ratio does NOT exclude sarcoidosis; BAL is also important to exclude infection.
Endobronchial ultrasound-guided lymph node sampling has high diagnostic yield with low procedural risk when hilar/mediastinal nodes are the prominent feature.
Sarcoidosis is upper-lobe predominant on imaging with a lymphangitic (perilymphatic/subpleural/peribronchovascular) nodular distribution; airflow obstruction can occur in up to one-third of patients despite the classically restrictive pattern.
Every sarcoidosis patient should be screened for cardiac involvement (baseline ECG) because severity of lung disease does not predict cardiac sarcoidosis and sudden death can be the initial presentation.
Baseline workup in every patient should include PFTs, chest imaging, an ophthalmologic exam (to detect clinically silent uveitis), an ECG, and serum creatinine/alkaline phosphatase/calcium/CBC.
Hypercalcemia from dysregulated macrophage/granuloma production of calcitriol (1,25-(OH)2 vitamin D) occurs despite suppressed PTH; patients should limit vitamin D intake, avoid excess sunlight, and stay hydrated.
Optic neuropathy in ocular sarcoidosis is an emergency requiring immediate immunosuppression to prevent sudden permanent vision loss.
Smoking is a consistent NEGATIVE predictor of incident sarcoidosis (reduced odds), an unusual feature among lung diseases.

# Additional investigations
Pulmonary function tests at baseline and serially to track impairment/progression
Baseline ophthalmologic examination to detect clinically silent uveitis
Baseline ECG in every patient to screen for conduction block, ST-T changes, Q waves, frequent PVCs, or resting tachycardia
Chest CT to define disease extent and guide biopsy (not required in all patients)
Bronchoscopy with endobronchial ultrasound-guided lymph node sampling and/or transbronchial biopsy when hilar/mediastinal nodes or lung infiltrates predominate
BAL with lymphocyte subset analysis (CD4:CD8 ratio >3.5 is supportive) and to exclude infection
18F-FDG PET-CT to localize active inflammation and guide biopsy in difficult cases
Serum creatinine, alkaline phosphatase, complete blood count, and liver enzymes
25- and 1,25-hydroxyvitamin D levels and urinary calcium (hypercalciuria up to ~50%)
Cardiac MRI with gadolinium (preferred) or cardiac PET-CT for suspected cardiac sarcoidosis; Holter/signal-averaged ECG/echocardiography as indicated
Gadolinium-enhanced brain/spinal MRI plus CSF analysis (mononuclear pleocytosis, raised protein; CSF ACE/sIL-2R/lysozyme are supportive but not diagnostic) for suspected neurosarcoidosis
Echocardiography to screen for pulmonary hypertension; right heart catheterization is the gold standard
Soluble IL-2 receptor and serum ACE as adjuncts to monitor disease activity/burden (not diagnostic)
# Red flags
Exclude infection (especially TB and fungi such as histoplasmosis) before starting immunosuppression — abundant necrosis on biopsy points away from sarcoidosis toward infection
Acute symptomatic hypercalcemia can require hospital admission and urgent corticosteroids
Optic neuropathy threatens sudden permanent vision loss and is an ophthalmologic emergency
Cardiac involvement (new-onset AV block, ventricular arrhythmia, cardiomyopathy in younger patients) carries risk of sudden death and can be the first presentation
Neurologic involvement (basal/cranial nerve disease, mass lesions, meningitis) can cause neurologic demise
Untreated hypercalcemia leads to nephrocalcinosis and renal failure
New reproductive-organ nodules/masses require exclusion of malignancy given the rarity of genital sarcoidosis
# Pitfalls
A normal BAL CD4:CD8 ratio, normal PFTs, or a normal lung exam does NOT exclude sarcoidosis despite active granulomatous inflammation.
Serum ACE has low sensitivity and is not a reliable diagnostic or prognostic test; it only roughly tracks granuloma burden and is best used to monitor activity.
Scadding (CXR) stages reveal overall resolution rates but do NOT predict disease activity, lung-function progression, or extrapulmonary involvement in an individual.
Cardiac involvement is frequently subclinical and endomyocardial biopsy has poor sensitivity (patchy granulomas, basal predominance, sparing of the right ventricle), so a negative biopsy does not exclude cardiac sarcoidosis.
Failing to screen for cardiac, ocular, and neurologic disease at baseline misses life- or sight-threatening involvement because pulmonary severity does not predict it.
Isolated extrapulmonary sarcoidosis (cardiac, hepatic, neurologic) can occur without lung disease and is easily overlooked.
Loosely formed granulomas suggest hypersensitivity pneumonitis and abundant necrosis suggests infection — misreading these as sarcoidosis is a classic error.
Always re-evaluate alternative causes for isolated hepatic granulomas, which carry a broad differential.
