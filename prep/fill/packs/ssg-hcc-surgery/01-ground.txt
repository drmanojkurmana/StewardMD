The Barcelona Clinic scheme stages hepatocellular carcinoma from 0 and A through D. Stages 0 and A with single tumours under 5 cm or 3 nodules under 3 cm suit curative resection or ablation, while portal vein tumour thrombus marks stage C disease needing systemic therapy rather than surgery.

Imaging can diagnose without biopsy. Alpha-fetoprotein over 400 ng per mL with arterial hyperenhancement plus portal washout on multiphasic computed tomography diagnoses HCC in cirrhosis, sparing the bleeding risk of needle biopsy.

Transplant cures tumour and liver together. The Milan criteria of a single tumour up to 5 cm or 3 nodules up to 3 cm without vascular invasion select candidates with 4-year survival over 70 percent, and living donation or extended criteria stretch access where deceased organs are scarce.

Small tumours burn well. Radiofrequency ablation cures lesions under 3 cm with 1 cm ablative margins, while microwave ablation suits perivascular lesions because of less heat-sink effect from adjacent flowing blood.

Intermediate disease embolises. Transarterial chemoembolisation with doxorubicin in oil suits multinodular disease without vascular invasion, repeated on demand by mRECIST response criteria on follow-up imaging.

Advanced disease now lives longer. Atezolizumab with bevacizumab is first-line systemic therapy for advanced HCC with preserved Child-Pugh A liver function after variceal screening, beating sorafenib in the IMBRAVE150 trial on survival.

Variants behave differently. Fibrolamellar HCC in young noncirrhotic livers shows a central scar with calcification, and aggressive resection with nodal clearance gives 5-year survival near 70 percent without underlying liver disease.

Rupture bleeds first, operates later. Ruptured HCC with haemoperitoneum needs transarterial embolisation first for haemostasis, then staged hepatectomy after 4 to 6 weeks once the patient stabilises and the liver recovers.
