# Pulmonary Tuberculosis
Pulmonary Tuberculosis, Respiratory / Tuberculosis.
# Pathogens
The clinical syndrome of prolonged cough with constitutional symptoms (weight loss, evening fever, night sweats) in an endemic country is classically tuberculous, but microbiological confirmation remains essential since the clinical picture alone is not specific enough to justify treatment, which carries real toxicity and public health implications (resistance, contact tracing).
Very likely: Mycobacterium tuberculosis.
# Possible
Non-tuberculous mycobacteria (if AFB smear positive but culture/PCR does not confirm M. tuberculosis)
# Investigations
Sputum smear for AFB (at least 2 samples)
Rapid, widely available test; positive smear supports diagnosis and indicates infectiousness for public health purposes.
CBNAAT / Xpert MTB-RIF (or equivalent rapid molecular test)
Provides faster diagnosis than culture and simultaneously detects rifampicin resistance, directly informing regimen choice — should be prioritized where available.
Sputum culture (liquid or solid media)
Gold standard for diagnosis and allows full drug-susceptibility testing if initial molecular testing suggests resistance or treatment failure occurs.
Chest X-ray, Supports diagnosis (upper lobe infiltrates, cavitation) and provides a baseline for treatment response monitoring.
HIV testing
TB-HIV co-infection changes management (ART timing, drug interactions) and is a standard part of TB workup per national guidelines.

# Pathophysiology
M. tuberculosis is an aerobic acid-fast bacillus whose mycolic-acid-rich cell wall confers acid fastness and low permeability (reducing antibiotic efficacy). Transmission is by airborne droplet nuclei (<5-10 um) from a person with infectious pulmonary or laryngeal TB; cavitary cases are most infectious (10^5-10^7 AFB/mL of sputum). Inhaled bacilli reaching the alveoli are phagocytosed by alveolar macrophages; the bacillus arrests phagosome maturation and phagosome-lysosome fusion, replicates, and (via the ESX-1/RD1 secretion system) ruptures the macrophage to infect newly recruited phagocytes. Around 2-4 weeks, cell-mediated (TH1/IFN-gamma-driven, CD4+ T-cell-dependent) macrophage activation and a delayed-type hypersensitivity tissue-damaging response develop; their balance determines disease form. Granulomas (tubercles) of activated macrophages, epithelioid and giant cells form, with central caseous (cheese-like) necrosis in which low oxygen and pH inhibit but do not kill bacilli. When the macrophage-activating response is weak, delayed hypersensitivity causes progressive caseous liquefaction, bronchial and vascular erosion, and cavity formation; bacilli multiply within cavities and are discharged into airways, driving transmission and bronchogenic spread of satellite lesions. Lymphohematogenous dissemination can seed distant organs (miliary disease, meningitis). The continuum from infection to disease is governed largely by host immune competence; only ~10% of immunocompetent infected persons progress to active disease over a lifetime, about half within ~18 months.
# Prognosis
Untreated, TB is often fatal: about one-third of patients die within 1 year of diagnosis, and historically ~70% (weighted mean) of smear-positive cases died within 10 years; paucibacillary smear-negative disease had a lower ~20% case-fatality at 5 years, and ~60% of 5-year survivors underwent spontaneous remission. With timely, proper drug-susceptible treatment the vast majority are cured. Improper therapy lowers mortality but generates chronic infectious, often drug-resistant cases. A substantial proportion of cured survivors suffer post-TB lung disease - chronic airflow impairment, dyspnea, residual cough, bronchiectasis, pulmonary hypertension, and predisposition to aspergilloma/chronic pulmonary aspergillosis in residual cavities.
# Severity classification
Infectiousness grades with cavitation and sputum bacillary load (smear-positive cavitary > smear-negative/culture-positive > culture-negative/extrapulmonary, the last essentially noninfectious).
# Clinical pearls
Pulmonary TB is traditionally split into primary (soon after initial infection, often middle/lower lung zones, common in children) and postprimary/adult-type (reactivation or recent reinfection, apical-posterior upper lobes); molecular data show many adult cases in endemic areas reflect recent infection rather than old reactivation.
Postprimary disease favours the apical and posterior segments of the upper lobes and the superior segments of the lower lobes, where higher local oxygen tension favours mycobacterial growth.
Cough develops in up to ~90% of cases and is often nonproductive initially before becoming purulent; hemoptysis occurs in 20-30%, and constitutional symptoms (diurnal fever, night sweats, weight loss, anorexia, malaise) are typical but nonspecific.
Absence of fever does not exclude TB; physical examination is frequently normal even with significant parenchymal disease, so reliance on exam findings can mislead.
Xpert MTB/RIF is the WHO first-line diagnostic, simultaneously detecting M. tuberculosis and rifampin resistance in under 2 hours; overall pulmonary sensitivity ~85% (up to 98% in smear-positive, ~70% in smear-negative), specificity 98%.
A negative NAAT (Xpert/Ultra) does NOT exclude TB and requires further workup; conversely culture remains the gold standard for definitive diagnosis.
Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra adds sensitivity (notably in smear-negative/culture-positive cases and PLWH) but its 'trace calls' can detect nonviable bacilli; trace calls in recently treated patients may represent dead-bacillus DNA.
At least one to two early-morning sputum specimens should be submitted for both AFB smear and mycobacterial culture; tissue for culture must NOT be placed in formaldehyde/preservative.
In HIV with partially preserved immunity, pulmonary TB looks classic (upper-lobe infiltrates and cavitation); with CD4 <200/uL it shifts to a primary-like pattern with diffuse/subtle interstitial infiltrates, little or no cavitation, pleural effusion and intrathoracic adenopathy.
Because HIV-associated TB cavitates less, such patients are often less infectious; PLWH with culture- and smear-positive disease may even have a normal chest radiograph.
Urine lipoarabinomannan (LF-LAM) is a point-of-care adjunct for TB diagnosis specifically in PLWH with advanced disease or low CD4 counts, not a general test.
MDR-TB is defined as resistance to at least isoniazid and rifampin; rifampin resistance (RR-TB) is used as a practical proxy for MDR; pre-XDR adds fluoroquinolone resistance, and XDR-TB adds resistance to a fluoroquinolone plus at least one additional group A drug (e.g., bedaquiline or linezolid).
# Additional differentials
Bronchogenic carcinoma (lung cancer) - upper-lobe mass/cavity, hemoptysis, weight loss; patients with such radiographic findings often undergo bronchoscopy/biopsy to distinguish from TB
Nontuberculous mycobacterial pulmonary disease (e.g., M. avium complex, M. kansasii) - AFB-positive smear with limited CXR abnormality, favoured by absence of TB risk factors and presence of underlying chronic lung disease
Chronic pulmonary aspergillosis / aspergilloma colonising an old TB cavity - hemoptysis, weight loss, fatigue, often misdiagnosed as TB recurrence; Aspergillus IgG precipitins and a fungal ball within a cavity suggest the diagnosis
Bacterial lung abscess / necrotising pneumonia (cavitary lesion with air-fluid level)
# Infection mimics
Nontuberculous mycobacteria (M. avium complex, M. kansasii) causing AFB-positive cavitary or nodular lung disease
Chronic pulmonary aspergillosis / aspergilloma in residual cavities mimicking TB relapse
Bacterial lung abscess or necrotising pneumonia producing upper-lobe cavitation
# Non infectious mimics
Bronchogenic carcinoma presenting as a cavitary upper-lobe mass with hemoptysis and weight loss
Solitary pulmonary nodule - TB can present as one, overlapping with neoplastic causes
Sarcoidosis and other granulomatous diseases (caseating vs non-caseating granuloma distinction)

Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra (next-generation NAAT with higher sensitivity in smear-negative and HIV-positive cases)

Liquid culture (MGIT) as WHO reference standard; solid media (Lowenstein-Jensen, Middlebrook 7H10/7H11) require 4-8 weeks
Universal drug-susceptibility testing - at minimum rifampin DST on all initial isolates
Targeted next-generation sequencing (tNGS) and whole-genome sequencing for comprehensive resistance profiling
Sputum induction with hypertonic saline when spontaneous sputum cannot be produced
Fiberoptic bronchoscopy with bronchial brushings, endobronchial/transbronchial biopsy, and bronchoalveolar lavage (submit for Xpert, culture, AFB smear)
Early-morning gastric lavage / induced sputum for children unable to expectorate
Urine lateral-flow lipoarabinomannan (LF-LAM) assay in advanced HIV
Chest CT to clarify equivocal plain-film findings
Tuberculin skin test (TST/PPD) and IFN-gamma release assays (IGRA: QuantiFERON-TB Gold/Plus, T-SPOT.TB) to identify TB infection - note both have low predictive value for who will progress and cannot distinguish infection from active disease
M. tuberculosis antigen-based skin tests (TBSTs using ESAT-6/CFP-10) with accuracy similar to IGRA
# Red flags
Massive hemoptysis from erosion of a vessel in a cavity wall or rupture of a Rasmussen's aneurysm - potentially life-threatening
Extensive disease causing dyspnea progressing to acute respiratory distress syndrome
Features of hematogenous/miliary dissemination (miliary reticulonodular CXR, hepatosplenomegaly, choroidal tubercles) - high mortality if unrecognised
Meningismus, headache, altered sensorium or cranial nerve palsy suggesting tuberculous meningitis - uniformly fatal if untreated
Advanced HIV with possible TB: NAAT-positive results warrant immediate treatment because diagnostic delay can be fatal
# Pitfalls
Treating on clinical/radiographic grounds alone: CXR has high sensitivity but poor specificity and no pattern (including a normal film) is pathognomonic in the HIV era - bacteriologic confirmation must always be pursued.
Interpreting a negative Xpert/NAAT or negative smear as ruling out TB - smear microscopy sensitivity is only 40-60% and a negative molecular test still requires culture and further workup.
AFB microscopy cannot distinguish M. tuberculosis from nontuberculous mycobacteria, and urine/gastric specimens are prone to false negatives (low organism load) and false positives (commensal mycobacteria).
Relying on the chest exam: physical findings are of limited use and may be entirely normal in significant pulmonary TB.
Missing diagnosis in atypical hosts (elderly nursing-home residents, teenagers, immunosuppressed/PLWH with lower-zone or noncavitary disease).
TST/IGRA pitfalls: false-negative TST in immunosuppression and overwhelming TB; false-positive from NTM or BCG; the TST 'boosting phenomenon' can mimic conversion; neither test distinguishes latent infection from active disease.
Putting tissue intended for culture into formaldehyde, which kills the organism and precludes culture.

# Coverage

# Framework
Do NOT start antitubercular therapy on clinical/radiological suspicion alone without microbiological attempt at confirmation (sputum smear, CBNAAT/Xpert MTB-RIF, culture) — premature ATT has consequences including masking the true diagnosis if it is not TB, and is a notifiable public health action.
Always send for rifampicin-resistance testing (e.g., via CBNAAT/Xpert) alongside initial diagnosis where available, since this directly determines whether standard first-line therapy or a drug-resistant TB regimen is appropriate.
Notify and register the case per national TB program requirements (e.g., NTEP in India) — this is a public health obligation, not optional.
Contact tracing and screening of close contacts (especially children and immunocompromised individuals) should be arranged once the diagnosis is confirmed.
