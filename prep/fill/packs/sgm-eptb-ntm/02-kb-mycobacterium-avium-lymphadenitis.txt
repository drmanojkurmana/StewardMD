# Non-tuberculous mycobacterial cervical lymphadenitis
Non-tuberculous mycobacterial cervical lymphadenitis, Infectious disease, Infectious Diseases.
Aliases: NTM lymphadenitis; atypical mycobacterial lymphadenitis; nontuberculous mycobacterial cervical lymphadenitis; MAC lymphadenitis.
# Pathophysiology
Nontuberculous mycobacterial cervical lymphadenitis develops when environmental mycobacteria, most often Mycobacterium avium complex, enter through the oral mucosa of a young child and spread to the draining submandibular and cervical lymph nodes. A granulomatous immune response with caseation forms within the node; because the child is immunocompetent, disease remains localised with minimal systemic features. Progressive necrosis can liquefy and extend to the skin, producing the typical violaceous discolouration, fluctuance, and eventual sinus formation. Multifocal, bilateral, recurrent, or unusually aggressive disease should prompt consideration of an underlying immune defect.
# Prognosis
The prognosis is excellent, particularly after complete surgical excision, which is usually curative. Untreated or incompletely treated disease tends to resolve over months but may leave scarring or a discharging sinus; recurrence is uncommon after complete excision.
# Clinical pearls
The most common form of nontuberculous mycobacterial disease in otherwise healthy young children, usually caused by Mycobacterium avium complex and less often M. scrofulaceum, M. haemophilum, or M. malmoense
Typically affects children aged one to five years, who are thought to acquire the organism from soil and water via the mouth
Presents as a unilateral, firm, painless, slowly enlarging node in the submandibular, cervical, or preauricular region with little systemic upset
The overlying skin often develops a characteristic violaceous discolouration and may progress to fluctuance and a chronically discharging sinus
The tuberculin skin test is usually negative or only weakly positive and interferon-gamma release assays are typically negative, helping to distinguish it from tuberculosis
Complete surgical excision of the affected nodes is the treatment of choice and offers the highest cure rate
Incision and drainage should be avoided because it commonly leads to a chronic discharging sinus
Macrolide-based antimicrobial therapy or watchful observation are alternatives when excision is incomplete, high risk to the facial nerve, or declined, as many nodes resolve slowly on their own
# Additional differentials
Tuberculous lymphadenitis
Reactive or pyogenic bacterial lymphadenitis
Cat-scratch disease
Lymphoma
Branchial cleft cyst or other congenital neck mass
Other granulomatous disease such as sarcoidosis
# Additional investigations
Excisional lymph-node biopsy, which is both diagnostic and therapeutic
Mycobacterial culture and molecular testing of tissue with speciation
Tuberculin skin test and interferon-gamma release assay to help exclude tuberculosis
Ultrasound of the neck to define nodal extent and proximity to the facial nerve
Histology showing granulomatous inflammation with or without caseation
Assessment of immune status in atypical, multifocal, or recurrent disease
# Red flags
Nodes near the facial nerve (preauricular or periparotid) where surgery risks nerve injury
Features suggesting tuberculosis, prompting exclusion and contact tracing
Multifocal, bilateral, or recurrent disease suggesting an underlying immunodeficiency
Rapidly enlarging or systemically symptomatic swelling suggesting malignancy
A chronically discharging sinus complicating incision and drainage
# Pitfalls
Performing incision and drainage, which promotes a chronic discharging sinus
Failing to exclude tuberculosis before attributing the adenitis to nontuberculous mycobacteria
Not sending tissue for mycobacterial culture and molecular testing
Risking facial-nerve injury during excision of periparotid or preauricular nodes
Overlooking an underlying immune defect in multifocal or recurrent disease
# Management
Undertake complete surgical excision of the affected nodes as the definitive treatment of choice, offering the highest cure rate
Avoid simple incision and drainage, which risks a chronically discharging sinus or fistula
Reserve macrolide-based antimycobacterial therapy (a macrolide such as clarithromycin combined with rifampicin or ethambutol) for incompletely excised, recurrent, or surgically hazardous disease near the facial nerve
Consider watchful observation in selected cases, since many nodes resolve slowly over months, weighing this against cosmetic scarring
Define nodal extent and proximity to the facial nerve with neck ultrasound to plan safe surgery
Assess immune status where disease is atypical, multifocal, bilateral, or recurrent
Refer for multidisciplinary care involving paediatric surgery or ENT and infectious diseases
