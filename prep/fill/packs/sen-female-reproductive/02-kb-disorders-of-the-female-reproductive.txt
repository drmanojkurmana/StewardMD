# Disorders of the Female Reproductive System
Disorders of the Female Reproductive System, Reproductive / Endocrine.
Aliases: Female reproductive endocrinology; Ovarian function disorders; Disorders of puberty in girls.
# Pathophysiology
The female reproductive axis is a tightly regulated hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian system. Pulsatile GnRH from roughly 7000 hypothalamic neurons, coordinated by pulsatile kisspeptin secretion (modulated by stimulatory neurokinin B and inhibitory dynorphin in KNDy neurons), drives pituitary FSH and LH release. FSH recruits secondary follicles from the resting pool (cyclic recruitment), restrained by AMH from small growing follicles. A single dominant follicle is selected within the first week of the cycle through high FSH-receptor density, high aromatase activity, and rising estradiol and inhibin A, which suppress FSH and the growth of competing follicles. Estradiol is produced by the cooperative two-cell mechanism (LH-driven theca androgen synthesis, FSH-driven granulosa aromatization). The mid-cycle estradiol-induced LH surge triggers resumption of oocyte meiosis, induction of COX-2, prostaglandins, and the progesterone receptor, and ovulation, followed by luteinization of theca and granulosa cells to form the progesterone-secreting corpus luteum. Disorders arise from defects at any level: genetic or anatomic GnRH-neuron migration defects, functional hypothalamic suppression from energy deficit, pituitary lesions, ovarian failure (gonadal dysgenesis, premature ovarian insufficiency, mutations in over 50 genes), or outflow-tract obstruction. Over 50 genes have been implicated in premature ovarian insufficiency, reflecting the genetic complexity of follicle endowment and survival.
# Prognosis
Many disorders are highly treatable when the level of the defect is identified. Functional hypothalamic amenorrhea from energy deficit is often reversible with restoration of energy balance. Outflow-tract obstruction has a good outcome with timely surgery, but delay increases the risk of endometriosis from retrograde menstruation. Premature ovarian insufficiency and gonadal dysgenesis carry permanent loss of fertility and require attention to long-term estrogen-deficiency consequences for bone and other tissues. In precocious puberty, untreated central precocity risks premature epiphyseal closure and compromised adult height, while appropriate management preserves height and addresses psychosocial impact.
# Severity classification
Amenorrhea is classified as primary (menses never occurred without hormonal treatment) versus secondary (cessation for 3-6 months). Abnormal uterine bleeding (AUB) replaces the older term dysfunctional uterine bleeding and is categorized etiologically by the PALM-COEIN system into structural causes (Polyp, Adenomyosis, Leiomyoma, Malignancy/hyperplasia) and nonstructural causes (Coagulopathy, Ovulatory dysfunction, Endometrial, Iatrogenic, Not yet classified). Precocious puberty is divided into central (GnRH-dependent) and peripheral (GnRH-independent) forms, and delayed puberty into hypergonadotropic (ovarian) and hypogonadotropic (central) forms.
# Clinical pearls
Normal cyclic reproductive function depends on coordinated signaling among the hypothalamus (GnRH), pituitary (FSH and LH), and ovary, producing repeating cycles of follicle growth, ovulation, and endometrial preparation; disruption at any level produces menstrual dysfunction.
The ovarian germ cell pool is non-renewable: oogonia peak at roughly 6-7 million by mid-gestation (about 20 weeks) and fall to only 1-2 million primordial follicles at birth, declining further through atresia thereafter.
In a normal ovulatory adult, cycle length averages about 28 days (range 25-35), bleeding lasts about 4-6 days, and the luteal phase is relatively fixed at 12-14 days, so most cycle-length variability reflects differences in follicular phase length.
Cycle-to-cycle variability for a consistently ovulating woman is only about plus or minus 2 days; cycles tend to shorten after age 35 and become more erratic and anovulatory as menopause approaches.
Documentation of ovulation can use a mid-luteal serum progesterone above 3 ng/mL (about 7 days after ovulation), a sustained basal body temperature rise of over 0.5 degrees F from the progesterone thermogenic effect, or a urinary LH surge; an apparent LH surge does not guarantee that ovulation actually occurs.
Estrogen synthesis requires cooperation of two ovarian cell types: theca cells make androgens under LH control, and granulosa cells aromatize these androgens to estradiol under FSH control (the two-cell, two-gonadotropin model).
Anti-mullerian hormone (AMH), produced by small growing preantral follicles, restrains FSH-driven recruitment, limiting the number of follicles entering the growing pool; AMH is a clinical marker of ovarian reserve and is elevated in PCOS.
GnRH neurons originate in the olfactory placode and migrate to the hypothalamus alongside olfactory neurons; failure of this migration links anosmia with hypogonadotropic hypogonadism (Kallmann phenotype).
FSH and LH are high in the neonatal period, fall into a quiescent childhood phase, and rise again at puberty; early in puberty gonadotropin secretion is detectable only during sleep before extending across the day.
Menarche occurs late in the pubertal sequence: it is preceded by adrenarche (pubic and axillary hair) and thelarche (breast development), with breast development typically about 2 years before menarche; U.S. average age at menarche is roughly 12.5 years.
Central precocious puberty in girls is idiopathic in about 85% of cases, but a neurogenic cause must be excluded; it shows pulsatile LH and a brisk LH/FSH response to GnRH stimulation, whereas peripheral precocity has suppressed gonadotropins with elevated estradiol.
Delayed puberty in girls (absence of secondary sexual characteristics by age 13) is of ovarian origin in 25-40% of cases, with Turner syndrome the leading cause; reversible functional causes such as undernutrition, excessive exercise, eating disorders, and chronic illness account for about 25%.
# Additional differentials
Pregnancy (the most common cause of amenorrhea, to be excluded in all cases)
Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) - leading cause of oligo-/anovulation
Premature ovarian insufficiency / gonadal dysgenesis including Turner syndrome
Functional hypothalamic amenorrhea from undernutrition, excessive exercise, or eating disorders
Hyperprolactinemia (pituitary lesions or medications)
Hypothalamic/pituitary tumors and infiltrative disease; Sheehan syndrome and lymphocytic hypophysitis postpartum
Mullerian agenesis (Mayer-Rokitansky-Kuster-Hauser syndrome) and androgen insensitivity syndrome as causes of primary amenorrhea
Late-onset (nonclassic) congenital adrenal hyperplasia and androgen-secreting tumors in heterosexual precocity
# Infection mimics
Genital tuberculosis as a cause of intrauterine adhesions (Asherman syndrome) in endemic regions, mimicking primary endometrial or ovulatory failure
CNS infection as a cause of central precocious puberty
# Non infectious mimics
Constitutional delay of growth and puberty mimicking pathologic delayed puberty
Premature thelarche (isolated breast development before age 2) without progression mimicking true precocious puberty
Premature adrenarche without progressive pubertal development, to be distinguished from nonclassic CAH and androgen-secreting tumors
McCune-Albright syndrome and congenital adrenal hyperplasia, in which initially peripheral precocity may convert to central precocity
# Additional investigations
Pregnancy test (beta-hCG) as the first step in any menstrual disturbance
Serum FSH and LH to localize the defect (ovarian vs central)
Estradiol and testosterone; DHEAS and 17-hydroxyprogesterone when hyperandrogenism is suspected
Prolactin in all patients with hypogonadotropic hypogonadism
TSH and free T4
Bone age and assessment of growth velocity in pubertal disorders
Pelvic ultrasound to assess follicle growth, endometrial thickness, corpus luteum, and reproductive-tract anatomy
Cranial MRI for suspected central/neuroanatomic causes; pelvic MRI to define tract anomalies before surgery
Karyotype in delayed puberty and suspected gonadal dysgenesis
AMH as a marker of ovarian reserve
Mid-luteal serum progesterone (>3 ng/mL) to confirm ovulation
# Red flags
Primary amenorrhea with absent secondary sexual characteristics, short stature, or height below the third percentile suggesting Turner syndrome or another pathologic cause requiring evaluation by age 13
Amenorrhea with galactorrhea, headache, visual symptoms, or diabetes insipidus suggesting a hypothalamic-pituitary mass
Cyclic pelvic pain with primary amenorrhea suggesting outflow-tract obstruction (imperforate hymen, transverse vaginal septum) requiring prompt surgery to limit endometriosis from retrograde flow
Secondary sexual development before age 8 (or breast/pubic hair before 6-7) warranting evaluation for precocious puberty, including exclusion of CNS tumors
Postpartum amenorrhea raising concern for pituitary necrosis (Sheehan syndrome) or lymphocytic hypophysitis
Heavy or prolonged bleeding (over 7 days) or markedly irregular intervals (over 35 or under 21 days) prompting evaluation for AUB causes including malignancy/hyperplasia
# Pitfalls
Failing to exclude pregnancy before any other workup of amenorrhea, since it is the most common cause.
Assuming the absence of premenstrual moliminal symptoms proves anovulation - their absence does not.
Treating a detected urinary LH surge as proof of ovulation when ovulation may still fail to occur.
Overlooking infrequent or absent menses because the patient does not report them, missing an underlying disorder with long-term health consequences.
Not measuring prolactin in hypogonadotropic hypogonadism, since hyperprolactinemia from lesions or drugs is a common reversible cause.
Forgetting that peripheral precocious puberty (e.g., McCune-Albright, CAH) can secondarily trigger central precocity, requiring reassessment.
Misattributing irregular cycles in adolescence or perimenopause to pathology when anovulatory irregularity is physiologic in the first few years after menarche and the years before menopause.
# Management
Direct therapy at the identified level of the axis: restore energy balance in functional hypothalamic amenorrhoea and use a dopamine agonist for hyperprolactinaemia
Provide cyclical oestrogen-progestogen replacement in hypogonadism to maintain bone health and secondary sexual characteristics
Induce ovulation for anovulatory infertility with letrozole, clomifene, or gonadotropins, or pulsatile GnRH in hypothalamic disease
Manage hyperandrogenism and polycystic ovary syndrome with combined hormonal contraceptives, antiandrogens, and lifestyle and metabolic measures
Correct outflow-tract obstruction surgically and without delay to limit the risk of endometriosis
Monitor bone mineral density in oestrogen-deficient states
Refer to endocrinology and reproductive gynaecology, and offer genetic counselling and screening where a genetic cause such as Turner syndrome is found
