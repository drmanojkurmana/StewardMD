# Primary ovarian insufficiency
Primary ovarian insufficiency, Endocrine / Reproductive, Paediatric & Reproductive Endocrinology.
Aliases: premature ovarian failure; premature ovarian insufficiency; hypergonadotropic hypogonadism (female); POI.
# Pathophysiology
Primary ovarian insufficiency reflects depletion or dysfunction of the ovarian follicular pool, so the ovary fails to produce adequate oestrogen and to complete folliculogenesis. Loss of ovarian feedback removes inhibition of the hypothalamic-pituitary axis, driving compensatory elevation of gonadotropins, especially FSH. Mechanisms include chromosomal abnormalities causing accelerated follicular atresia (as in Turner syndrome), the FMR1 premutation, other genetic and metabolic disorders (such as galactosaemia), autoimmune destruction of ovarian tissue, and iatrogenic gonadotoxic insults from chemotherapy, radiation, or surgery. The resulting oestrogen deficiency impairs pubertal development or causes menstrual cessation and predisposes to reduced bone mineral density.
# Prognosis
Fertility is substantially reduced and often permanently lost, though intermittent ovarian function means occasional spontaneous ovulation; with appropriate hormone replacement, secondary sexual characteristics, bone health, and cardiovascular risk can be well managed, and assisted reproduction with donor oocytes offers a route to pregnancy.
# Clinical pearls
Defined by loss of normal ovarian function before the age of 40, presenting with primary or secondary amenorrhoea, oestrogen deficiency, and impaired fertility
The biochemical signature is hypergonadotropic hypogonadism: a persistently raised follicle-stimulating hormone with a low oestradiol, reflecting failure of ovarian negative feedback
Turner syndrome (45,X and variants) is a leading cause in the paediatric age group and should be considered in any girl with delayed puberty and short stature
An FMR1 (fragile X) premutation is an important heritable cause and warrants testing, particularly where there is a family history of ovarian insufficiency, intellectual disability, or ataxia/tremor
Autoimmune oophoritis may occur in isolation or as part of an autoimmune polyglandular syndrome, so adrenal and thyroid autoimmunity should be screened for
Iatrogenic insufficiency from chemotherapy, pelvic irradiation, or ovarian surgery is increasingly common among survivors of childhood cancer
Management centres on oestrogen and progestogen replacement to induce or maintain secondary sexual characteristics, protect bone and cardiovascular health, and address fertility and psychological needs
Residual, intermittent ovarian activity can occur, so the condition is not always absolute and spontaneous conception is occasionally possible
# Additional differentials
Constitutional delay of growth and puberty
Hypogonadotropic hypogonadism (central causes, including isolated GnRH deficiency)
Polycystic ovary syndrome with oligomenorrhoea
Pregnancy as a cause of secondary amenorrhoea
Hyperprolactinaemia
Anatomical causes such as Müllerian agenesis or outflow obstruction
Functional hypothalamic amenorrhoea from weight loss, exercise, or illness
# Additional investigations
Serum FSH and LH (elevated) with oestradiol (low), repeated to confirm
Karyotype to detect Turner syndrome or Y-chromosome material
FMR1 premutation testing for fragile X-associated ovarian insufficiency
Adrenal and thyroid autoantibodies and screening for associated autoimmune endocrinopathy
Pelvic ultrasound to assess uterine size and ovarian morphology
Bone mineral density assessment given the risk of oestrogen-deficiency bone loss
# Red flags
Presence of Y-chromosome material on karyotype, which mandates gonadectomy because of malignant germ cell tumour risk in dysgenetic gonads
Features of adrenal insufficiency in association with autoimmune ovarian failure
Turner syndrome features requiring cardiac and aortic surveillance
Severe oestrogen deficiency with low bone density and fracture risk
# Pitfalls
Attributing amenorrhoea to stress or weight without measuring gonadotropins, thereby missing hypergonadotropic hypogonadism
Failing to karyotype and so overlooking Turner syndrome or occult Y-chromosome material requiring gonadectomy
Neglecting to test for the FMR1 premutation and its familial implications
Underestimating the long-term bone and cardiovascular consequences of untreated oestrogen deficiency
Telling patients that pregnancy is impossible, ignoring the small chance of spontaneous ovulation
