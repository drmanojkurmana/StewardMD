# Hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis
Hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis, Multisystem, Rheumatology.
Aliases: Anti-C1q vasculitis; HUVS; Hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis (anti-C1q); Hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis syndrome; McDuffie syndrome; anti-C1q antibody; hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis syndrome; hypocomplementemic urticarial vasculitis.
# Pathophysiology
Hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis is an immune-complex-mediated small-vessel vasculitis. Autoantibodies against the collagen-like region of C1q form immune complexes that activate the classical complement pathway, consuming C1q, C4 and C3 and generating anaphylatoxins. These recruit neutrophils to postcapillary venules in the skin and other organs, producing a leukocytoclastic vasculitis with endothelial injury, extravasation and the characteristic long-lived urticarial lesions. The same immune-complex and complement mechanisms underlie extracutaneous involvement of the joints, eyes, kidneys and lungs, and account for the close relationship with systemic lupus erythematosus.
# Prognosis
Skin disease is often chronic and relapsing but the overall outlook is largely determined by systemic involvement; progressive obstructive lung disease and renal disease are the main causes of morbidity and mortality. Many patients require prolonged immunomodulatory therapy.
# Clinical pearls
The hallmark is recurrent urticarial lesions that persist beyond 24 hours, resolve with bruising or pigmentation and are often painful or burning rather than purely itchy.
Persistently low complement, particularly C1q, defines the syndrome and correlates with more severe systemic disease.
Circulating anti-C1q antibodies are characteristic and drive classical-pathway complement consumption.
There is substantial clinical overlap with systemic lupus erythematosus, and some patients evolve into or coexist with lupus.
Systemic features include arthralgia or arthritis, uveitis or episcleritis, obstructive lung disease and glomerulonephritis.
Angio-oedema and laryngeal involvement can occur and may be life-threatening.
Skin biopsy shows leukocytoclastic vasculitis, distinguishing it from ordinary chronic urticaria.
Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, sometimes severe, is a recognised and prognostically important association, especially in smokers.
Additional differentials: Chronic spontaneous urticaria; Systemic lupus erythematosus; Normocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis; Cryoglobulinaemic vasculitis; Schnitzler syndrome; Serum sickness or drug hypersensitivity; IgA vasculitis.
# Additional investigations
Complement profile including C1q, C4 and C3, typically all reduced
Anti-C1q antibody assay
Skin biopsy demonstrating leukocytoclastic vasculitis
ANA, anti-dsDNA and extended autoimmune serology to assess lupus overlap
Urinalysis and renal function for glomerulonephritis
Pulmonary function tests and chest imaging where respiratory symptoms are present
Ophthalmological assessment for uveitis or episcleritis
Red flags: Laryngeal oedema or airway compromise; Rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis; Progressive dyspnoea suggesting obstructive lung disease; Evolving multi-system involvement indicating lupus overlap; Ocular pain with visual loss.
# Pitfalls
Managing the rash as ordinary chronic urticaria and missing the underlying vasculitis and low complement.
Failing to check C1q and anti-C1q antibodies, which are central to the diagnosis.
Overlooking the strong association with systemic lupus erythematosus.
Underestimating pulmonary risk, particularly the interaction with smoking.
Attributing persistent painful weals to allergy and delaying biopsy.
# Management
Control inflammation with systemic corticosteroids as the mainstay for active or systemic disease
Add steroid-sparing agents such as hydroxychloroquine, dapsone, colchicine, azathioprine or mycophenolate for maintenance
Reserve rituximab or other biologic and immunosuppressive therapy for refractory disease or serious renal or pulmonary involvement
Use antihistamines for symptomatic relief of the urticarial lesions, recognising their limited effect in vasculitic disease
Monitor complement (C1q, C4, C3), renal function, urinalysis and pulmonary function to detect organ involvement early
Treat angio-oedema and laryngeal involvement as an emergency with airway protection and adrenaline where the reaction is anaphylactoid
Screen for and co-manage overlapping systemic lupus erythematosus
Refer to rheumatology, with nephrology and respiratory input as organ involvement dictates
