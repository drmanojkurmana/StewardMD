# Tubulointerstitial Kidney Disease
Tubulointerstitial Kidney Disease, Nephrology.
Aliases: interstitial nephritis; acute interstitial nephritis; renal tubular acidosis.
# Pathophysiology
A group of disorders that primarily affect the renal tubules and interstitium with relative sparing of the glomeruli and vessels, divided into acute and chronic tubulointerstitial nephritis (TIN). Acute TIN causes AKI either via aggressive inflammatory infiltrates producing tissue edema, tubular cell injury, and compromised tubular flow, or via frank tubular obstruction by casts, cellular debris, or crystals; flank pain may arise from renal capsule distention. Acute interstitial nephritis (AIN) is most commonly an allergic, immune-mediated drug reaction (antibiotics such as beta-lactams/sulfonamides/quinolones/vancomycin/rifampin, NSAIDs/COX-2 inhibitors, proton pump inhibitors, diuretics, anticonvulsants, allopurinol, immune checkpoint inhibitors), but may also be immune-mediated within autoimmune syndromes (Sjogren's, SLE, TINU, granulomatous/sarcoid, IgG4-related disease) or infection-associated. Chronic TIN produces interstitial fibrosis with patchy mononuclear cell infiltration, widespread tubular atrophy, luminal dilation, and thickened tubular basement membranes, manifesting as tubular dysfunction (nephrogenic DI, Fanconi's syndrome, RTA) and progressive azotemia. Because the chronic histopathology is nonspecific, biopsy rarely yields a specific diagnosis, so diagnosis relies on history, drug/toxin exposure, associated symptoms, and imaging.
# Prognosis
In allergic/drug-induced AIN, discontinuation of the offending agent often reverses the kidney injury, though recovery may be incomplete depending on duration of exposure and degree of tubular atrophy and interstitial fibrosis. Granulomatous, idiopathic, TINU, and Sjogren's-related interstitial nephritis generally respond to glucocorticoids, especially when treated early before significant fibrosis, but may relapse and require maintenance immunosuppression. Chronic TIN damage that has already occurred is impossible to reverse, but further deterioration may be slowed by treating glomerular hypertension. Reflux nephropathy affecting both kidneys often progresses inexorably to ESRD over years even without ongoing infection or reflux. Lithium-associated CKD is often irreversible and slowly progresses to ESRD even after lithium is stopped. AKI from acute hypercalcemia may be fully reversible, whereas chronic hypercalcemic kidney dysfunction may not improve even after correcting the calcium disorder.
# Clinical pearls
Tubulointerstitial diseases primarily affect the tubules and interstitium with relative sparing of the glomeruli and renal vessels, and are divided into acute and chronic tubulointerstitial nephritis (TIN).
Classic allergic acute interstitial nephritis (AIN) presents with fever, rash, peripheral eosinophilia, and oliguric acute kidney injury occurring after 7-10 days of a beta-lactam (e.g., methicillin) - but this classic tetrad is the exception rather than the rule; patients are more often found incidentally to have a rising creatinine.
Acute TIN most often presents as acute kidney injury; today AIN is far more often an allergic reaction to a drug than a postinfectious phenomenon.
Urinalysis in allergic AIN shows pyuria with white blood cell casts and hematuria; white blood cell (leukocyte) casts are a characteristic finding of interstitial nephritis.
The mainstay of treatment for drug-induced AIN is discontinuation of the offending agent, which often leads to reversal of kidney injury; glucocorticoids may accelerate recovery but are NOT required in most cases and are reserved for severe injury.
Chronic TIN is more indolent and manifests with disorders of tubular function: polyuria from impaired concentrating ability (nephrogenic diabetes insipidus), Fanconi's syndrome from defective proximal reabsorption (glycosuria, phosphaturia, aminoaciduria, hypokalemia, and type II/proximal RTA from bicarbonaturia), or non-anion-gap metabolic acidosis with hyperkalemia (type IV RTA) from impaired ammoniagenesis.
Proteinuria in chronic TIN is usually modest (rarely >2 g/d) from decreased tubular reabsorption of filtered proteins, but nephrotic-range albuminuria may occur when secondary focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS) develops.
Tubulointerstitial nephritis with uveitis (TINU) accounts for <5% of AIN, affects females 3x more than males, with median onset age 15 years; hallmark is painful anterior uveitis plus lymphocyte-predominant interstitial nephritis, with sterile pyuria, mild proteinuria, features of Fanconi's syndrome, and elevated ESR.
Sterile pyuria with elevated creatinine and features of Fanconi's syndrome should raise suspicion for TINU; ocular symptoms precede or accompany kidney disease in only one-third of cases.
Papillary necrosis is a key feature of analgesic nephropathy; a sloughed necrotic papilla can cause gross hematuria and ureteric colic from ureteral obstruction.
Renal ultrasound in reflux nephropathy characteristically shows asymmetric small kidneys with irregular outlines, thinned cortices, and regions of compensatory hypertrophy.

# Infection mimics
Acute bacterial pyelonephritis - infection-associated AIN should be distinguished from it; pyelonephritis does not generally cause AKI unless bilateral or causing septic shock
Reflux nephropathy was previously mislabeled 'chronic pyelonephritis' because it was thought to result from recurrent childhood UTIs
Infection-associated AIN, currently most often polyomavirus BK reactivation in kidney transplant recipients
Granulomatous interstitial nephritis - tuberculosis must be ruled out before treatment, as it is a rare cause
# Non infectious mimics
Acute tubular necrosis and other causes of unexplained AKI (biopsy-proven AIN accounts for no more than ~15% of unexplained AKI, likely an underestimate)
Primary glomerulonephritis causing secondary tubulointerstitial damage; in advanced disease the initial cause is often difficult to discern on biopsy
Behcet's disease and sarcoidosis as alternative causes of uveitis when considering TINU
# Additional investigations
Urinalysis - active sediment with leukocytes and cellular casts in acute TIN; pyuria with white blood cell casts and hematuria in allergic AIN; bland sediment in aristolochic acid nephropathy
Peripheral blood eosinophil count - supports AIN but present in only a minority of patients
Urinary eosinophils - NOT recommended; neither sensitive nor specific for AIN
Serum creatinine and BUN to assess azotemia
Anti-Ro (SS-A) and anti-La (SS-B) antibodies when Sjogren's syndrome suspected
Serum and urine protein electrophoresis and immunofixation, plus serum/urine free light chains, when light chain cast nephropathy suspected
Renal ultrasonography - 'medical renal disease' changes (increased echogenicity, loss of corticomedullary differentiation); asymmetric scarred kidneys in reflux nephropathy
Kidney biopsy - not usually required for AIN diagnosis but shows interstitial/tubular leukocyte infiltration including eosinophils; required (with other criteria) for aristolochic acid nephropathy
# Red flags
Classic allergic AIN tetrad: fever, rash, peripheral eosinophilia, and oliguric AKI after a drug
Particularly severe and rapid-onset AIN upon reintroduction of rifampin after a drug-free period
Painful anterior uveitis (often bilateral, with blurred vision and photophobia) with sterile pyuria and AKI - suggests TINU
Gross hematuria with ureteric colic from a sloughed necrotic papilla in analgesic or sickle cell nephropathy
Triad of saturnine gout, hypertension, and impaired kidney function suggesting lead nephropathy
Anemia disproportionately severe relative to kidney dysfunction in aristolochic acid nephropathy (with very high risk of upper urinary tract urothelial cancer)
# Pitfalls
The classic AIN tetrad (fever, rash, eosinophilia, oliguric AKI) is the exception rather than the rule; relying on it misses most cases.
NSAID-induced AIN often lacks fever, rash, and eosinophilia but presents with AKI and heavy proteinuria.
Urinary eosinophil testing is neither sensitive nor specific for AIN and is not recommended.
Absence of peripheral eosinophilia does not exclude AIN, as it is present in only a minority of patients.
A negative urine dipstick for protein with elevated total spot urine protein is highly suggestive of Bence-Jones protein (light chains), which dipsticks do not detect - missing myeloma kidney.
Reflux nephropathy may go unnoticed until adulthood and can be mistaken for 'chronic pyelonephritis'; a single affected kidney may be silent except for hypertension.
Because chronic TIN histopathology is nonspecific, biopsy rarely gives a specific diagnosis; over-reliance on biopsy without history/exposure analysis is a pitfall.
# Management
Prompt withdrawal of the causative drug (implicated beta-lactam, sulfonamide, NSAID, proton pump inhibitor, allopurinol or immune checkpoint inhibitor) as the mainstay of drug-induced acute interstitial nephritis
A course of corticosteroids for severe or non-resolving acute interstitial nephritis to accelerate renal recovery
Treatment of the underlying cause in secondary disease, such as immunosuppression for sarcoidosis or Sjogren's and cessation of exposure in aristolochic-acid or heavy-metal nephropathy
Supportive correction of tubular dysfunction in chronic disease, with electrolyte and bicarbonate replacement for renal tubular acidosis and management of nephrogenic diabetes insipidus and Fanconi features
Avoidance of further nephrotoxins and dose adjustment of renally cleared drugs
Monitoring of renal function and electrolytes, with dialysis support for severe acute kidney injury
Nephrology referral and, where the diagnosis is uncertain, renal biopsy to confirm the diagnosis and guide treatment
