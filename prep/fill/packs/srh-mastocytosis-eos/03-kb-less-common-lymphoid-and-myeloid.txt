# Less Common Lymphoid and Myeloid Malignancies
Less Common Lymphoid and Myeloid Malignancies, Hematologic / Oncologic.
Aliases: Rare lymphoid malignancies; Rare myeloid malignancies; Unusual hematologic neoplasms.
# Pathophysiology
These entities are individually rare clonal neoplasms of the lymphoid or myeloid lineages. Rare lymphoid tumors are mature B-cell, T-cell, or NK-cell neoplasms; several B-cell variants of diffuse large B-cell lymphoma have distinctive biology, such as intravascular large B-cell lymphoma (confined to vessel lumens, postulated to reflect defective adhesion molecule and homing function with absent beta-1 integrin and ICAM-1), primary effusion lymphoma (HHV-8/KSHV-driven, frequently EBV co-infected), and lymphomatoid granulomatosis (an angiodestructive EBV-infected monoclonal B-cell proliferation outnumbered by reactive T cells). Many T/NK entities are EBV-associated and angiocentric. On the myeloid side, distinct molecular drivers define the entities: activating CSF3R mutations in chronic neutrophilic leukemia, GM-CSF hypersensitivity from dysregulated RAS/MAPK signaling (mutually exclusive RAS, PTPN11, NF1 mutations) in juvenile myelomonocytic leukemia, somatic GATA1 mutations in transient myeloproliferative disorder of Down syndrome, and constitutively active tyrosine kinase gene fusions (involving PDGFRA such as FIP1L1-PDGFRA, PDGFRB, FGFR1, ABL1, JAK2, FLT3) in eosinophilic myeloid/lymphoid neoplasms. Mastocytosis reflects neoplastic mast cell proliferation typically driven by KIT D816V. Dendritic/histiocytic neoplasms arise from antigen-presenting or antigen-processing cell lineages; Langerhans cell histiocytosis cells are S100+/CD1a+ with Birbeck granules and about 55% carry BRAF V600E.
# Prognosis
Outcomes vary widely by entity. Indolent disorders include splenic marginal zone lymphoma (median survival over 10 years), T-cell large granular lymphocytic leukemia, and primary cutaneous CD30+ T-cell lymphoma (5-year survival over 90%). Aggressive entities carry poor outcomes: T-cell prolymphocytic leukemia (median survival about 12 months), enteropathy-type T-cell lymphoma (median 7-11 months), hepatosplenic T-cell lymphoma (median about 2 years), primary effusion lymphoma (death typically within 6 months), and intravascular large B-cell lymphoma. Mediastinal large B-cell lymphoma is potentially curable with good multi-year survival. In extranodal NK/T-cell lymphoma, nasal type, survival tracks with the number of risk factors (B symptoms, advanced stage, elevated LDH, regional node involvement), with 5-year survival ranging from 81% (zero risk factors) down to 7% (three to four factors). Chronic neutrophilic leukemia has a median survival of about 2 years, atypical CML around 10-25 months, and CMML survival ranges by Mayo Molecular Model risk from about 16 to 97 months. Histiocytic sarcoma has a poor prognosis with median survival measured in months to about 2 years.
# Severity classification
Several entities have formal staging/risk schemes. Myeloid neoplasms are classified by the ICC and WHO systems using blood counts, smear, marrow morphology, and cytogenetic/molecular tests. CMML is graded as CMML-1 (<10% marrow and <5% blood blasts) and CMML-2 (10-19% marrow or 5-19% blood blasts), with the Mayo Molecular Model assigning low to high risk by truncating ASXL1 mutation, absolute monocytes >10 x10^9/L, hemoglobin <10 g/dL, platelets <100 x10^9/L, and circulating immature myeloid cells. Systemic mastocytosis is subclassified into indolent, smoldering (requires two or more B findings), aggressive, SM with associated myeloid/hematologic neoplasm, and mast cell leukemia (>=20% mast cells in marrow aspirate); advanced SM is defined by an associated neoplasm or organopathy (C findings). Hypereosinophilia is defined as absolute eosinophil count >=1500 x10^9/L, with hypereosinophilic syndrome requiring eosinophil-mediated organ damage. Extranodal NK/T-cell lymphoma nasal type uses a four-factor risk model (B symptoms, advanced stage, elevated LDH, regional node involvement).
# Clinical pearls
This chapter covers the uncommon hematologic neoplasms, each individually accounting for under 1% of hematologic malignancies; the rare lymphoid tumors are all mature B-cell, T-cell, or NK-cell neoplasms.
Splenic marginal zone lymphoma cells express surface immunoglobulin and CD20 but are negative for CD5, CD10, CD43, and CD103; absence of CD5 separates it from CLL and absence of CD103 separates it from hairy cell leukemia, and circulating cells are villous lymphocytes.
Hairy cell leukemia is a frequent cause of a 'dry tap' on marrow aspiration because of increased reticulin fibers; it shows profound monocytopenia (predisposing to atypical mycobacterial infection), strong CD22/CD25/CD103 expression, tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase positivity, and the activating BRAF V600E mutation in most cases.
Mediastinal (thymic) large B-cell lymphoma typically remains confined to the mediastinum, affects women more than men (ratio about 1:2-3) at a median age of 35-40 years, is CD20 positive and CD45 positive (unlike classical Hodgkin's), and may lack surface immunoglobulin and HLA class I/II expression.
Primary effusion lymphoma presents with effusions usually without a tumor mass, is caused by HHV-8/KSHV (often EBV co-infected), occurs mainly in immunodeficiency such as AIDS, and follows a rapidly fatal course.
T-cell prolymphocytic leukemia is aggressive with very high WBC counts often exceeding 100,000/uL, hepatosplenomegaly, and adenopathy; chromosome 14 inversion between q11 and q32 occurs in about 80% of patients.
T-cell large granular lymphocytic leukemia is dominated by severe neutropenia and is frequently associated with systemic autoimmune disease such as rheumatoid arthritis; pure red cell aplasia occurs in 15-20% and the course is generally indolent.
Extranodal NK/T-cell lymphoma, nasal type, is an EBV-related angiocentric/angiodestructive CD56-positive lesion prevalent in Asia, Mexico, and Central/South America; historic names include lethal midline granuloma and polymorphic reticulosis.
Enteropathy-type T-cell lymphoma is a rare complication of longstanding celiac disease, usually arising in jejunum or ileum, with most patients carrying the celiac-associated HLA DQA1*0501 or DQB1*0201 genotype.
