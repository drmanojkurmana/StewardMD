# Paget's Disease of Bone
Paget's Disease of Bone, Endocrine / Metabolic Bone.
Aliases: osteitis deformans.
# Pathophysiology
The principal abnormality is increased number and activity of osteoclasts, which are large, increased 10- to 100-fold in number, and contain many nuclei (up to 100 vs 3-5 in normal osteoclasts), creating a sevenfold rise in resorptive surfaces. Contributing mechanisms include osteoclast precursor hypersensitivity to 1,25(OH)2D3, hyperresponsiveness to RANK ligand (RANKL), increased RANKL expression by marrow stromal cells, increased IL-6 (raising precursor recruitment), increased c-fos expression, and overexpression of the antiapoptotic oncogene Bcl-2. Osteoblasts are recruited to resorption sites and lay down large amounts of new bone matrix, so turnover is high and bone mass is normal or increased (not reduced) unless there is concomitant calcium/vitamin D deficiency. The process passes through an initial osteolytic phase (prominent resorption and hypervascularization, seen radiographically as an advancing lytic wedge or 'blade of grass' lesion), an active mixed phase replacing lamellar bone with haphazard woven bone (marrow may be replaced by fibrous tissue), and a final sclerotic ('burned-out') phase of hard, dense, less vascular mosaic bone; all three phases may coexist at different sites. Etiology remains unknown but evidence supports both genetic (family history in 15-25%; familial syndromes linked to TNFRSF11B/osteoprotegerin, TNFRSF11A/RANK, profilin 1, valosin-containing protein, and the common SQSTM1/p62 mutation) and viral (paramyxovirus-like inclusions and viral mRNA in osteoclasts) contributions.
# Prognosis
Most patients are asymptomatic and have a benign course; for unclear reasons the prevalence and severity are decreasing and the age of diagnosis is increasing. Effective potent antiresorptive (bisphosphonate) therapy can normalize bone turnover and is associated with an apparently decreasing incidence of sarcoma. Serious complications - fractures, deformity, secondary osteoarthritis, neurologic compromise (hearing loss, cranial nerve palsies, cord/brainstem compression), high-output cardiac state, and rare (<0.5%) malignant transformation (usually osteosarcoma) - drive morbidity. Whether early therapy prevents late complications remains to be determined; deformities may take many years to manifest.
# Severity classification
Disease activity is gauged by the extent of skeletal involvement and the degree of bone-turnover marker elevation (notably total ALP, with ALP roughly four times above normal cited as marking high activity and cardiovascular risk when 15-35% of the skeleton is involved). Pathologically the disease evolves through three phases that may coexist: an osteolytic phase, an active mixed (woven-bone) phase, and a final sclerotic 'burned-out' phase.
# Clinical pearls
A localized bone-remodeling disorder affecting widespread, noncontiguous areas of the skeleton; initiated by overactive osteoclastic resorption followed by compensatory osteoblastic new bone formation, producing a disorganized mosaic of woven and lamellar bone.
Most patients are asymptomatic; diagnosis is often made incidentally from an elevated alkaline phosphatase (ALP) on routine blood chemistry or an abnormal skeletal radiograph obtained for another indication.
