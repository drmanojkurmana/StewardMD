# Pathophysiology
Hepatic encephalopathy is an alteration of mental status and cognition resulting from liver failure, in which gut-derived neurotoxins are not cleared because of portosystemic vascular shunting and decreased functional hepatic mass and thus reach the brain. Ammonia is the principal candidate toxin: high ammonia concentrations are thought to drive increased synthesis of glutamine within astrocytes, causing osmotic astrocyte swelling, mitochondrial energy failure, and generation of reactive nitrogen and oxygen species, together with increased levels of the inhibitory neurotransmitter GABA and synthesis of putative 'false' neurotransmitters. No single one of these mechanisms fully explains the confusion and stupor, and which is mainly responsible for hepatic coma is not known. Other contributing compounds include certain false neurotransmitters and mercaptans. Chronic HE is characteristically accompanied by diffuse astrocytosis. In acute liver failure (but not chronic liver disease) cerebral edema with gray-matter swelling can occur and carries a risk of cerebral herniation.
# Prognosis
Development of hepatic encephalopathy in chronic liver disease is a poor prognostic sign, although the complication can be managed in the vast majority of patients. Hepatic encephalopathy grade is one of the five Child-Pugh variables (with bilirubin, albumin, prothrombin time, and ascites severity) used to stratify cirrhosis into classes A-C of predicted survival. In acute hepatitis, the appearance of encephalopathy defines acute liver failure and indicates poor prognosis.
# Severity classification
Severity is graded clinically along a continuum from the earliest covert/subtle changes (altered sleep, personality change, irritability, mental dullness — detectable mainly by mental-status testing such as the trail-making test) through confusion and disorientation to stupor and coma. Encephalopathy grade is incorporated as one component of the Child-Pugh classification of cirrhosis severity.
# Clinical pearls
Hepatic encephalopathy is far more common in chronic liver disease than in acute liver injury; in severe acute liver injury its presence is itself a defining criterion for acute liver failure.
Asterixis (the 'liver flap') is typically present and is elicited by having the patient extend the arms and dorsiflex the wrists, looking for a sudden forward jerk of the wrist; it requires a cooperative patient.
Earliest manifestations are subtle and nonspecific: altered sleep pattern, personality change, irritability, and mental dullness, progressing to confusion, disorientation, stupor and coma.
Patients may be agitated, violent and hard to manage, or alternatively very sleepy and difficult to rouse.
In acute liver failure, presentation may include excitability or mania rather than depressed consciousness.
Fetor hepaticus, a sweetish ammoniacal breath odor, can accompany liver failure, especially with portosystemic shunting of blood.
After a first episode, patients and caregivers often learn to recognize recurrences early and can self-medicate to prevent worsening.
Gut-derived neurotoxins that bypass the liver because of vascular shunting and reduced hepatic mass reach the brain and produce the syndrome.
# Additional differentials
Other causes of altered mental status must always be considered, since the diagnosis is clinical
Sedative/narcotic intoxication
Renal failure / uremic encephalopathy
Respiratory failure
Electrolyte imbalance as a primary cause of confusion
# Non infectious mimics
Metabolic encephalopathy from other causes (hypoglycemia, hyponatremia, hyperosmolar states, hypercapnia, hypercalcemia, renal failure) — asterixis and multifocal myoclonus are nonspecific and indicate a metabolic or toxic process generally, not HE specifically
Drug intoxication, which likewise can produce asterixis in a drowsy, confused patient
Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome and alcohol withdrawal in patients with alcohol-associated liver disease
# Additional investigations
Careful mental status examination is the central assessment
Trail-making (connect-the-dots / number-connection) test: normal 15-30 s, markedly prolonged in early HE
Bedside cognitive tasks such as drawing abstract objects or comparing the patient's signature with prior examples
Diagnostic paracentesis of ascites to exclude infection as a precipitant
Evaluate for GI bleeding
Serum electrolytes
EEG and visual evoked potentials can detect mild forms but are rarely clinically useful
# Red flags
Encephalopathy appearing during acute hepatitis is the major criterion for acute liver failure and indicates a poor prognosis
In acute liver failure, severe encephalopathy with cerebral edema can progress to cerebral herniation
Other presenting features of poor prognosis in acute hepatitis: ascites, peripheral edema, prolonged PT, hypoalbuminemia, hypoglycemia, very high bilirubin
HE may herald acute-on-chronic liver failure (brain is one of the failing organ systems), which carries 28-day mortality of 20-70% rising with the number of organ failures
# Pitfalls
Ammonia levels are typically elevated but correlate poorly with severity of liver disease; most hepatologists do not rely on ammonia to make the diagnosis or to follow clinical progress.
Always identify and treat a precipitant — in cirrhosis, encephalopathy is usually triggered by volume depletion/dehydration, GI bleeding, infection, constipation, hyponatremia, overdiuresis, uremia, electrolyte imbalance, or sedatives/narcotic analgesics; correcting hydration and electrolytes alone is sometimes sufficient.
Asterixis is nonspecific — it also occurs in other metabolic and toxic encephalopathies, so it does not by itself establish HE.
The diagnosis is clinical and requires an experienced clinician to integrate the various features; there is no single confirmatory test.
# Confirm
Confusion/asterixis with chronic liver disease or porto-systemic shunt; a precipitant (GI bleed, infection, constipation, electrolytes, sedatives) is usually present.
Admit; ICU if airway risk.
Treat the precipitant + lactulose (± rifaximin)
# Steps
Identify and treat the PRECIPITANT (GI bleed, sepsis/SBP, constipation, hypokalaemia, dehydration, sedatives).
Lactulose titrated to 2–3 soft stools/day (oral or enema).
Add rifaximin for inadequate response / secondary prevention.
Avoid sedatives; do not over-restrict protein; correct electrolytes.
Manage underlying cirrhosis; assess for transplant.
Treat precipitant + lactulose
