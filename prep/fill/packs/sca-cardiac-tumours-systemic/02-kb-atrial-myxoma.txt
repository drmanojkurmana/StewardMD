# Atrial myxoma
Atrial myxoma, Cardiovascular, Cardiology / Cardiac surgery.
Aliases: cardiac myxoma.
# Pathophysiology
An atrial myxoma is a benign neoplasm of primitive mesenchymal cells, typically pedunculated and attached to the atrial septum. Its gelatinous, friable surface and mobility explain the clinical triad: a pedunculated mass can prolapse into the atrioventricular valve during diastole causing intermittent inflow obstruction, fragments or surface thrombus can embolise, and cytokine production (notably interleukin-6) drives constitutional and inflammatory features. Left atrial tumours embolise systemically, while right atrial tumours embolise to the lungs.
# Prognosis
Surgical excision is usually curative for sporadic myxomas with an excellent prognosis, though recurrence occurs, particularly in familial (Carney complex) cases requiring surveillance.
# Clinical pearls
The commonest primary cardiac tumour, benign, and most often arising in the left atrium near the fossa ovalis
The classic triad of manifestations is obstructive, embolic and constitutional
Obstruction can mimic mitral stenosis, sometimes with a tumour plop and positional symptoms
Embolism (systemic from left-sided, pulmonary from right-sided tumours) can cause stroke or limb ischaemia
Constitutional features include fever, weight loss and raised inflammatory markers, mimicking endocarditis or vasculitis
Most are sporadic, but multiple or recurrent myxomas suggest the Carney complex; treatment is surgical excision
# Additional differentials
Mitral stenosis
Infective endocarditis
Left atrial thrombus
Other cardiac tumours (papillary fibroelastoma, sarcoma)
Vasculitis or other cause of constitutional illness
# Additional investigations
Echocardiography (transthoracic and transoesophageal) to characterise the mass
Cardiac MRI for tissue characterisation and extent
Assessment for embolic complications
Inflammatory markers
Evaluation for Carney complex if multiple, recurrent or young-onset
# Red flags
Embolic stroke or systemic embolism
Syncope or haemodynamic compromise from valve obstruction
Features suggesting malignant cardiac tumour
Recurrent or multiple tumours suggesting a familial syndrome
# Pitfalls
Mistaking it for infective endocarditis or vasculitis given constitutional features
Attributing obstructive symptoms solely to mitral stenosis
Overlooking a myxoma as a source of embolic stroke
Not considering Carney complex in young or recurrent cases
# Management
Undertake prompt complete surgical excision of the tumour together with its septal attachment as the curative mainstay, given the ongoing embolic and obstructive risk
Characterise the mass fully with echocardiography before operation and expedite surgery rather than delay
Manage complications encountered at presentation, including heart failure from inflow obstruction and embolic events such as stroke or limb ischaemia
Perform surveillance echocardiography after resection to detect recurrence
Evaluate for the Carney complex when the myxoma is multiple, recurrent, familial or arises in a young patient, and screen first-degree relatives echocardiographically
Coordinate care between cardiology and cardiac surgery
