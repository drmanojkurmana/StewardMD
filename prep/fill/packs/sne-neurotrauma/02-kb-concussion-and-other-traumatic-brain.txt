# Concussion and Other Traumatic Brain Injuries
Concussion and Other Traumatic Brain Injuries, Nervous system.
Aliases: Traumatic brain injury; TBI; Concussion; Mild TBI; Head injury; Closed head injury.
# Pathophysiology
TBI results from external mechanical force - the head being struck or striking an object, acceleration/deceleration of the brain, penetration, or blast/explosion - producing primary structural lesions and secondary injury. A skull fracture marks the site and severity of impact and, when the arachnoid is torn, opens a pathway for CSF leak and bacterial entry (meningitis risk); basilar fractures may cause CSF leak, pneumocephalus, and delayed cavernous-carotid fistula. Extra-axial hematomas arise from torn meningeal arteries (epidural) or bridging veins (subdural) and exert mass effect that raises intracranial pressure. Cortical surface bruising (contusion) occurs at coup and contrecoup sites with petechial hemorrhage, edema, and tissue destruction. Shearing of axons (traumatic/diffuse axonal injury) at impact, with associated microhemorrhages, disrupts white-matter connectivity. Traumatic subarachnoid hemorrhage from rupture of small cortical vessels is common and, when diffuse after severe TBI, increases mortality. In concussion/mild TBI, conventional CT is often normal while higher-resolution 3T MRI may reveal contusion or microhemorrhage; advanced imaging shows reduced cerebral blood flow, altered functional connectivity, and white-matter microstructural change that broadly track injury severity and recovery, though subtle changes may persist after clinical recovery (a neurobiologic recovery 'tail').
# Prognosis
The typical concussion syndrome has a good prognosis with little risk of subsequent deterioration. After sport-related concussion, recovery is generally favorable: in a large collegiate cohort, median symptom resolution was about 6.4 days and median return to sport 12.8 days, with ~92% symptom resolution and ~85% clearance to return by one month; most athletes across ages fully recover over 1-4 weeks. A subset develop persistent postconcussion symptoms (PPCS) beyond ~3 months; preinjury mental health disorder, migraine, and prior concussion predispose to prolonged recovery. Recovery from severe TBI can take years, and long-term outcome prediction is limited and often incorrect; best practice recommends aggressive therapy for at least 72 h (absent brain death). TBI is now recognized as a chronic, dynamic condition, with meaningful improvement or decline observed 2-7 years post-injury regardless of initial severity. TBI (mild to severe) is associated with a 63-96% increased risk of all-cause dementia, with risk greatest after severe injury; TBI with LOC >1 h has been linked to subsequent Parkinson's disease and Lewy body accumulation.
# Severity classification
Glasgow Coma Scale-based grading: mild/concussion GCS 13-15, moderate GCS 9-12, severe GCS 3-8 (scores range 3-15). Mild TBI with CT and/or MRI abnormality is termed 'complicated mTBI' and carries a higher risk of unfavorable outcome. Loss of consciousness and posttraumatic amnesia duration further characterize severity. CTE neuropathology has been proposed to be staged as 'low' or 'high' severity (criteria not yet established).
# Clinical pearls
TBI is defined as an externally caused alteration in brain function or other evidence of brain pathology, marked by any of the following: loss/decrease in consciousness, retrograde or posttraumatic amnesia, neurologic deficits, or altered mental state at the moment of injury.
Incidence shows a bimodal age distribution: motor vehicle crashes predominate in younger persons (more often men), while falls are the leading cause in older adults (more often women); roughly 70-90% (most estimates ~85%) of treated TBIs are mild.
Severity is graded with the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS): mild 13-15, moderate 9-12, severe 3-8; the individual eye/verbal/motor components (e.g., E3V4M6) are more informative than the summed total, which should only describe groups.
In moderate-to-severe TBI, GCS plus the duration of loss of consciousness (LOC) and posttraumatic amnesia (PTA) predict outcome well; in mild TBI, LOC and PTA are far less predictive of recovery time and outcome, and postinjury symptom burden is the most robust predictor.
Epidural hematoma classically follows tearing of the middle meningeal artery beneath a temporal-bone fracture, evolves rapidly, and may be preceded by a lucid interval; rapid surgical evacuation usually yields a favorable outcome.
Acute subdural hematoma is usually venous in origin, can occur without major trauma (especially in the elderly or anticoagulated, or from acceleration/whiplash forces), and carries significant morbidity and mortality when surgery is required - in contrast to the generally better-treated epidural hematoma.
Contusions follow coup (under the impact) and contrecoup (antipolar) mechanisms; blunt deceleration typically bruises the orbitofrontal and anterior/basal temporal lobes, and old contusion sites become gliotic scars that are highly epileptogenic.
Traumatic axonal injury (TAI) from shearing forces is among the most common TBI lesions; four or more foci constitute diffuse axonal injury (DAI), which when widespread is proposed to underlie prolonged coma and the vegetative state.
Anosmia and apparent loss of taste occur in ~10% of serious head injuries (especially falls onto the back of the head) from shearing of olfactory filaments at the cribriform plate; persistent bilateral anosmia for several months carries a poor prognosis.
Second-impact syndrome is a rare, often fatal diffuse cerebral swelling that follows a second minor head injury before the first has resolved - a key reason no athlete should return to play until fully asymptomatic and cleared.
