# Irritable bowel syndrome
Irritable bowel syndrome, Gastroenterology.
# Red flags
Weight loss, bleeding or anaemia argue AGAINST IBS — investigate
# Investigations
Diagnosis of exclusion (Rome criteria)
Check alarm features absent
# Pathophysiology
IBS is a multifactorial functional bowel disorder with no structural lesion. Proposed mechanisms include altered GI motor activity (e.g., exaggerated postprandial and distention-evoked colonic contractions, increased high-amplitude propagating contractions with rapid transit in IBS-D), visceral hypersensitivity (lower thresholds to rectal balloon distention with sparing of somatic pain pathways), and disordered brain-gut interaction with central neural dysregulation (greater activation of mid-cingulate and prefrontal cortices on functional imaging). Psychological factors (anxiety, depression, somatization, prior physical/sexual abuse) modulate pain thresholds, and genetic factors such as serotonin transporter (SERT) polymorphisms are implicated. Peripheral contributors include low-grade mucosal immune activation with mast cells, lymphocytes and proinflammatory cytokines (IL-6, IL-1beta, TNF), upregulated TRPV1 sensory channels, increased intestinal permeability ('leaky gut', chiefly in IBS-D and post-infectious IBS), gut dysbiosis (reduced microbial diversity, fewer Bifidobacterium/Faecalibacterium, more Enterobacteriaceae), abnormal serotonin pathways with increased enterochromaffin-cell serotonin in IBS-D, and bile acid malabsorption in up to ~25% of IBS-D.
# Severity classification
Overall symptom severity ranges from mild to severe with significant quality-of-life impairment, but no formal graded severity score is specified.
# Clinical pearls
Rome IV makes IBS a positive symptom-based diagnosis, not purely one of exclusion: recurrent abdominal pain on average at least 1 day/week over the prior 3 months plus at least two of being related to defecation, a change in stool frequency, or a change in stool form, with onset at least 6 months earlier.
Rome IV is stricter than Rome III: it requires pain at least weekly and dropped 'discomfort' as a qualifying symptom; painless diarrhoea or constipation alone does NOT meet criteria.
Abdominal pain is mandatory for the diagnosis; it is typically episodic and crampy, often worsened by eating or emotional stress and relieved by passing flatus or stool.
Subtype by predominant stool form: IBS-C (constipation), IBS-D (diarrhoea), and IBS-M (mixed) — but subtypes are unstable, with about three-quarters of patients changing subtype and ~29% switching between IBS-C and IBS-D over a year.
Onset is usually before age 45 and IBS is two to three times more common in women, who make up ~80% of severe cases.
Symptoms commonly overlap with other functional disorders (fibromyalgia, headache, backache, genitourinary symptoms) and with functional dyspepsia, suggesting a shared, more extensive digestive disorder.
Bloating and a subjective sense of excess gas are common, but most patients actually generate a normal volume of intestinal gas and instead have impaired gas transit/tolerance and proximal reflux of gas.
Malnutrition is exceedingly rare in IBS and the pain almost always occurs only during waking hours, so sleep disruption from pain is unusual.

Infectious diarrhoea

Non infectious mimics: Coeliac disease; Lactase deficiency; Microscopic colitis; Bile acid malabsorption; Inflammatory bowel disease; Hyperthyroidism / hypothyroidism.
# Additional investigations
Complete blood count for most patients
Fecal calprotectin or fecal lactoferrin, and/or C-reactive protein, in IBS-D even without alarm features to help exclude IBD
Serologic testing for coeliac disease in IBS-D where population prevalence is >=1%
Stool examination for ova and parasites in IBS-D when parasitic infection is endemic or suggested by history (e.g., untreated water)
Colonoscopy when alarm features are present, symptom onset is after age 40, or symptoms fail to respond to standard therapy
Random colonic biopsies to exclude microscopic colitis if diarrhoea is persistent/chronic
Hydrogen breath test (or trial of a 3-week lactose-free diet) for suspected lactase deficiency
Glucose hydrogen breath test for suspected small-bowel bacterial overgrowth with excessive gas/bloating
Gallbladder ultrasonography for postprandial right-upper-quadrant pain
Upper GI radiographs or upper endoscopy when dyspeptic symptoms coexist
# Red flags
First appearance of symptoms in old age
Progressive course from the time of onset
Rectal bleeding
Persistent diarrhoea after a 48-hour fast
Nocturnal diarrhoea
Steatorrhoeal stools
Anaemia, elevated sedimentation rate, or leukocytes/blood in stool
Stool volume >200-300 mL/day
Family history of colorectal malignancy (lowers threshold for thorough evaluation)
# Pitfalls
Treating IBS as a diagnosis of exclusion only and over-investigating: few tests are needed when symptoms are typical and alarm features are absent, and unnecessary investigations may be costly and even harmful.
Missing organic disease in higher-risk patients: older age, rapidly progressive symptoms, or alarm features should prompt a thorough work-up to exclude IBD, coeliac disease and colorectal cancer rather than labelling as IBS.
Attributing bloating to excess gas when most patients produce a normal gas volume but have impaired gas transit.
Over-interpreting an abnormal hydrogen breath test, which can be positive simply from rapid small-bowel transit and does not reliably predict treatment response.

Chronic abdominal pain related to defecation with altered bowel habit and no alarm features; positive symptom-based (Rome IV) diagnosis after excluding organic disease.

# Steps
Confirm alarm features absent (weight loss, bleeding, anaemia, nocturnal symptoms, onset >50) and basic tests normal.
Diet/lifestyle: regular meals, fibre adjustment, trial of low-FODMAP with dietitian; address triggers.
Symptom-targeted drugs: antispasmodics for pain, laxatives for IBS-C, loperamide for IBS-D.
Low-dose tricyclic or SSRI for pain/global symptoms; psychological therapies (CBT/gut-directed hypnotherapy).
Reassurance and a positive diagnosis; review and safety-net.
Positive diagnosis + symptom-targeted therapy (outpatient)
