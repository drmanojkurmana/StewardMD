# Staphylococcal Infections
Staphylococcal Infections, Infectious Diseases.
Aliases: Staph aureus infection; MRSA.
# Pathophysiology
S. aureus expresses cell wall-anchored adhesins called MSCRAMMs (microbial surface components recognizing adhesive matrix molecules, e.g., SdrC) that mediate binding to host extracellular-matrix proteins and underpin adherence and colonization. Like other extracellular pathogens it can form biofilms, which contribute to device- and prosthesis-associated infection. Disease is driven partly by potent secreted toxins. Superantigens — including toxic shock syndrome toxin-1 (TSST-1) and the staphylococcal enterotoxins — bind invariant regions of MHC class II molecules and the T-cell receptor, nonspecifically activating vast numbers of T cells and triggering a cytokine storm responsible for fever, rash, and the shock of TSS. S. aureus also produces an erythrotoxin causing scarlatiniform rash and exfoliative toxin ("exfoliatin") that cleaves the superficial epidermis to produce bullous impetigo and SSSS. It elaborates several pore-forming toxins — leukocidins, alpha- and gamma-hemolysins, and phenol-soluble modulins — that lyse host cells, protect the organism from phagocytosis, and impair development of protective adaptive immunity. Methicillin resistance results from acquisition of the mobile mecA gene carried on the SCCmec cassette, which has spread widely through the S. aureus population.
# Prognosis
Outcome varies sharply by syndrome. For TSS, reported mortality is about 5% for menstruation-associated and 10-15% for nonmenstrual disease (compared with 30-70% for streptococcal TSS). Acute bacterial endocarditis, including native-valve S. aureus/MRSA disease, carries mortality of roughly 10-40%, and older patients with S. aureus endocarditis often present with nonspecific symptoms that delay diagnosis and worsen prognosis. Rapid recognition and intervention are crucial across invasive staphylococcal syndromes.
# Severity classification
No single graded severity scale is presented; staphylococcal disease is categorized by clinical syndrome and invasiveness — superficial skin/soft-tissue infection (impetigo, furunculosis), toxin-mediated syndromes (TSS, SSSS, scarlatiniform eruption), and invasive disease (bacteremia, infective endocarditis, osteomyelitis, septic intracranial/epidural infection). TSS is defined by formal clinical criteria (fever, diffuse erythroderma, hypotension, multiorgan involvement with mucocutaneous features).
# Clinical pearls
Staphylococcus aureus causes a wide spectrum of disease, ranging from localized skin and soft-tissue infection to invasive bloodstream, endocardial, and bone infection and to toxin-mediated syndromes; methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) is now common in both community and health-care settings.
Furunculosis (boils) is caused by S. aureus and has risen in prominence with community-acquired MRSA (CA-MRSA); patients frequently mistake furuncles for spider or insect bites, and family members or close contacts may also be affected.
Whenever possible, send lesional material from staphylococcal skin infection for culture; incision and drainage may be adequate therapy for a small solitary furuncle without surrounding cellulitis or systemic symptoms.
Impetigo is caused most often by S. aureus (and sometimes group A streptococci) and forms a characteristic honey-colored crust; bullous impetigo, with tense clear bullae, is produced by S. aureus exfoliative toxin (phage type II).
In staphylococcal scalded-skin syndrome (SSSS) the site of staphylococcal infection is usually extracutaneous (e.g., conjunctivitis, rhinorrhea, otitis media, pharyngitis) and the skin lesions themselves are sterile, whereas in bullous impetigo the skin lesions are the actual site of infection.
Toxic shock syndrome (TSS) is defined by clinical criteria — fever, diffuse macular (sunburn-type) erythroderma, hypotension, and multiorgan involvement — with three criteria involving mucocutaneous sites (diffuse skin erythema, desquamation of palms and soles 1-2 weeks after onset, and mucous-membrane hyperemia of the vagina, oropharynx, or conjunctivae).
In staphylococcal TSS, the vagina or a postoperative wound is typically colonized rather than overtly infected, and mucosal areas appear hyperemic but not infected; this contrasts with streptococcal TSS, which is more often linked to overt skin/soft-tissue infection and bacteremia.
Acute bacterial endocarditis from S. aureus follows a far more aggressive course than subacute endocarditis, can attack previously normal native valves, and native-valve S. aureus (including MRSA) endocarditis is increasing in frequency.
Coagulase-negative staphylococci are skin commensals; a single positive blood-culture bottle for skin flora (especially one taking >24 h to turn positive) should raise suspicion of contamination or catheter colonization rather than true bloodstream infection, and cultures should be repeated from both central and peripheral sites.
# Additional differentials
Group A streptococcal infection (overlaps with staphylococci in impetigo, cellulitis, necrotizing fasciitis, and toxic shock syndrome)
Streptococcal toxic shock syndrome (more often with overt soft-tissue infection/necrotizing fasciitis and bacteremia, less prominent exanthem)
Toxic epidermal necrolysis and severe drug eruptions (in the differential of widespread skin blistering versus SSSS)
Kawasaki disease and scarlet fever (other causes of scarlatiniform rash with palm/sole desquamation)
# Infection mimics
Streptococcal toxic shock syndrome
Scarlet fever (group A streptococcal erythrogenic toxin)
Clostridium sordellii toxic shock (childbirth-associated or black-tar heroin injection; fever typically absent)
# Non infectious mimics
Toxic epidermal necrolysis / severe drug eruption (mimics SSSS)
Kawasaki disease (polymorphous rash, conjunctival injection, desquamation; clues are cervical lymphadenopathy, cheilitis, thrombocytosis)
SARS-CoV-2-associated multisystem inflammatory syndrome in children
# Additional investigations
Blood cultures, repeated from both central and peripheral sites, to confirm bacteremia and distinguish true infection from skin-flora contamination/catheter colonization (differential time-to-positivity can localize a catheter source)
Frozen section of the blister roof or exfoliative cytology of blister contents to distinguish SSSS from toxic epidermal necrolysis rapidly
Culture of lesional material from skin/soft-tissue infection to guide therapy and detect MRSA
mecA gene detection as the genotypic basis of methicillin resistance, enabling rapid genotypic MRSA identification
Double-disk diffusion (D-zone) test to detect inducible clindamycin resistance when an isolate is erythromycin-resistant but clindamycin-susceptible
Echocardiography and a search for metastatic foci in S. aureus bacteremia to evaluate for endocarditis
# Red flags
S. aureus bacteremia warrants an active search for infective endocarditis and metastatic/seeded foci, because any primary focus can give rise to bloodstream infection with secondary seeding.
In TSS, rapid hypotension developing within hours of symptom onset, and early renal failure preceding hypotension, are warning features (early renal failure helps distinguish TSS from other septic shock syndromes).
Acute S. aureus endocarditis: a changing murmur, congestive heart failure, rapid valvular destruction (especially aortic) with pulmonary edema and hypotension, myocardial abscess with conduction block/arrhythmia, and large friable vegetations causing arterial emboli, metastatic infection, or stroke.
Widespread skin blistering or radial perioral crusting with diffuse tenderness suggests SSSS and demands prompt evaluation.
S. aureus among organisms that can present as intractable hypotension/septic shock with a primary soft-tissue focus.
# Pitfalls
Attributing a single positive blood culture for coagulase-negative staphylococci to true bacteremia when it may represent skin-flora contamination or catheter colonization — repeat paired cultures and assess time-to-positivity.
Assuming a primary skin focus is present in staphylococcal TSS; colonization (vaginal or wound) without overt infection is typical, so the portal may be subtle.
Missing the diagnosis of S. aureus endocarditis in older patients, whose nonspecific presentation delays recognition.
Confusing SSSS with toxic epidermal necrolysis or severe drug eruption; SSSS occurs mainly in infants/toddlers, is more superficial, spares the oral mucosa, and is toxin- rather than drug-mediated.
Inducible clindamycin resistance can cause treatment failure in apparently clindamycin-susceptible isolates unless the double-disk (D-zone) test is performed.
# Management
Incision and drainage for abscesses and furuncles, which may be sufficient for a small solitary lesion without surrounding cellulitis or systemic features
Anti-staphylococcal antibiotics guided by susceptibility: beta-lactams such as flucloxacillin for methicillin-susceptible disease, and agents such as vancomycin for MRSA
Prompt removal or replacement of infected prosthetic devices and intravascular catheters, given biofilm-associated infection
For toxic shock syndrome, remove the focus or retained tampon, give aggressive fluid resuscitation and organ support, add a protein-synthesis-inhibiting antibiotic such as clindamycin to suppress toxin production, and consider intravenous immunoglobulin
Echocardiography and a search for metastatic foci in S. aureus bacteraemia, with prolonged intravenous therapy for endocarditis or deep-seated infection
Supportive skin and fluid care for scalded-skin syndrome, treating the extracutaneous focus of infection
Decolonisation and hygiene measures for recurrent furunculosis, extended to affected close contacts to limit spread
