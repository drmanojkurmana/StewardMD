# Pathogens
Device-related infections are dominated by skin-flora gram-positive organisms (coagulase-negative staphylococci, S. aureus) that colonize the device surface and form biofilm, with Candida becoming relevant in patients with prolonged central access and risk factors such as TPN or broad-spectrum antibiotic exposure.

# Pathophysiology
Cardiac implantable electronic devices (pacemakers, implantable defibrillators, cardiac resynchronization devices) and other prosthetic intravascular hardware provide an avascular foreign surface that organisms colonize and on which they persist. Infection localizes to the generator pocket and/or the intravascular leads, and may extend to the endothelium at points of lead contact, producing endocarditis (CIED-IE). Staphylococci dominate because skin flora is the principal portal; S. aureus and coagulase-negative staphylococci cause the majority of CIED-IE cases. Once organisms establish on the foreign material, antimicrobial therapy alone usually fails to eradicate them, because the retained device continues to act as a nidus for persistent or recurrent infection.
# Prognosis
Removal of the infected CIED during the initial hospitalization is associated with increased 30-day and 1-year survival compared with antibiotic therapy and device retention. Attempted salvage of an infected device with antibiotics alone plus long-term suppressive therapy is usually unsuccessful and is reserved only for patients whose devices cannot be removed or who decline removal; left in place, the device remains a nidus for persistent or recurrent infection.
# Clinical pearls
CIED-related endocarditis (CIED-IE) involves either the device hardware itself or the endothelium at points of device contact, and occasionally coexists with concurrent valvular infection.
The timing of CIED-IE is split roughly into thirds: about one-third present within 3 months of implantation or manipulation, one-third between 3 and 12 months, and one-third more than a year afterward.
CIED infection can be obvious from a generator pocket infection (especially within 6 months of device manipulation) or cryptic, arising through bacteremic seeding without any visible pocket involvement.
Risk of device-associated endocarditis is reported as higher on implanted defibrillators and resynchronization devices than on permanent pacemakers.
In a patient with a CIED, an unexplained Staphylococcus aureus bacteremia (and persistent coagulase-negative staphylococcal bacteremia) without another source is likely to indicate CIED-IE or valvular IE and should be investigated and managed as such.
Relapse of bloodstream infection after a course of antimicrobial therapy in a CIED patient increases the likelihood that the device is the source and warrants treatment as CIED-IE.
S. aureus device-related infections tend to be acute with both local and systemic manifestations and to progress more rapidly than the indolent course seen in non-S. aureus staphylococcal infections; a pyogenic collection at the device site is relatively common.
# Additional differentials
Left-sided native-valve or prosthetic-valve endocarditis (may coexist with or be mistaken for CIED-IE)
Bloodstream infection from an extracardiac focus (e.g., gram-negative, streptococcal, or enterococcal bacteremia) not arising from the device
Remote-site metastatic infection complicating bacteremic CIED infection, such as vertebral osteomyelitis
# Infection mimics
Non-device bloodstream infection from a separate source presenting with fever and sepsis in a CIED patient
# Additional investigations
Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE): detects vegetations in >90% of patients with definite IE; an initial study may be falsely negative in a minority, so a negative TEE when IE is likely should be repeated in 7-10 days rather than used to exclude the diagnosis.
Three or more separate blood culture sets (two-bottle, ~10 mL per bottle) from different venipuncture sites; if negative at 48-72 h, obtain additional sets and consult the lab for optimal techniques.
FDG-PET/CT: less sensitive than TEE for intracardiac pathology in CIED-IE but useful for assessing CIED pocket and lead infection and extracardiac/remote complications.
Aspiration of any pyogenic collection at the device/pocket site, combined with blood cultures, to establish the diagnosis.
Echocardiographic assessment is specifically indicated in monomicrobial S. aureus bacteremia when an intracardiac device is present.
# Red flags
S. aureus bacteremia in a patient with a CIED with no alternative source
Persistent or relapsing bacteremia after antimicrobial therapy in a device patient
Generator pocket inflammation/pyogenic collection at the device site
Lead vegetation >2 cm, which carries a risk of pulmonary embolism
# Pitfalls
Treating an infected CIED with antibiotics alone (salvage/suppression) usually fails and should not be the default approach.
Using a negative TEE to rule out CIED-IE when clinical suspicion is high, rather than repeating the study in 7-10 days.
In critically ill patients already on antibiotics for other indications, blood cultures may be negative and device-related endocarditis missed.
Assuming every bloodstream infection in a CIED patient indicates the device: gram-negative, streptococcal, or enterococcal bacteremia from an extracardiac focus may not be device-related, though relapse afterward raises suspicion for CIED-IE.
# Deescalation
Narrow to the most targeted agent once organism and susceptibility are confirmed; duration is determined by organism and whether endocarditis/deep complications are excluded by echocardiography and clinical course.

# Framework
Device removal or exchange is frequently necessary for source control, particularly for S. aureus or Candida — antibiotics alone often fail with the device retained, especially for biofilm-forming organisms.
For suspected prosthetic valve or CIED (pacemaker/ICD) infection specifically, infectious disease and cardiology/cardiac surgery input is essential — these infections often require device extraction.
Document duration of bacteremia after line removal — persistent positive cultures despite removal should prompt evaluation for endocarditis or other deep-seated infection.
