# Aortic dissection
Aortic dissection, Vascular emergency.
# Red flags
Tearing chest/back pain with pulse/BP differential — emergency imaging
# Investigations
CT aortogram (urgent)
BP in both arms
Control HR & BP
# Pathophysiology
Dissection begins with a tear of the intima, most often along the high-shear right lateral wall of the ascending aorta or in the descending thoracic aorta just below the ligamentum arteriosum. Pulsatile flow dissects along the elastic lamellar plates of the media to create a false lumen; the initiating event may be a primary intimal tear with secondary dissection into the media, or a medial hemorrhage that secondarily disrupts the intima. The dissection usually propagates distally (sometimes proximally), may be limited distally by atherosclerotic plaque, and can re-enter the true lumen through a secondary distal intimal disruption. Underlying medial degeneration and conditions that raise aortic wall stress predispose to dissection. Two related radiologic/pathologic variants are intramural hematoma (rupture of vasa vasorum bleeding into the wall, without an intimal flap) and penetrating atherosclerotic ulcer (plaque erosion into the media); both can progress to dissection and rupture.
# Prognosis
In-hospital mortality after surgical treatment of dissection is reported at about 15-25%; for medically treated uncomplicated type B dissection it is about 12%. Major perioperative causes of death and morbidity include myocardial infarction, paraplegia, renal failure, tamponade, hemorrhage, and sepsis. With careful follow-up the long-term outlook after discharge is generally good, with roughly 60% 10-year survival; patients with Marfan syndrome are at high risk for post-dissection complications.
# Severity classification
Stanford classification: type A involves the ascending aorta (proximal dissection) regardless of tear site or distal extension; type B is limited to the arch and/or descending aorta (distal dissection). DeBakey classification: type I — intimal tear in ascending aorta with propagation to arch, descending and possibly abdominal aorta; type II — limited to the ascending aorta; type III — tear in the descending aorta with distal propagation. Stanford A/B is more practical for management since DeBakey I and II are managed similarly.
# Clinical pearls
Pain characteristically migrates as the dissection propagates, often shifting to the interscapular region of the back.
Systemic hypertension coexists in about 70% of patients with aortic dissection.
Peak incidence is in the sixth and seventh decades, with men affected about twice as often as women.
A normal ECG showing no ischemia helps distinguish dissection from myocardial infarction in a patient with chest pain, though dissection can rarely involve a coronary ostium and cause an actual MI.
On chest x-ray, ascending dissection often produces a widened superior mediastinum; a pleural effusion (usually left-sided) may appear and is typically serosanguineous rather than a sign of rupture unless accompanied by hypotension and a falling hematocrit.
Although 'tearing' or 'ripping' pain is classic, acute aortic emergencies also commonly present with knifelike pain.
Stanford type A or B classification is more practical for management than DeBakey, because DeBakey types I and II are managed similarly.
