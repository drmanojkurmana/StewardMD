An adrenal incidentaloma is a mass found by accident on imaging ordered for another complaint, and no lump earns the label silent until hormones are checked. Every patient gets three screens: an overnight dexamethasone suppression test for cortisol excess, plasma or 24 hour urinary fractionated metanephrines for pheochromocytoma, and a plasma aldosterone to renin ratio for aldosteronism. Functioning lesions go to surgery or tablets while true nonfunctioning adenomas under 4 cm are watched with repeat scans.

Plain CT density separates fat filled adenomas from the rest. Benign cortical adenomas carry abundant intracytoplasmic lipid, so unenhanced attenuation under 10 Hounsfield units (HU) is pathognomonic, and absolute contrast washout above 60% confirms benignity even when density sits higher. Lesions above 20 HU with absolute washout under 40% behave suspiciously and need cancer workup rather than reassurance.

Pheochromocytoma grows from chromaffin cells of the adrenal medulla and announces itself with paroxysmal hypertension plus headache, sweating, and palpitations. The diagnostic standard is plasma free fractionated metanephrines or 24 hour urinary fractionated metanephrines and normetanephrines, the breakdown products of adrenaline and noradrenaline, elevated 3 to 4 fold above normal, since minor rises follow stress, tricyclics, and cocaine. Imaging with CT or MIBG follows only after biochemistry convicts.

No pheochromocytoma is touched without alpha blockade first. Phenoxybenzamine starts at 10 mg twice daily and titrates over 10 to 14 days until orthostatic hypotension, nasal stuffiness, and tingling scalp mark adequate blockade, with volume loading alongside. Beta blockade for tachycardia starts only after alpha cover is established, because beta first leaves alpha vasoconstriction unopposed and precipitates hypertensive crisis.

Primary hyperaldosteronism, or Conn syndrome, pairs hypertension with hypokalemia and metabolic alkalosis from autonomous aldosterone. Screening divides morning plasma aldosterone by renin activity, and a ratio above 20 to 30 earns confirmatory salt loading or saline infusion testing. Adrenal vein sampling (AVS) then lateralizes: unilateral adenoma goes to laparoscopic adrenalectomy while bilateral hyperplasia takes lifelong mineralocorticoid receptor blockade.

Adrenocortical carcinoma (ACC) is large, dense, and slow to wash. Typical flags are size above 4 cm to 6 cm, unenhanced attenuation above 20 HU, absolute washout under 40%, and heterogeneous texture with necrosis, haemorrhage, and calcification. These masses proceed to open oncologic resection with en bloc margins and lymphadenectomy, and mitotane based chemotherapy follows for advanced disease.

Laparoscopic adrenalectomy through transperitoneal or retroperitoneal routes is the gold standard for benign functioning tumours and solitary adrenal metastases under 6 cm, giving less pain and faster discharge than open surgery. Posterior retroperitoneal access suits small bilateral disease and prior abdominal surgery, while suspected carcinoma with local invasion still demands open resection.

Percutaneous adrenal biopsy is forbidden in suspected pheochromocytoma and in resectable adrenocortical carcinoma, where the needle risks hypertensive crisis or tumour seeding along its track. Sampling is reserved for confirming metastatic deposits from a known extra adrenal primary when histology would redirect systemic therapy, and pheochromocytoma must be excluded biochemically first.
