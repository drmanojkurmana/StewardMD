# Pathophysiology
DRESS is a severe, delayed T-cell-mediated drug hypersensitivity reaction, thought to arise from impaired drug detoxification pathways or specific HLA associations that lead to a robust immune response against drug-modified proteins. The resulting systemic activation of eosinophils and lymphocytes drives multi-organ inflammation. Reactivation of latent herpesviruses, particularly HHV-6, is commonly observed and may amplify and prolong the immune response, contributing to the characteristic waxing-waning course.
# Prognosis
DRESS carries a mortality risk of roughly 5-10%, primarily related to fulminant hepatic, cardiac, or pulmonary involvement. Even after recovery, patients can develop delayed autoimmune sequelae such as thyroiditis, type 1 diabetes, or other autoimmune conditions over the following months to years, warranting longitudinal follow-up.
# Clinical pearls
Onset is classically delayed, typically 2-8 weeks after starting the culprit drug, distinguishing it from more immediate hypersensitivity reactions.
Look for the triad of fever, widespread morbilliform (often confluent and edematous) rash, and facial edema, especially periorbital swelling.
Systemic involvement is the hallmark: lymphadenopathy plus at least one internal organ (liver most common, also kidney, lung, heart) helps separate DRESS from simple drug rash.
Peripheral eosinophilia and/or atypical lymphocytosis on blood smear strongly support the diagnosis but may lag behind skin findings.
Common culprits include aromatic anticonvulsants, allopurinol, sulfonamides, and certain antibiotics — always take a careful medication history covering the prior 2 months.
