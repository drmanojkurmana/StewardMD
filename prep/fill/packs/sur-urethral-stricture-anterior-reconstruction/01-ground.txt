Short bulbar strictures are excised and rejoined. Bulbar narrowings under 2 cm without dense spongiofibrosis are repaired by excision and primary anastomosis, transecting or non-transecting, with success above 90 percent. Removing the scarred ring entirely gives the highest durable patency of any option, which is why length and tissue quality decide the operation.

Longer or penile strictures need substitution, not excision. Defects over 2 cm or any penile urethral involvement require substitution urethroplasty, and autologous buccal mucosa graft is the gold standard material. Cheek lining is wet, tough, hairless, and rich in capillaries, so it survives in the hostile urethral bed better than any skin graft.

The dorsal bed holds grafts best. The Barbagli dorsal onlay fixes the buccal graft directly onto the tunica albuginea of the corpora cavernosa, a flat, immobile, highly vascular surface that gives excellent graft take. Quilting the graft to the corpora prevents hematoma lifting it off, and the rigid backing keeps the new lumen open during healing.

Ventral placement suits the proximal bulb. Where thick healthy spongiosum overlies the proximal bulbar urethra, a ventral onlay graft gains tension-free coverage from the spongiosum itself. This route avoids mobilizing the urethra off its dorsal neurovascular bundles, trading a slightly less rigid bed for a less disruptive dissection.

Lichen sclerosus forbids skin flaps. This chronic inflammatory dermatosis, also called balanitis xerotica obliterans, produces dense panurethral and meatal strictures, and genital skin flaps are strictly contraindicated because the disease attacks transplanted skin too. Buccal mucosa or staged repairs are used instead, since cheek lining resists the disease.

The penile urethra must never be shortened. Excision and primary anastomosis is forbidden in penile strictures because removing urethral length causes chordee and penile shortening on erection. Substitution urethroplasty with buccal graft is mandatory here, no matter how short the stricture looks, since even a centimetre of penile loss bends the organ.

Destroyed beds are rebuilt in two stages. The Johanson or Bracka two-stage urethroplasty lays a free graft onto the opened urethral plate first and tubularizes it months later, and it is indicated for complex redo strictures, failed hypospadias repairs, and severe lichen sclerosus. Staging lets the graft mature its blood supply before it is asked to carry urine under pressure.

Internal urethrotomy rarely cures. Direct vision internal urethrotomy has a definitive long-term cure rate under 10 to 20 percent once repeated, because the cut simply scars back down. Guidelines strongly discourage repeating it beyond a single failure, and recurrent strictures after one failed urethrotomy should go to formal urethroplasty instead of a second cut.
