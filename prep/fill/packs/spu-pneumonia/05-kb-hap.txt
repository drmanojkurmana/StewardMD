# Hospital Acquired Pneumonia
Hospital Acquired Pneumonia, Respiratory.
# Pathogens
HAP arising after 48 hours of hospitalization reflects a shift toward hospital flora, which is enriched for gram-negative organisms with higher resistance rates, particularly in patients with prior antibiotic exposure or ICU stay.
Very likely: Pseudomonas aeruginosa; Klebsiella pneumoniae; Escherichia coli.
Likely: Staphylococcus aureus (including MRSA); Acinetobacter baumannii.
# Possible
Enterobacter species
Other MDR gram-negatives based on local ICU flora
# Investigations
Sputum/endotracheal aspirate culture, Essential to identify the causative organism and de-escalate from broad empiric therapy.
Blood cultures, Identifies bacteremic spread and an organism for targeted therapy.
Chest X-ray, Confirms new infiltrate consistent with pneumonia.
Procalcitonin (serial), Supports decisions about duration and early discontinuation if levels fall rapidly with clinical improvement.
Antibiotics recommended
# Reason
New fever, cough and radiographic infiltrate developing after 48 hours of hospitalization meet criteria for hospital-acquired pneumonia. The nosocomial setting shifts pathogen likelihood toward resistant gram-negatives, warranting empiric therapy that accounts for local resistance patterns and prior antibiotic exposure.
# Pathophysiology
Three factors are central to VAP pathogenesis: colonisation of the oropharynx with pathogenic organisms, aspiration of those organisms into the lower respiratory tract, and compromise of host defences. The endotracheal tube is the single most important risk factor: it bypasses normal anti-aspiration mechanisms, and although it blocks large-volume aspiration, microaspiration is worsened by secretions pooling above the cuff. The tube and suctioning damage tracheal mucosa and facilitate colonisation, and pathogenic bacteria form a protective glycocalyx biofilm on the tube surface that shields them from antibiotics and host defences; biofilm fragments can embolise to distal airways or be dislodged during suctioning to reinoculate the trachea. Normal oropharyngeal flora is frequently replaced by pathogenic organisms under antibiotic selection pressure, cross-infection, severe systemic illness, and malnutrition. Almost all intubated patients have transient colonisation, but progression to pneumonia reflects a large bacterial inoculum overwhelming host defences amid post-admission immunoparalysis. More broadly, pneumonia is now viewed as an emergent disturbance of the lung microbiota driven by self-accelerating inflammatory feedback rather than simple invasion of a sterile space.
# Prognosis
Crude VAP mortality is reported as high as 50-70%, but the key measure is attributable mortality, since many VAP patients have underlying disease that would itself be fatal. Attributable mortality exceeded 25% in one matched-cohort study, whereas more recent estimates are much lower (5-10%); VAP complicating COVID-19 carries higher attributable mortality than other VAP. VAP in trauma patients shows no attributable mortality, likely because many were previously healthy. MDR pathogens carry significantly greater attributable mortality than non-MDR organisms, and some pathogens (e.g., S. maltophilia) chiefly mark a severely immunocompromised, high-risk host. Nonintubated non-ICU HAP has lower mortality and lower risk of antibiotic failure than VAP and ventilated HAP because of better host defences.
# Severity classification
The frequency of VAP also varies with duration of ventilation, with the highest hazard in the first 5 days, a plateau (~1%/day) after about 2 weeks, and a cumulative rate up to ~70% by 30 days of ventilation.
