# Community Acquired Pneumonia (non-severe)
Community Acquired Pneumonia (non-severe), Respiratory.
# Pathogens
Outpatient/ward-level CAP without severity criteria is overwhelmingly community-acquired and typical-organism predominant. S. pneumoniae remains the leading cause across age groups; H. influenzae is more frequent in smokers and COPD. Atypicals (Mycoplasma, Chlamydophila) are added because clinical and radiographic findings cannot reliably distinguish typical from atypical pneumonia.
Very likely: Streptococcus pneumoniae; Haemophilus influenzae.
Likely: Mycoplasma pneumoniae; Chlamydophila pneumoniae.
Possible: Legionella pneumophila; Respiratory viruses (influenza, RSV).

# Pathophysiology
Pneumonia results from proliferation of microbial pathogens in the alveoli together with the host inflammatory response. Organisms reach the lower respiratory tract chiefly by microaspiration of oropharyngeal flora, but also by inhalation and direct mucosal spread; mechanical defenses (nasal hairs/turbinates, branching airways, mucociliary clearance, gag and cough reflexes) are imperfect barriers. Rather than simple invasion of a sterile space, current models view pneumonia as an emergent disturbance of the lung microbiota driven by self-reinforcing positive-feedback loops: an inciting epithelial/endothelial injury (often a viral infection plus microaspiration) releases cytokines, chemokines, and catecholamines that can selectively favor growth of organisms such as S. pneumoniae and P. aeruginosa, amplifying inflammation. When innate and adaptive immunity fail to contain this, mediators (IL-6 and TNF cause fever; IL-8 and G-CSF recruit neutrophils) drive an alveolar-capillary leak producing hypoxemia and radiographic infiltrates. Bacterial toxins amplify inflammation, and some pathogens blunt hypoxic vasoconstriction, worsening hypoxemia; reduced compliance, increased secretions, raised respiratory drive, and intrapulmonary shunting cause dyspnea and, when severe, respiratory failure. Pathologically, classic (lobar pneumococcal) pneumonia passes through edema, red hepatization, gray hepatization, and resolution; a bronchopneumonia pattern predominates in nosocomial disease while a lobar pattern is more typical of bacterial CAP, and viral pneumonias are alveolar rather than interstitial processes.
# Prognosis
Outcome depends on age, comorbidities, and site of care. Young patients without comorbidity generally recover fully in about 2 weeks, whereas older patients or those with comorbid illness may take several weeks longer. Overall mortality is under 5% for outpatients but ranges from roughly 12% to 40% for hospitalized patients depending on the patient category and the timeliness of appropriate antibiotics; in older patients the 1-year mortality after CAP exceeds the 30-day mortality. Fever and leukocytosis typically resolve in 2-4 days in otherwise healthy patients, while chest radiographic clearance is slowest (4-12 weeks). About 18% of hospitalized CAP patients are readmitted within a month.
# Severity classification
Two validated tools guide the site-of-care decision. The Pneumonia Severity Index (PSI) is a prognostic model using 20 variables (age, comorbidities, abnormal exam and laboratory findings) that stratifies patients into five classes with rising mortality (class 1 ~0.1%, class 2 ~0.6%, class 3 ~2.8%, class 4 ~8.2%, class 5 ~29.2%); classes 1-2 are generally suitable for outpatient care and class 3 for an observation unit. CURB-65 scores one point each for Confusion, Urea >7 mmol/L, Respiratory rate >=30/min, low Blood pressure (systolic <=90 or diastolic <=60 mmHg), and age >=65: a score of 0 (30-day mortality ~1.5%) suggests outpatient treatment, 1-2 generally warrants admission (unless driven only by age >=65), and >=3 (mortality ~22%) may need ICU care. PSI is better validated and more discriminating but harder to compute. Severe CAP is defined by either major criterion (invasive mechanical ventilation for respiratory failure, or septic shock requiring vasopressors), mandating ICU admission; presence of at least 3 of 9 minor criteria (RR >=30/min; PaO2/FiO2 <=250; multilobar infiltrates; confusion/disorientation; uremia with BUN >=20 mg/dL; leukopenia <4000/uL; thrombocytopenia <100,000/uL; hypothermia <36 degrees C; hypotension needing aggressive fluids) warrants ICU or high-level monitoring. Independent of these scores, inability to maintain oral intake, concerns about compliance (e.g. cognitive impairment, homelessness), or room-air O2 saturation <92% are indications for hospitalization.
# Clinical pearls
Typical and atypical pathogens cannot be reliably separated on clinical or radiographic grounds because both can produce overlapping syndromes; atypicals (Mycoplasma, Chlamydia, Legionella) are not seen on Gram stain or grown on standard media and are intrinsically resistant to beta-lactams.
In more than half of CAP cases no specific pathogen is identified despite history, examination, and imaging; in one large emergency-department series an etiology was established in under 8%.
Physical examination has limited diagnostic accuracy in pneumonia (sensitivity ~58%, specificity ~67%), so a compatible clinical picture plus a new infiltrate on chest film remains the diagnostic standard.
In the elderly the presentation may be subtle, manifesting as new or worsening confusion or decompensation of a chronic illness with few classic respiratory findings.
Gross hemoptysis raises concern for necrotizing pneumonia, such as that caused by community-associated MRSA.
Up to about 20% of patients have gastrointestinal symptoms (nausea, vomiting, diarrhea) in addition to constitutional and pulmonary findings.
Aspiration pneumonia sits on a continuum with CAP/HAP and accounts for roughly 5-15% of CAP cases; modern organisms mirror the usual CAP/HAP flora, with anaerobes mainly relevant in poor dentition, lung abscess, necrotizing pneumonia, or empyema.
Epidemiologic exposures can flag specific organisms (e.g. birds for Chlamydia psittaci/Histoplasma, recent hotel or cruise stay for Legionella, local influenza activity for influenza/pneumococcus/S. aureus).
CAP can be complicated by cardiovascular events (heart failure, arrhythmia, myocardial infarction, stroke), especially in the elderly with pneumococcal or influenza pneumonia, attributed to increased myocardial load and inflammation-driven plaque destabilization, and these events may occur acutely or up to a year later.

# Additional investigations
Sputum Gram stain and culture (recommended chiefly for hospitalized patients, particularly severe cases or those at risk for MRSA or P. aeruginosa; an adequate specimen needs >25 neutrophils and <10 squamous epithelial cells per low-power field)
Blood cultures (reserved for high-risk patients such as neutropenia, asplenia, complement deficiency, chronic liver disease, or severe CAP, and those at risk for MRSA/P. aeruginosa; yield only ~5-14% in hospitalized CAP)
Pneumococcal and Legionella urinary antigen tests (reserved for severe cases or relevant epidemiology; Legionella test detects only serogroup 1)
Multiplex respiratory PCR / nasopharyngeal swab PCR (standard for viral diagnosis including influenza and coronaviruses; can also detect Legionella, M. pneumoniae, C. pneumoniae, mycobacteria)
Procalcitonin and C-reactive protein (adjuncts for stewardship only; PCT is not accurate enough to diagnose bacterial CAP or to withhold initial antibiotics, and CRP is even less sensitive)
CT chest (for suspected loculated effusion, cavitary disease, or postobstructive pneumonia from tumor or foreign body)
Diagnostic pleural fluid sampling for a significant effusion
Follow-up chest radiograph ~4-6 weeks after hospitalization (not routinely needed in well, nonsmoking outpatients whose symptoms resolve in 5-7 days)
# Red flags
Septic shock requiring vasopressors or respiratory failure requiring invasive mechanical ventilation (major severe-CAP criteria mandating direct ICU admission)
Room-air oxygen saturation <92%
Confusion or disorientation, including new or worsening confusion in the elderly
Respiratory rate >=30 breaths/min
Multilobar infiltrates
Hypotension requiring aggressive fluid resuscitation
Gross hemoptysis suggesting necrotizing pneumonia
Failure to improve by ~day 3 of therapy, prompting reassessment for resistant or unexpected pathogen, sequestered focus (abscess/empyema), or a noninfectious mimic
# Pitfalls
Treating a macrolide as adequate empirical monotherapy where high-level pneumococcal macrolide resistance is prevalent risks treatment failure.
Relying on chest radiography or clinical features to determine etiology is unreliable; imaging often cannot distinguish CAP from other conditions and seldom yields a specific organism.
Using procalcitonin or CRP in isolation to decide on or withhold antibiotics is inappropriate, as neither is sufficiently accurate for diagnosing bacterial CAP.
Neither PSI nor CURB-65 is designed to determine the need for ICU admission, so they should not be used for that purpose.
Delayed ICU transfer of a deteriorating ward patient is associated with higher mortality than initial ICU monitoring of equally ill patients.
Inability to expectorate sputum may reflect dehydration rather than absence of disease; rehydration can yield a productive sample and a more apparent infiltrate.
Pneumonia that relapses or recurs in the same lung segment should prompt evaluation for an underlying neoplasm or focal abnormality such as bronchiectasis.
# Deescalation
If sputum or blood culture identifies a specific organism with known susceptibility, narrow to the most targeted agent (e.g., penicillin-susceptible S. pneumoniae → amoxicillin alone, discontinuing macrolide).
