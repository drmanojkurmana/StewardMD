The NIH prostatitis classification sorts disease into Category I to IV: I acute bacterial, II chronic bacterial, III pelvic pain split into IIIA inflammatory and IIIB non inflammatory, and IV silent inflammation, with each box taking different drugs. Acute bacterial prostatitis strikes with high fever, perineal pain, and severe voiding symptoms, and clinicians must avoid vigorous DRE, no hard prostate squeeze, since massage showers bacteria into blood and precipitates urosepsis; broad antibiotics start after cultures without delay.

In retention from acute prostatitis, clinicians must avoid urethral catheter, no tube through the gland, draining instead by fine suprapubic decompression, a belly needle under ultrasound left till flow returns, since urethral instrumentation seeds bacteraemia. Chronic bacterial prostatitis needs fluoroquinolones such as ciprofloxacin 500 mg twice daily, or trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, co-trimoxazole, for 4 to 6 weeks to cross the prostatic lipid barrier, the oily germ shield, since short courses relapse. The Meares-Stamey test, the four-glass test of VB1 first void, VB2 midstream, EPS expressed prostate fluid after massage, and VB3 post massage urine, localises by counts and microscopy before chronic labelling, discounting skin contaminants.

Prostatic abscess, a prostate pus pocket, complicates uncontrolled infection in diabetics and the immunocompromised, shown by TRUS diagnosis of multilocular fluid on rectal ultrasound or CT, with swinging fever despite drugs; aspirate under cover rather than awaiting rupture. Collections over 1.5 cm to 2 cm take abscess aspiration under ultrasound guidance or transurethral unroofing through TURP roofing removal, with antibiotics alongside and re-imaging for residual locules. Urethral caruncle, a meatal berry of the posterior lip in women, is a painful bleeding benign vascular polyp calmed first by topical estrogen local hormone cream before excision, biopsied when atypical since cancers mimic it. Type III chronic pelvic pain without proven bacteria needs multimodal care, pelvic floor physiotherapy, and alpha blockade trials rather than repeated antibiotics. Prostatitis syndromes classify first and drain pus early.
