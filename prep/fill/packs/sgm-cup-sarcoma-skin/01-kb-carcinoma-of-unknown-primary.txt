# Carcinoma of Unknown Primary
Carcinoma of Unknown Primary, Oncology / Hematology.
Aliases: CUP; Cancer of Unknown Primary; Occult primary malignancy; Metastatic carcinoma of unknown origin.
# Pathophysiology
The reasons cancers present without an identifiable primary remain unclear. One hypothesis is that the primary tumor regresses after seeding metastases or stays too small to detect, possibly contained or eliminated by host defenses including the immune system. Alternatively, CUP may reflect a distinct malignant event producing increased metastatic spread or survival relative to the primary; whether CUP metastases represent a genetically and phenotypically unique clone is unresolved. Searches for a unique CUP signature have been unrevealing: aneuploidy occurs in about 70% of metastatic adenocarcinoma/undifferentiated CUP, and overexpression of RAS, BCL2 (about 40%), HER2 (about 11%), and P53 (about 26-53%) is seen but is shared with many other malignancies. Adenocarcinoma CUP is more often driven by RTK/Ras/MAPK pathway alterations than non-adenocarcinoma CUP. No distinct molecular signature has been established.
# Prognosis
Median survival for most patients with disseminated CUP is about 6-10 months, and the prognosis for advanced disease remains poor with early resistance to available cytotoxic therapy. Prognosis is heterogeneous: favorable subsets (e.g., women with isolated axillary adenopathy treated as breast cancer, women with primary peritoneal serous carcinoma treated as ovarian cancer, men with midline poorly differentiated carcinoma resembling germ cell tumor, squamous cervical adenopathy, solitary metastatic sites, low-grade neuroendocrine tumors, and men with blastic bone metastases and elevated PSA) can achieve prolonged survival and occasionally cure. Recognized prognostic factors include performance status, site and number of metastases, response to therapy, and serum LDH; prognostic models (Culine using performance status and LDH; Raghav nomogram using gender, ECOG performance status, histology, number of metastatic sites, and neutrophil-lymphocyte ratio) stratify outcomes.
# Severity classification
No single formal staging system; risk stratification distinguishes favorable prognostic subsets (treatable as a specific putative primary, with potential for prolonged survival/cure) from unfavorable disseminated disease. Prognostic models include the Culine model (performance status plus serum LDH, dividing patients into two outcome subgroups) and the Raghav nomogram (gender, ECOG performance status, histology, number of metastatic sites, and neutrophil-lymphocyte ratio).
# Clinical pearls
CUP is a biopsy-proven malignancy whose anatomic site of origin stays unidentified despite a standardized, detailed diagnostic workup; it ranks among the 10 most frequently diagnosed cancers worldwide and accounts for roughly 3-5% of all malignancies.
Most authorities restrict the CUP label to epithelial or undifferentiated cancers and exclude lymphoma, metastatic melanoma, and metastatic sarcoma, since those have distinct histology and stage-based management even without an identified primary.
On light microscopy about 60-65% of CUP is adenocarcinoma and roughly 5% is squamous cell carcinoma, with the remaining 30-35% being poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma/carcinoma or poorly differentiated neoplasm; neuroendocrine tumors make up about 2%.
Most serum tumor markers (CEA, CA-125, CA 19-9, CA 15-3) are nonspecific and do not localize the primary; in contrast, PSA tissue/serum staining and thyroglobulin are among the most specific markers but have low yield because prostate and thyroid cancers rarely present as CUP.
CK7 and CK20 cytokeratin patterns help narrow the putative primary: CK7 marks lung, ovary, endometrium, breast, and upper GI/pancreaticobiliary tumors, while CK20 is expressed in GI epithelium, urothelium, and Merkel cells.
TTF-1 nuclear staining is positive in about 68% of lung adenocarcinomas and roughly 25% of squamous lung cancers and helps distinguish a lung primary from metastatic adenocarcinoma in pleural, mediastinal, or parenchymal sites.
Comprehensive genomic profiling shows about 85% of CUP samples carry at least one clinically relevant genomic alteration, averaging about 4.2 alterations per tumor; the most common are TP53 (about 55%), KRAS (about 20%), CDKN2A (about 19%), and ARID1A (about 11%).
Tissue-agnostic biomarkers matter in a minority of CUP cases: MSI-high / mismatch-repair-deficient tumors and high tumor mutational burden tumors, as well as NTRK fusion-positive tumors, identify candidates for established histology-agnostic therapy.
Adequate tissue acquisition (preferably excisional or core needle biopsy rather than fine-needle aspiration alone) is critical, both for histology/IHC and for molecular profiling that can suggest a primary and actionable biomarkers.
Liver is a common site of CUP presentation, so intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma is frequently misdiagnosed as CUP; cholangiocarcinoma incidence is rising while CUP incidence is declining, partly due to improved molecular diagnostics and clinician awareness.
DNA methylation profiling predicted a primary in about 87% of 216 CUP patients in one study, and tissue-of-origin classifier assays suggest a plausible primary in roughly 70-80% of patients, though accuracy cannot be fully verified because the true primary is by definition unconfirmed.
Latent primary cancers (a new primary emerging after months to years) are uncommon with modern imaging, occurring in 5% or fewer of CUP patients, typically in indolent presentations or highly treatment-responsive disease.
# Additional differentials
Intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (commonly misdiagnosed as liver-presenting CUP)
Extragonadal germ cell tumor in men with poorly/undifferentiated midline adenopathy
Occult breast carcinoma in women with isolated axillary adenopathy
Primary peritoneal papillary serous carcinoma versus colon or pancreaticobiliary cancer in women with peritoneal carcinomatosis
Prostate carcinoma in men with blastic bone-only metastases and elevated PSA
Lung or head-and-neck primary in older smokers presenting with mediastinal adenopathy
# Non infectious mimics
Lymphoma, metastatic melanoma, and metastatic sarcoma (excluded from CUP because they have specific histology/staging)
Peritoneal mesothelioma mislabeled as peritoneal carcinomatosis (calretinin/D2-40 positive; BerEp4/MOC-31 negative)
# Additional investigations
Contrast CT of chest, abdomen, and pelvis is the standard baseline workup in all CUP patients absent contraindication
Thorough history (risk factors, prior surgeries, family history) and physical exam including digital rectal exam in men and breast/pelvic exam in women
Mammography (and breast MRI if mammogram/ultrasound negative) in women with metastatic adenocarcinoma, especially isolated axillary adenopathy
For cervical squamous CUP: CT/MRI plus laryngoscopy, bronchoscopy, upper endoscopy, and consideration of staging tonsillectomy
18-FDG-PET, most useful in squamous cervical CUP (identifies a head/neck primary in about 21-30%) and in solitary metastasis or bone-metastasis candidates for aggressive multimodality therapy
Serum PSA in men with adenocarcinoma and osteoblastic metastasis; beta-hCG and AFP for suspected extragonadal germ cell tumor
Detailed pathology with hematoxylin-eosin and a tiered IHC panel (e.g., CK7/CK20, TTF-1, CDX-2, GCDFP-15/GATA3, PSA, thyroglobulin) plus molecular/tissue-of-origin profiling and next-generation sequencing
Endoscopy/colonoscopy/bronchoscopy reserved for symptomatic patients or those with findings suggesting high diagnostic yield
# Red flags
Liver, brain, or adrenal metastatic involvement, which carries a poor prognosis
Disseminated disease with two or more involved sites
Poor performance status and elevated serum LDH, which are adverse prognostic factors
Peritoneal carcinomatosis from metastatic adenocarcinoma, raising concern for gastric, appendiceal, colon, or pancreaticobiliary primaries
# Pitfalls
No IHC stain is 100% sensitive or specific; under- or overinterpretation should be avoided, and poor differentiation reduces sensitivity even of hallmark markers.
CDX-2 positivity, while used for GI adenocarcinomas, can also occur in tumors with enteric/mucinous differentiation from other sites such as mucinous ovarian cancer.
Tissue antigenicity and IHC interpretation are affected by antigen retrieval, fixation/processing, observer variability, antibody performance, and tissue heterogeneity or inadequacy from small biopsies.
Pathologic features should not always override clinical or radiologic findings when deciding to test for therapeutic biomarkers such as EGFR, ALK, or HER2.
More IHC stains are not necessarily better; staining must be interpreted alongside clinical presentation and imaging, with close clinician-pathologist communication.
Not all peritoneal carcinomatosis in women is primary peritoneal serous carcinoma; careful pathology can reveal a colon or pancreaticobiliary profile or mislabeled mesothelioma.
Two randomized trials of gene-expression-directed site-specific therapy versus empiric chemotherapy failed to show a significant survival benefit, so molecular classifier results must be interpreted cautiously.
# Management
Identify treatable favourable subsets and manage them as the corresponding known cancer, for example women with isolated axillary adenopathy treated as breast cancer and midline germ-cell-like disease as germ cell tumour
Immunohistochemistry (including CK7/CK20, TTF-1 and PSA) and, where available, molecular tissue-of-origin profiling to direct therapy
Site-directed therapy for favourable presentations, such as neck dissection and radiotherapy for cervical squamous nodal disease
Empirical platinum-based combination chemotherapy for the unfavourable disseminated adenocarcinoma majority
Consideration of immunotherapy or molecularly targeted agents where actionable biomarkers are identified
Palliative and supportive care with symptom control, given the generally poor prognosis of disseminated disease
Multidisciplinary oncology review integrating pathology, imaging and treatment decisions
