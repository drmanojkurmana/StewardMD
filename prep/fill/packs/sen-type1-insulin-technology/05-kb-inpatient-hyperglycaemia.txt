# Inpatient hyperglycaemia and perioperative glucose management
Adults
Ward, ICU, perioperative
Treat persistent glucose of 180 mg/dL (10 mmol/L) or more with scheduled insulin (basal plus prandial plus correction if eating, basal plus correction if not), aiming for 100 to 180 mg/dL on wards and 140 to 180 mg/dL in ICU, avoid sliding scale alone, and match insulin to steroids and surgery.
Aliases: inpatient diabetes; hospital hyperglycaemia; basal bolus; basal plus; sliding scale; correction insulin; steroid-induced hyperglycaemia; perioperative diabetes; stress hyperglycaemia; VRIII.
# Assess on admission
Hospital hyperglycaemia: any glucose above 140 mg/dL (7.8 mmol/L). Check HbA1c if none in the past 3 months; HbA1c 6.5% or more suggests diabetes predating admission.
Record diabetes type (type 1 must always have basal insulin), home drugs and doses, nutrition route (eating, nil by mouth, enteral, parenteral), steroid use, renal function and hypoglycaemia risk.
Point-of-care glucose before meals if eating; every 4 to 6 hours if not eating or on continuous feeds; every 30 minutes to 2 hours on IV insulin. Confirm unexpected readings in the lab.
# Targets and choice of regimen
Start or intensify insulin for persistent glucose of 180 mg/dL (10.0 mmol/L) or more (two readings within 24 hours).
Targets: ward (non-critical) 100 to 180 mg/dL (5.6 to 10.0 mmol/L); ICU 140 to 180 mg/dL (7.8 to 10.0 mmol/L), with 110 to 140 mg/dL only for selected patients (for example cardiac surgery) if achievable without hypoglycaemia.
ICU: continuous IV insulin infusion using a validated written or computerised protocol.
Eating adequately: basal plus prandial plus correction insulin. Poor or no oral intake: basal insulin plus correction insulin.
No prior diabetes, mild hyperglycaemia: correction insulin alone is acceptable at first; add scheduled insulin if 2 or more readings in 24 hours are 180 mg/dL or more. Diabetes with confirmed admission glucose 180 mg/dL or more: start scheduled insulin.
Correction-only insulin (sliding scale) as the sole regimen is discouraged, and is contraindicated in type 1 diabetes. Premixed insulins (70/30, 50/50) are not routinely recommended in hospital because of more hypoglycaemia.
# Starting doses (non-ICU)
Insulin-naive: total daily dose (TDD) 0.3 to 0.6 units/kg/day by weight and insulin sensitivity; use the lower end for older adults, CKD and poor intake.
Give half the TDD as basal (glargine once daily or NPH twice daily) and half as prandial rapid-acting or regular insulin split across three meals; hold prandial doses if a meal is missed. If not eating, give the basal half plus correction.
Already on insulin: start at about 80% of the home dose, adjusted for intake and illness. Basal-heavy home regimens (basal 0.6 to 1.0 units/kg/day or more) may need a 10 to 20% basal reduction.
From an IV infusion: estimate TDD from the stable rate over the last 6 to 8 hours extrapolated to 24 hours and use about 60% of it. Give SC basal 2 hours before stopping the infusion.
Correction insulin before meals (or every 4 to 6 hours if not eating), starting at 140 to 150 mg/dL (7.8 to 8.3 mmol/L), with a low, medium or high scale for TDD below 40, 40 to 80, or above 80 units/day.
Human insulins (NPH plus regular) give similar control to analogues where analogues are unaffordable, with somewhat more hypoglycaemia. Titrate doses daily.
# Non-insulin drugs in hospital
Hold metformin when oral intake cannot be maintained, with AKI or risk of it, and on the day of surgery or procedures.
SGLT2 inhibitors are not for inpatient glucose control: stop in severe illness, ketonaemia, prolonged fasting and surgery. They may be started or continued for heart failure once stable, with no contraindication.
Hold GLP-1 RAs in acutely ill patients. Sulfonylureas cause hypoglycaemia when intake is unreliable and are usually withheld.
Type 2 diabetes with mild hyperglycaemia (admission glucose below 180 to 200 mg/dL, HbA1c below 7.5%): a DPP-4 inhibitor plus correction insulin is an option. Avoid saxagliptin and alogliptin in heart failure.
# Glucocorticoid-induced hyperglycaemia
Morning prednisolone raises glucose from midday into the evening with near-normal fasting values: check pre-lunch and pre-dinner glucose, not only fasting.
No known diabetes: if glucose is above 216 mg/dL (12 mmol/L) on 2 occasions in 24 hours, test 4 times a day and start treatment (JBDS). Target 108 to 180 mg/dL (6 to 10 mmol/L), 108 to 216 mg/dL (6 to 12 mmol/L) acceptable.
Once-daily morning steroid: morning NPH insulin given with the steroid, starting 10 units and increasing by 10 to 20% daily to target (NPH-based or basal-bolus regimens are equally acceptable). Mild cases: gliclazide 40 mg each morning, titrated daily to a maximum of 240 mg.
Dexamethasone or multiple daily doses: long-acting basal insulin is usually needed; high doses often need 40 to 60% more prandial and correction insulin.
Reduce insulin or sulfonylurea doses in step with the steroid taper to avoid hypoglycaemia.
