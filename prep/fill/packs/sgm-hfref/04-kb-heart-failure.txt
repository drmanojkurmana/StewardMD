# Acute heart failure / pulmonary edema
Acute heart failure / pulmonary edema, Cardiology.
Red flags: Address precipitant; not an infection.
# Investigations
CXR
BNP/NT-proBNP
ECG, troponin
Echocardiogram
# Pathophysiology
HFrEF is a progressive disease in which an index injury (e.g. acute MI, chronic pressure/volume overload, genetic or congenital disease) triggers months-to-years of structural and functional cardiovascular remodelling; patients may stay asymptomatic for long periods because compensatory mechanisms are activated that ultimately drive further deterioration. Pressure overload produces concentric hypertrophy and volume overload produces eccentric (dilated) hypertrophy, accompanied by myocyte hypertrophy, interstitial fibrosis, apoptosis and re-expression of fetal genes. Sustained activation of the sympathetic nervous system and the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system initially supports cardiac output but becomes maladaptive, causing excessive vasoconstriction, sodium/water retention, electrolyte and renal disturbance, direct myocardial toxicity and arrhythmias; counter-regulatory natriuretic peptides, prostaglandins, bradykinin and nitric oxide are upregulated but inadequate. Therapies that antagonise RAAS and the sympathetic system (RAAS inhibitors, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, beta blockers) attenuate or reverse remodelling and improve outcomes. Secondary mitral regurgitation arises from ventricular dilatation, sphericity and annular changes, perpetuating a vicious cycle. HFpEF reflects multiple mechanisms beyond diastolic dysfunction (impaired calcium reuptake, vascular stiffness, renal dysfunction, sodium avidity, inflammation related to adiposity, oxidative/nitrosative stress, insulin resistance). The cardiorenal syndrome is driven not only by low forward flow but importantly by systemic venous congestion and raised intra-abdominal/splanchnic pressure, so relief of congestion improves renal function.
# Prognosis
Overall 5-year survival after a HF diagnosis is roughly 50%, and in severe HF 1-year mortality may reach 40%; one in eight U.S. deaths lists HF on the certificate, most dying of progressive HF or sudden cardiac death. After a HF admission, mortality runs from 8-14% at 30 days to 26-37% at 1 year and up to 75% at 5 years, with readmission of 20-25% at 60 days and nearly 50% at 6 months; risk of death rises with each subsequent admission. Independent predictors of adverse outcome include older age, male sex, diabetes, CKD, CAD, advanced NYHA class, a third heart sound or raised JVP, reduced LV (or RV) EF, secondary mitral/tricuspid regurgitation, raised wedge pressure, pulmonary hypertension, low peak VO2, hyponatraemia, hyperuricaemia, worsening renal function, raised troponin/natriuretic peptides and neurohormones, wide QRS/LBBB, atrial fibrillation and ventricular arrhythmia. HFrecEF (recovered EF) generally carries a better prognosis than HFrEF or HFpEF, but withdrawing therapy can cause relapse in up to half within 6 months.
# Severity classification
HFrEF/HFmrEF/HFpEF phenotype by EF: reduced EF (HFrEF) <=40%, mildly reduced EF (HFmrEF) 41-49%, preserved EF (HFpEF) >=50%; LVEF >=50% is considered normal systolic function. NYHA functional class I-IV grades symptoms by the activity that provokes them: class I none with ordinary activity, class II mild symptoms on ordinary exertion, class III symptoms on less-than-ordinary activity, class IV symptoms (or angina) at rest worsening with any activity. ACC/AHA stages describe disease progression: stage B asymptomatic ventricular dysfunction, stage C symptomatic HF, stage D advanced/refractory HF with high mortality where transplant or mechanical circulatory support may be considered. Killip classification (originally for acute MI) grades pump failure: class I no congestion; class II basal rales, S3 or right-heart congestion; class III pulmonary oedema; class IV cardiogenic shock (SBP <90 mmHg with peripheral vasoconstriction, cyanosis, confusion, oliguria) - historically rising mortality from class I to IV.
# Clinical pearls
HF is a clinical syndrome, not a single disease; it is the common final pathway for most chronic cardiovascular conditions, especially hypertension, coronary artery disease and valvular disease.
About half of patients with HF have a reduced ejection fraction and half have a near-normal/preserved EF, so a normal LVEF does not exclude HF.
The signs and symptoms of HF are neither specific nor sensitive, so a high index of suspicion is needed in higher-risk patients (older, with hypertension, diabetes or CKD).
NYHA functional class correlates poorly with objective measures such as LV size, EF or peak oxygen consumption.
An S3 gallop usually accompanies volume overload and tachycardia, suggests significant haemodynamic compromise and carries adverse prognostic weight; an S4 is non-specific and may be heard in HFpEF from hypertension.
Bendopnea (breathlessness on bending forward, e.g. tying shoes) reflects raised filling pressure, particularly with low cardiac output.
Holosystolic murmurs of mitral/tricuspid regurgitation often appear in advanced HF even when the valves are structurally normal (functional/secondary regurgitation).
In acute or younger chronic HF patients, leg oedema may be absent despite marked systemic venous hypertension.
A very low BNP/NT-proBNP can help exclude HF as the cause of dyspnoea in non-obese patients.
The obesity paradox: obese HF patients tend to have a more favourable prognosis than those with low or normal BMI.
# Additional differentials
Chronic lung disease (orthopnoea and nocturnal dyspnoea can mimic HF; relieved by coughing/expectoration rather than sitting upright)
Pulmonary arterial hypertension
Neuromuscular disease causing dyspnoea
Anaemia / iron-deficiency anaemia
Venous insufficiency, lymphoedema or obesity as non-cardiac causes of leg oedema
Nephrotic syndrome and deep vein thrombosis (causes of oedema)
Hepatic cirrhosis, portal vein thrombosis, malignant carcinomatosis (causes of ascites)
Constrictive pericarditis, pericardial effusion or superior vena cava syndrome (causes of jugular venous distension)
# Infection mimics
Low-grade fever from cytokine activation may occur in severe HF and resolves once compensation is restored, mimicking infection
Congestive hepatomegaly with raised transaminases, alkaline phosphatase and bilirubin can be misread as biliary tract disease
Chronic lower-limb oedema in HF may become weeping or progress to cellulitis
# Non infectious mimics
Acute respiratory distress syndrome and other non-cardiogenic pulmonary oedema
Acute or chronic kidney disease causing salt/water retention with normal cardiac structure and function
Restrictive cardiomyopathy versus constrictive pericarditis (distinguished by simultaneous right- and left-heart filling pressures)
Reactive airways disease/bronchial asthma versus cardiac asthma (cardiac asthma more often shows diaphoresis and cyanosis)
# Additional investigations
Comprehensive metabolic panel, complete blood count, coagulation studies and urinalysis as baseline; targeted testing for diabetes, lipids and thyroid function
Serum sodium and potassium (hyponatraemia is an adverse prognostic marker; hypo-/hyperkalaemia predispose to arrhythmia)
Renal function (urea/creatinine, consider cystatin C which is less affected by muscle mass)
Iron studies/ferritin to detect iron deficiency (with or without anaemia)
Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging for tissue characterisation and aetiology (ischaemic, myocarditis, amyloidosis, haemochromatosis)
Cardiac CT / coronary CT angiography to exclude pericardial disease, apical thrombus or obstructive CAD when CMR not feasible
Cardiac PET for ischaemia/viability and to assess cardiac sarcoid inflammation
Cardiopulmonary exercise testing (peak VO2, VE/VCO2 slope) for prognosis and transplant/MCS evaluation
Transoesophageal echocardiography or contrast cardiac CT to exclude left atrial appendage thrombus before cardioversion
Right-heart catheterisation / pulmonary artery catheter to distinguish cardiogenic from non-cardiogenic pulmonary oedema and guide therapy in severe HF
Endomyocardial biopsy in selected cases (suspected myocarditis - lymphocytic, eosinophilic, sarcoid or giant cell - amyloidosis, or allograft rejection)
Newer biomarkers galectin-3 and soluble ST2 are approved for prognosis but not widely used
# Red flags
Acute pulmonary oedema with pink frothy sputum reflects marked elevation of pulmonary capillary wedge pressure
Hypotension with narrow pulse pressure and a rapid, thready pulse indicates severe HF; pulsus alternans signals severely depressed LV function
Cool extremities, peripheral cyanosis, mental dullness/confusion and oliguria suggest low-output/advanced-stage HF
Marked elevation of transaminases and lactic acid suggests cardiogenic shock with severe low output
Cardiac cachexia (unintentional oedema-free weight loss >5% over 12 months) marks longstanding severe HF
An S3 gallop carries negative prognostic significance
Cheyne-Stokes/periodic breathing and central sleep apnoea are features of advanced HF
# Pitfalls
Abandon the old terms systolic and diastolic HF: most HF patients, regardless of EF, have both systolic and diastolic abnormalities
BNP/NT-proBNP may be falsely low in obesity and falsely elevated by age, female sex, CKD, atrial fibrillation, pulmonary embolism and pulmonary hypertension, so interpret in context
Inflammatory markers (ESR, CRP, uric acid) are non-specific and do not establish the diagnosis of HF
Congestive hepatopathy and gut-wall oedema can be mistaken for primary biliary or bowel disease
Distinguishing cardiac from pulmonary dyspnoea may be impossible when both coexist (common in older smokers); post-diuresis pulmonary function tests or cardiopulmonary exercise testing can help
Failure to identify and correct a precipitant can leave HF persistent despite adequate treatment; a precipitant is identifiable in 50-90% of admissions
Nonpitting oedema unresponsive to escalating diuretics may be lymphoedema, not HF
High-output HF (anaemia, thyrotoxicosis, AV shunt, cirrhosis, severe obesity, beriberi) will be missed if not actively considered in predisposed patients
