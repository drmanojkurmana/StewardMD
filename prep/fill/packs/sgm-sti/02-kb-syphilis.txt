# Syphilis
Syphilis, Infectious Diseases.
Aliases: Treponema pallidum infection.
# Pathophysiology
Treponema pallidum subsp. pallidum is a thin spiral organism with a lipid-rich outer membrane and periplasmic endoflagella that drive motility; it has severely limited metabolic capacity and depends on host amino acids, carbohydrates, and lipids. Humans are the only known natural host. After penetrating intact mucous membranes or skin abrasions, treponemes reach the lymphatics and blood within hours, producing systemic dissemination and metastatic foci well before the primary lesion appears (median incubation ~21 days, rarely >6 weeks). Tissue damage reflects the host immune response rather than a direct treponemal toxin: lesions show perivascular infiltration by CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, plasma cells, and macrophages with endarteritis obliterans (obliteration of small vessels). Antigenic variation of the TprK outer-membrane protein allows immune evasion and persistence, explaining the paradoxical appearance of secondary lesions after primary lesions clear. Tertiary lesions (gumma, aortitis of the vasa vasorum of the ascending aorta, parenchymal neurosyphilis) arise from a slowly progressive inflammatory process that begins early in infection.
# Prognosis
Untreated primary and secondary lesions resolve spontaneously, but infection persists. In the preantibiotic era about one-third of untreated patients with latent infection progressed to tertiary disease; the Tuskegee Study showed untreated syphilis raised the death rate ~17%, largely from cardiovascular disease. With early treatment, prognosis is excellent and tertiary syphilis is now nearly eliminated in Western countries; the clinical response to therapy for benign tertiary (gumma) disease is usually impressive. Congenital syphilis prognosis is poor when fulminant disease is clinically apparent at birth, but adequate maternal treatment before the 16th week of pregnancy should prevent fetal damage. Response to therapy is monitored by a fourfold decline in VDRL/RPR titer.
# Severity classification
Disease is described by clinical stage rather than a graded severity score: primary syphilis (chancre with regional lymphadenopathy); secondary syphilis (generalized mucocutaneous rash including palms and soles, condylomata lata, mucous patches, constitutional symptoms); latent syphilis (reactive serology, normal CSF, no clinical signs; early latent <1 year vs late latent >=1 year or unknown duration); tertiary/late syphilis (gumma, cardiovascular syphilis, late neurosyphilis). Neurosyphilis spans a continuum: early (asymptomatic meningitis, meningeal and meningovascular syphilis) and late parenchymatous disease (general paresis, tabes dorsalis). Congenital syphilis is divided into early (within first 2 years) and late (after 2 years).
# Clinical pearls
Chronic systemic infection by Treponema pallidum subsp. pallidum, usually sexually transmitted, characterized by episodes of active disease separated by asymptomatic latent periods.
Staging is the organizing framework: a primary chancre at the site of inoculation, then generalized secondary mucocutaneous disease, followed by latency, and in a minority of untreated patients tertiary disease (gumma, cardiovascular, late neurosyphilis).
Primary chancre is typically a single painless papule that erodes into an indurated ulcer with a firm cartilaginous edge; it is accompanied by regional (usually inguinal) lymphadenopathy that is firm, nonsuppurative, and painless, and heals spontaneously in about 4-6 weeks.
Secondary syphilis classically produces a widespread macular-to-papular, nonpruritic rash that frequently involves the palms and soles, plus generalized nontender lymphadenopathy (noted in ~85% of patients).
Condylomata lata (broad, moist, highly infectious lesions in intertriginous areas, ~10% of secondary cases) and mucous patches (painless silver-gray erosions of oral/genital mucosa) are characteristic secondary lesions.
Latent syphilis is defined by reactive serology with a normal CSF and no clinical manifestations; early latent is within the first year, late latent is >=1 year or of unknown duration.
Two classes of serologic tests: lipoidal (nontreponemal) RPR/VDRL, which are quantitative and reflect disease activity, and treponemal tests (FTA-ABS, TPPA, EIA/CIA); a reactive lipoidal screen must be confirmed by a treponemal test.
RPR is preferred for rapid serologic diagnosis; the VDRL remains the standard for CSF examination and is superior to RPR for that purpose.
