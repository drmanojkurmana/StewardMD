# Pure autonomic failure
Pure autonomic failure, Nervous system / Autonomic, Neurology / Autonomic.
Aliases: PAF; Bradbury-Eggleston syndrome; idiopathic orthostatic hypotension.
# Pathophysiology
Pure autonomic failure is a neurodegenerative disorder confined largely to the peripheral autonomic nervous system. Misfolded alpha-synuclein accumulates within postganglionic sympathetic and parasympathetic neurons and causes their degeneration. Loss of postganglionic sympathetic fibres impairs noradrenaline release, so vasoconstriction fails on standing and blood pools in the lower body, producing orthostatic hypotension. Because supine noradrenaline is low and does not increase appropriately on standing, the lesion is localised distal to the ganglion. Denervation supersensitivity of vascular adrenoceptors and impaired baroreflex buffering contribute to the coexisting supine hypertension.
# Prognosis
Pure autonomic failure generally progresses slowly and carries a better outlook than multiple system atrophy, but a meaningful proportion of patients phenoconvert to a central synucleinopathy over years, and disabling orthostatic symptoms with supine hypertension require ongoing management.
# Clinical pearls
A sporadic, adult-onset, slowly progressive degenerative autonomic disorder presenting with orthostatic hypotension in the absence of central neurological deficits
Reflects peripheral (postganglionic) sympathetic denervation, with low supine plasma noradrenaline that fails to rise on standing
Characterised by pathological alpha-synuclein deposition in peripheral autonomic neurons, placing it within the synucleinopathies
Symptoms include light-headedness, fatigue, visual greying, and a suboccipital 'coat-hanger' ache on standing, all relieved by sitting or lying
Additional features include reduced sweating, bladder and bowel disturbance, and erectile dysfunction
A substantial minority phenoconvert over years to a central synucleinopathy such as Parkinson disease, dementia with Lewy bodies, or multiple system atrophy
Supine hypertension frequently coexists with orthostatic hypotension and complicates treatment
Management is non-pharmacological first: volume and salt expansion, compression garments, physical counter-manoeuvres, and head-up tilt of the bed overnight
Additional differentials: Multiple system atrophy; Parkinson disease with autonomic involvement; Diabetic or amyloid autonomic neuropathy; Dopamine beta-hydroxylase deficiency; Medication-induced orthostatic hypotension; Volume depletion or adrenal insufficiency.
# Additional investigations
Head-up tilt-table testing with beat-to-beat blood pressure recording
Supine and standing plasma catecholamines showing low, non-responsive noradrenaline
Cardiovascular autonomic reflex battery (Valsalva manoeuvre, deep breathing, sustained handgrip)
QSART and thermoregulatory sweat test for sudomotor involvement
Cardiac MIBG scintigraphy demonstrating reduced myocardial uptake
Serial neurological review to detect phenoconversion to a central synucleinopathy
# Red flags
Emergence of parkinsonism, cerebellar signs, or early severe autonomic failure suggesting multiple system atrophy
Rapid progression or prominent bladder failure pointing to a central synucleinopathy
Severe supine hypertension threatening end-organ damage
Recurrent syncope with fall-related injury
# Pitfalls
Overlooking coexisting supine hypertension when treating orthostatic hypotension
Attributing symptoms to simple dehydration and missing the underlying degenerative disorder
Failing to monitor for phenoconversion to Parkinson disease, dementia with Lewy bodies, or multiple system atrophy
Overtreating orthostatic hypotension and worsening nocturnal supine hypertension
