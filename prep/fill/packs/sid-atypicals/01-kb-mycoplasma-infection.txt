# Mycoplasma Infection
Mycoplasma Infection, Infectious Diseases.
Aliases: Mycoplasma pneumoniae; atypical pneumonia; walking pneumonia.
# Pathophysiology
Mycoplasmas are prokaryotes of the class Mollicutes, the smallest organisms capable of independent replication; they lack a cell wall and are bound only by a cell membrane, which explains the inactivity of beta-lactam antibiotics (penicillins and cephalosporins) against them. M. pneumoniae is generally an extracellular pathogen that attaches to ciliated respiratory epithelial cells via a complex terminal organelle using interactive adhesins and accessory proteins. Tissue injury is thought to be mediated by production of hydrogen peroxide and an ADP-ribosylating and vacuolating cytotoxin similar to pertussis toxin. Lacking a cell wall, mycoplasmas lack cell-wall-derived innate immune stimulators (lipopolysaccharide, lipoteichoic acid, peptidoglycan), but membrane lipoproteins act through Toll-like receptors (primarily TLR2). Innate immunity provides most lung defense, cellular immunity may be immunopathogenic, and humoral immunity protects against dissemination; patients with humoral immunodeficiency are prone to disseminated disease (arthritis, meningitis, osteomyelitis). Extrapulmonary manifestations may arise from disseminated infection, postinfectious autoimmune phenomena (e.g., Guillain-Barre syndrome), or possibly an ADP-ribosylating toxin.
# Prognosis
M. pneumoniae pneumonia is generally self-limited, with symptoms usually resolving within 2-3 weeks of onset even without treatment; appropriate antimicrobial therapy significantly shortens the duration of clinical illness. Infection uncommonly results in critical illness and only rarely in death. Long-term recurrent wheezing or reactive airway disease may follow resolution of acute pneumonia, and a possible link to asthma is under investigation. Immunity following severe infection is more protective and longer-lasting than after mild infection, and genuine second attacks of pneumonia are reported infrequently.
# Severity classification
No formal severity classification is given for Mycoplasma infection; M. pneumoniae illness is generally graded clinically from mild self-limited upper respiratory illness through pneumonia to severe/refractory or critical disease, with macrolide resistance and extrapulmonary involvement marking more severe courses.
# Clinical pearls
M. pneumoniae pneumonia is also called Eaton agent pneumonia, primary atypical pneumonia, and 'walking' pneumonia.
Onset of pneumonia is usually gradual over several days; the most common presenting symptom is cough, which is typically nonproductive, though headache, malaise, chills, and fever are noted in the majority of patients.
Pneumonia develops in only 3-13% of infected individuals; upper respiratory illness is far more common, with an estimated incidence up to 20 times that of pneumonia.
Wheezes or rales are detected in about 80% of patients with M. pneumoniae pneumonia, but in many patients pneumonia can be diagnosed only by chest radiography.
The most common radiographic pattern is peribronchial pneumonia with thickened bronchial markings, streaks of interstitial infiltration, and subsegmental atelectasis; segmental or lobar consolidation is not uncommon.
Clinical presentation does not reliably distinguish M. pneumoniae pneumonia from other causes of community-acquired pneumonia.
Many patients with extrapulmonary M. pneumoniae disease do not have respiratory disease.
Hematologic manifestations include hemolytic anemia, aplastic anemia, cold agglutinins, disseminated intravascular coagulation, and hypercoagulopathy; when anemia occurs it generally develops in the second or third week of illness.
Erythema multiforme major (Stevens-Johnson syndrome) is the most clinically significant skin eruption associated with M. pneumoniae and appears to occur more commonly with M. pneumoniae than with other infectious agents; in M. pneumoniae-induced rash and mucositis (MIRM)/reactive infectious mucocutaneous eruption (RIME) there may be minimal cutaneous involvement with prominent mucosal disease.
M. pneumoniae has been implicated as a likely etiologic agent in 5-7% of cases of encephalitis.
Diagnosis is most sensitively and rapidly made by combining PCR of respiratory tract secretions with serologic testing of paired acute- and convalescent-phase samples.
M. pneumoniae is the most frequently detected 'atypical' organism in adult community-acquired pneumonia; older pooled data gave a prevalence of about 22.7%, and more recent (2002-2015) analyses gave about 7.2%.
# Additional differentials
Chlamydia (Chlamydophila) pneumoniae community-acquired pneumonia
Legionella species pneumonia
Streptococcus pneumoniae and other bacterial community-acquired pneumonia
Viral respiratory tract infection / pneumonia
Infection mimics: Chlamydophila pneumoniae pneumonia; Legionella pneumonia; Viral atypical pneumonia.
# Non infectious mimics
Asthma / reactive airway disease (wheezing overlap)
Drug-induced or herpes-associated erythema multiforme major (overlapping with Mycoplasma-associated SJS/MIRM)
# Additional investigations
Respiratory PCR for M. pneumoniae (sensitivity 65-90%, specificity 90-100%) - allows rapid early diagnosis
Paired acute- and convalescent-phase serology (IgM and IgG) by enzyme-linked immunoassay; a single acute sample is inadequate because antibodies may not develop until ~2 weeks
Respiratory culture on special media (sensitivity <=60%, specificity 100%) - not recommended for routine diagnosis as it may take weeks
Chest radiography
Cerebrospinal fluid PCR for extrapulmonary (CNS) infection
Macrolide-resistance testing by culture and/or PCR when resistance is suspected
# Red flags
Neurologic manifestations: meningoencephalitis, encephalitis, Guillain-Barre syndrome, aseptic meningitis, transverse myelitis, acute disseminated encephalomyelitis, cerebrovascular thromboembolic events, acute psychosis, cerebellar ataxia, cranial neuropathy
Severe mucocutaneous disease: erythema multiforme major / Stevens-Johnson syndrome / MIRM
Hemolytic anemia, disseminated intravascular coagulation, hypercoagulopathy
Disseminated infection (septic arthritis, meningitis, osteomyelitis), especially in hypogammaglobulinemic / humorally immunodeficient patients
Community-acquired pneumonia failing to respond to a penicillin or cephalosporin
# Pitfalls
Because mycoplasmas lack a cell wall, beta-lactam antibiotics (penicillins and cephalosporins) are ineffective; consider M. pneumoniae particularly when community-acquired pneumonia fails to respond to these agents.
M. pneumoniae lacks a cell wall and is therefore not visible on Gram stain.
Measurement of cold agglutinin titers is no longer recommended for diagnosis because the findings are nonspecific and specific M. pneumoniae assays are now available.
A single acute-phase serum sample is not adequate for diagnosis, and IgM antibody can persist for up to 1 year, so its presence may indicate recent rather than acute infection - paired samples are needed.
Little evidence supports the commonly held belief that M. pneumoniae is an important cause of otitis media, with or without bullous myringitis.
A test of cure for eradication is not suggested, because appropriate antibiotics do not appear to shorten the duration of detection by culture or PCR.
Ciprofloxacin and ofloxacin are not recommended for M. pneumoniae owing to high MICs and poor experimental performance, in contrast to respiratory fluoroquinolones.
# Management
Treat with a macrolide such as azithromycin or clarithromycin as first-line therapy, avoiding beta-lactams because the wall-less organism is intrinsically resistant to them
Use a tetracycline such as doxycycline or a respiratory fluoroquinolone where macrolide resistance is suspected or in adults
Provide supportive care with antipyretics, analgesia, hydration, and rest, recognising that many infections are self-limiting
Relieve wheeze with bronchodilators and reserve hospital admission and supplemental oxygen for severe pneumonia or respiratory compromise
Identify and manage extrapulmonary manifestations such as haemolytic anaemia, neurological involvement, and severe mucocutaneous reactions
Send macrolide-resistance testing by culture or PCR when response to first-line therapy is poor
