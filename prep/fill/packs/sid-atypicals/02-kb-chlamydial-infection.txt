# Chlamydial Infections
Chlamydial Infections, Infectious Diseases.
Aliases: Chlamydia trachomatis; Chlamydophila pneumoniae; LGV.
# Pathophysiology
Chlamydiae are nonmotile, gram-negative, obligate intracellular bacteria that lack peptidoglycan (structural integrity depends on disulfide cross-linking of outer-membrane proteins) and are energy parasites using host-cell ATP. They have a unique biphasic growth cycle alternating between the infectious, extracellular-adapted elementary body (0.25-0.35 um) and the metabolically active, non-infectious intracellular reticulate body (0.5-1 um), which divides by binary fission within a membrane-bound cytoplasmic inclusion while preventing phagosome-lysosome fusion; the cycle lasts ~48-72 h, giving a long clinical incubation (generally 1-3 weeks). C. trachomatis has two biovars (trachoma and LGV) and 18 serovars (trachoma A/B/Ba/C; oculogenital D-K; LGV L1-L3); the trachoma biovar parasitizes squamocolumnar epithelium whereas the more invasive LGV biovar involves lymphoid cells. Disease is driven largely by immunopathology rather than direct cytotoxicity: the chlamydial heat-shock protein shares epitopes with human and other bacterial proteins and, with repeated/persistent infection, drives fibrosis and mucosal scarring leading to tubal-factor infertility, ectopic pregnancy and chronic pelvic pain (genital) or cicatricial trichiasis and blindness (ocular). Antigenic variation in the major outer-membrane protein (omp1) permits reinfection because immunity to one serovar is not protective against others.
# Prognosis
Uncomplicated genital chlamydial infection responds well to recommended antibiotics, and clinically significant drug resistance has not been observed in C. trachomatis; apparent treatment failure usually reflects non-adherence or reinfection. However, untreated or recurrent genital infection causes substantial long-term morbidity through mucosal scarring - PID with tubal-factor infertility, ectopic pregnancy, and chronic pelvic pain - and women bear the greatest burden of sequelae. Reactive arthritis episodes typically last 2-6 months. Untreated ocular disease progresses to blinding trachoma; the WHO estimates ~6 million people have been blinded and ~1.3 million remain blind from trachoma. Untreated psittacosis pneumonia can be fatal in up to 10% of cases. Previous chlamydial infection raises the risk of reinfection, prompting rescreening at 3 months.
# Severity classification
No formal grading scale. Genital C. trachomatis infections are categorized as uncomplicated (lower genital tract) versus complicated (e.g., PID, epididymitis) requiring longer therapy; LGV is treated as invasive disease with extended therapy. Trachoma is described by phase - active trachoma (follicular conjunctivitis) versus cicatricial disease (conjunctival scarring, trichiasis, entropion) - underpinning the WHO clinical signs used for diagnosis.
# Clinical pearls
C. trachomatis genital infection is the most prevalent bacterial STI worldwide and is reported more than any other infectious disease in the United States; case counts are heavily dependent on screening coverage.
Most genital infections are silent: up to 80-90% of women and >50% of men with C. trachomatis genital infection lack symptoms, and pharyngeal/rectal infection is typically asymptomatic and may persist for years, sustaining a large transmission reservoir.
C. trachomatis is the most common cause of nongonococcal urethritis (NGU) and postgonococcal urethritis (PGU), implicated in 30-50% of NGU cases.
NAATs are the CDC-recommended diagnostic assay of choice; less-invasive specimens (urine for both sexes, vaginal swabs for women) are acceptable, and self-collected or clinician-collected vaginal swabs are slightly more sensitive than urine in women.
Because of frequent co-infection, testing for or treating chlamydia should be considered in patients diagnosed with N. gonorrhoeae.
Trachoma (serovars A, B, Ba, C) remains a leading cause of preventable infectious blindness worldwide; the WHO SAFE-era programs target elimination as a public health problem by 2030.
LGV (serovars L1, L2, L3) is an invasive STI presenting classically as a transient painless genital ulcer followed by painful inguinal lymphadenopathy with bubo formation, or as hemorrhagic proctocolitis in MSM.
C. pneumoniae causes atypical (community-acquired) pneumonia, bronchitis, pharyngitis and sinusitis, accounting for as many as 10% of CAP cases though more typically 1-2%; PCR of respiratory secretions is the preferred diagnostic method.
Psittacosis (C. psittaci) is a zoonosis acquired from birds (psittacines, poultry) presenting as atypical pneumonia or systemic febrile illness; person-to-person transmission has never been demonstrated.
Neonatal exposure during vaginal delivery causes inclusion conjunctivitis (in ~20-30% of exposed infants) and, in 10-15%, pneumonia at 2 weeks to 4 months of age; standard gonococcal ocular prophylaxis does NOT prevent chlamydial conjunctivitis.
# Additional differentials
Neisseria gonorrhoeae urethritis/cervicitis/proctitis (clinically indistinguishable; frequent co-infection)
Mycoplasma genitalium (now a leading cause of Chlamydia-negative and persistent NGU)
Trichomonas vaginalis and herpes simplex virus as causes of NGU
Mycoplasma pneumoniae and other atypical pneumonia pathogens (for C. pneumoniae)
Crohn's disease of the rectum (mimicked by LGV proctocolitis on sigmoidoscopy and histology)
Adenovirus or HSV follicular conjunctivitis (mimics adult inclusion/oculogenital conjunctivitis)
# Infection mimics
Neisseria gonorrhoeae (urethritis, cervicitis, PID, proctitis, ophthalmia neonatorum)
Mycoplasma genitalium urethritis/cervicitis
Trichomonas vaginalis urethritis
Herpes simplex virus (urethritis, proctitis, ulcerative conjunctivitis)
Haemophilus influenzae or Streptococcus pneumoniae neonatal conjunctivitis
Mycoplasma pneumoniae and other atypical CAP agents (for C. pneumoniae pneumonia)
Adenovirus follicular conjunctivitis
Haemophilus ducreyi and other genital-ulcer pathogens (for LGV primary lesion)
# Non infectious mimics
Crohn's disease of the rectum (resembles LGV/serovar proctocolitis: granulomas, giant cells, crypt abscesses)
Testicular torsion or testicular tumor (mimic chlamydial epididymitis; torsion must be excluded promptly)
Intrauterine contraceptive device-induced cervical inflammation
Functional urinary symptoms / non-infectious dysuria (acute urethral syndrome in women)
Reactive arthritis / spondyloarthropathy in the differential of HLA-B27-associated joint disease
# Additional investigations
NAAT (nucleic acid amplification test) on urine, vaginal/cervical, urethral, rectal, pharyngeal or conjunctival specimens - diagnostic assay of choice
First-void urine or vaginal swab self-collection for screening programs and outreach
Microscopy for leukocytes (Gram stain of urethral exudate or cervical mucus; first-void urine pyuria; leukocyte esterase) to support presumptive diagnosis of urethritis/cervicitis
Giemsa-stained smears for intracytoplasmic inclusions (conjunctival scrapings, neonatal specimens; less sensitive than NAAT/culture)
Microimmunofluorescence (MIF) serology - test of choice for LGV and neonatal pneumonia and the serologic gold standard, though labor-intensive and largely research-only
Complement fixation (CF) serology (genus-level only via LPS; fourfold titer rise supports psittacosis)
PCR of respiratory secretions for C. pneumoniae (preferred) and respiratory NAAT for C. psittaci
Cell culture (former gold standard) - now performed only in specialized laboratories
Sigmoidoscopy with rectal biopsy for proctitis/LGV (granulomas, crypt abscesses)
Laparoscopy / endometrial biopsy (plasma-cell endometritis) in suspected salpingitis/PID and perihepatitis
Serovar/molecular genotyping when LGV is suspected (longer treatment duration required)
