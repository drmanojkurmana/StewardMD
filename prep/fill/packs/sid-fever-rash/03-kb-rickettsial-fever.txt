# Rickettsial Fever (Spotted Fever Group)
Rickettsial Fever (Spotted Fever Group), Tropical Fever.
# Pathogens
Spotted fever group rickettsioses are transmitted by ticks (rather than the chiggers/mites responsible for scrub typhus) and classically present with a generalized maculopapular or petechial rash rather than a single eschar, though clinical overlap exists and careful skin examination for an eschar should always be performed to distinguish from scrub typhus.
# Very likely
Rickettsia conorii / other spotted fever group Rickettsia species
# Possible
Orientia tsutsugamushi (if eschar is found on closer examination — would reclassify as scrub typhus)
# Investigations
Careful skin examination for eschar and rash characterization
Helps distinguish spotted fever group rickettsiosis (maculopapular/petechial rash, no eschar) from scrub typhus (eschar), though management is similar.
Rickettsial serology (IgM/IgG, species-specific where available) or Weil-Felix (less specific)
Supports diagnosis, though treatment should not be delayed pending results when clinical suspicion is high.
CBC, LFTs, renal function, Assesses organ involvement; thrombocytopenia and transaminitis are common supportive findings.
Antibiotics recommended
# Reason
Fever with maculopapular rash and headache, particularly with tick exposure history, is consistent with a spotted fever group rickettsial infection (distinct from scrub typhus, which classically presents with an eschar from chigger/mite bites rather than a generalized maculopapular rash from tick-borne transmission). Empiric doxycycline should be started promptly given the excellent and rapid response to treatment and the risk of progression if delayed.
# Pathophysiology
Rickettsiae and Orientia are obligately intracellular gram-negative coccobacilli that primarily infect vascular endothelial cells. After tick saliva inoculates organisms into the dermis (in RMSF after roughly 6 h of feeding), they spread lymphohematogenously and infect contiguous foci of endothelium throughout the body, producing a widespread infectious vasculitis with increased vascular permeability. The resulting edema, hypovolemia, and tissue ischemia (rather than occlusive thrombosis) drive the organ injury: prerenal azotemia, noncardiogenic pulmonary edema, and CNS dysfunction. Platelet consumption causes thrombocytopenia in about a third to half of RMSF cases, but frank DIC with hypofibrinogenemia is rare; the coagulation changes appear to be homeostatic responses to endothelial injury forming nonocclusive hemostatic plugs.
# Prognosis
Outcome is strongly time-dependent: untreated RMSF carries mortality of roughly 20-25% (historic) with confirmed-case fatality around 9-10% where R. rickettsii is the sole agent, versus ~3-5% when treated early; delayed recognition and treatment markedly increase death. For unclear reasons RMSF case-fatality approaches 50% in Mexico and Brazil. Severe Mediterranean spotted fever has mortality near 50% without treatment but improves dramatically with even a single day of appropriate therapy. Untreated epidemic typhus historically killed up to 60% and still exceeds 10-15% in outbreaks. Many survivors of severe RMSF recover fully, but permanent neurologic deficits and gangrene requiring amputation can follow severe illness.
# Severity classification
Higher-risk groups include children <10 years, adults >70 years, the immunosuppressed, and (for fulminant RMSF) males with G6PD deficiency.
# Clinical pearls
All acute rickettsial illnesses look alike for roughly the first 5 days: fever, headache, and myalgias, often with nausea, vomiting, or cough; the distinguishing features (rash, eschar, pneumonitis, meningoencephalitis) emerge only as the illness progresses.
An inoculation eschar (tache noire) is a necrotic, often painless black-crusted lesion with a surrounding erythematous halo at the bite site; it can appear during the incubation period and is common in Mediterranean spotted fever (~50%), African tick-bite fever (~90%), and rickettsialpox (~90%), but is essentially absent in classic RMSF (<1%) and in epidemic/murine typhus.
African tick-bite fever (R. africae) is the rickettsiosis most often imported into Europe and North America via travelers returning from sub-Saharan Africa; because Amblyomma ticks feed in groups, multiple eschars and regional lymphadenopathy are characteristic, and rash may be vesicular, sparse, or absent.
In RMSF the rash is centrifugal and evolving: erythematous macules begin on wrists and ankles, spread to extremities and trunk, and only later involve palms and soles; macules progress to non-blanching petechiae as vascular damage advances.
Palm/sole involvement and the classic centripetal/centrifugal spread are late and inconsistent: in RMSF palms/soles are involved after day 5 in only ~43% and never in 18-64% of cases.
The full classic triad of fever, rash, and recalled tick exposure is present in only ~3% of RMSF patients during the first 3 days, so its absence does not exclude the diagnosis.
Characteristic supportive labs across rickettsioses are thrombocytopenia, a normal or low (not elevated) WBC count, mildly elevated hepatic aminotransferases, and hyponatremia; hyponatremia in RMSF (~56%) reflects appropriate ADH secretion in a hypovolemic state.
Spotted fever group serology cross-reacts among species, so a positive titer confirms a SFG rickettsiosis but generally cannot identify the specific organism.
# Additional differentials
Human monocytotropic ehrlichiosis (Ehrlichia chaffeensis) and human granulocytotropic anaplasmosis (Anaplasma phagocytophilum) - tick-borne, often rashless, share thrombocytopenia/leukopenia/transaminitis
Epidemic (louse-borne) typhus (R. prowazekii) and recrudescent Brill-Zinsser disease
Murine (endemic) typhus (R. typhi)
Q fever (Coxiella burnetii)
# Infection mimics
Influenza and enteroviral infection (mimic early rashless RMSF)
Infectious mononucleosis
Viral hepatitis
Leptospirosis
Typhoid fever (epidemic typhus is frequently misdiagnosed as typhoid in tropical countries)
Gram-negative or gram-positive bacterial sepsis / septic shock
Meningococcemia and disseminated gonococcal infection
Secondary syphilis
Measles (rubeola) and rubella
Dengue and hantavirus infection (share epidemiologic and clinical features)
Non infectious mimics: Drug hypersensitivity reaction; Immune (idiopathic) thrombocytopenic purpura; Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura; Kawasaki syndrome; Immune complex vasculitis; Toxic shock syndrome.
# Additional investigations
Indirect immunofluorescence assay (IFA) for SFG IgG - the most common confirmatory test; sensitivity 89-100% and specificity 99-100%, but a reactive titer (>=64) is not detectable until 7-10 days after onset and is usually negative at presentation
Immunohistologic examination or PCR of a 3-mm punch biopsy of a rash lesion - the only test useful during acute illness (immunohistology ~70% sensitive, 100% specific; PCR likely higher)
PCR of eschar biopsy or eschar swab, or skin biopsy, for SFG rickettsiae
Note: PCR of peripheral blood is NOT adequately sensitive because circulating organism quantities are low
Platelet count, WBC count, hepatic aminotransferases, serum sodium, and renal function as supportive clues
