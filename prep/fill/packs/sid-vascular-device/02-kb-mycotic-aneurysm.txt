# Mycotic (infective) aneurysm
Mycotic (infective) aneurysm, Rheumatology / Vascular, Rheumatology / Vascular Medicine.
Aliases: infective aneurysm; infected aneurysm; microbial arteritis with aneurysm; Salmonella aortitis; Staphylococcus aureus aortitis.
# Pathophysiology
A mycotic (infective) aneurysm forms when microorganisms infect and destroy the arterial wall. Bacteria reach the vessel through haematogenous seeding of the vasa vasorum, septic embolism (as in endocarditis), contiguous spread from adjacent infection, or direct inoculation. Organisms such as Salmonella, with its affinity for atherosclerotic and diseased intima, and Staphylococcus aureus incite suppurative inflammation that weakens and digests the wall, producing a rapidly expanding, often saccular aneurysm. The infected, structurally compromised wall is prone to early rupture and life-threatening haemorrhage.
# Prognosis
Mycotic aneurysm carries high mortality, particularly if rupture occurs, but prompt combined surgical or endovascular repair and prolonged targeted antimicrobial therapy substantially improve survival. Relapse of infection and graft complications require long-term follow-up.
# Clinical pearls
Refers to an infected arterial aneurysm, not a fungal one; most cases are bacterial
Salmonella species and Staphylococcus aureus are the most common causative organisms
Presents with fever, localised pain, and a tender, often rapidly enlarging pulsatile mass
Imaging may show a saccular, lobulated, or rapidly growing aneurysm with periaortic gas, fluid, or soft tissue
Positive blood cultures with a new or expanding aneurysm strongly support the diagnosis
Rupture risk is high and the process can progress quickly, so it is a surgical emergency
Treatment combines urgent surgical or endovascular repair with prolonged targeted antimicrobial therapy
Additional differentials: Degenerative (atherosclerotic) aneurysm; Inflammatory aortic aneurysm; Non-infectious aortitis; Contained aneurysm rupture; Perivascular abscess or retroperitoneal infection; Penetrating aortic ulcer.
# Additional investigations
Multiple blood cultures before starting antimicrobials
CT angiography for aneurysm morphology and periaortic gas or fluid
PET or labelled white-cell imaging in diagnostically uncertain cases
Echocardiography to assess for infective endocarditis as a source
Inflammatory markers and full blood count
Intraoperative tissue and pus samples for microbiological culture
# Red flags
Sudden severe pain or haemodynamic collapse indicating rupture
Rapidly enlarging aneurysm on serial imaging
Sepsis with a pulsatile, tender mass
Periaortic gas suggesting aggressive infection
# Pitfalls
Attributing a rapidly enlarging aneurysm to degeneration and missing infection
Starting antimicrobials before obtaining blood cultures
Delaying surgical referral in a febrile patient with a tender pulsatile mass
Underestimating the duration of antimicrobial therapy needed after repair
# Management
Treat as a surgical emergency with urgent open surgical or endovascular repair, involving debridement of infected tissue, aneurysm resection, and revascularisation
Take multiple blood cultures before starting antimicrobials and give prolonged targeted intravenous therapy guided by results, using anti-staphylococcal agents for Staphylococcus aureus and a fluoroquinolone or ceftriaxone for Salmonella
Start empirical broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics after cultures while awaiting microbiology, then narrow to targeted therapy
Identify and manage the source, including treating infective endocarditis where septic embolism is the origin
Resuscitate and provide haemodynamic support for rupture and haemorrhage, which is the dominant life-threatening risk
Maintain long-term follow-up with serial imaging and inflammatory markers for relapse and graft complications, using prolonged or suppressive antibiotics in selected patients
Manage through a multidisciplinary team of vascular surgery and infectious diseases
