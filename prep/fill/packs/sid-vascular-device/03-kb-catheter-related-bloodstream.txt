# Catheter-related bloodstream infection
Catheter-related bloodstream infection, Infectious Diseases, Infectious Diseases.
Aliases: CRBSI; CLABSI; central line-associated bloodstream infection; line infection.
# Pathophysiology
Intravascular catheters can become infected by several routes: migration of skin flora along the external catheter surface to the tip, contamination of the catheter hub and lumen during handling, haematogenous seeding from a distant focus, or, rarely, contaminated infusate. Microorganisms adhere to the catheter and form a biofilm within a matrix that shields them from host defences and antibiotics, allowing persistent shedding of organisms into the bloodstream. This biofilm explains why eradication frequently requires removing the device rather than antibiotics alone.
# Prognosis
Prognosis depends on the organism and host; uncomplicated coagulase-negative staphylococcal infection often resolves with line removal, whereas S. aureus, Candida, or complicated infections carry higher morbidity, mortality, and risk of metastatic complications.
# Clinical pearls
A bloodstream infection attributable to an intravascular catheter, most often a central venous line, in a patient with no other apparent source
Coagulase-negative staphylococci, Staphylococcus aureus, enterococci, Gram-negative bacilli, and Candida species are the usual culprits
Diagnosis is supported by paired blood cultures drawn simultaneously from the catheter and a peripheral vein, with a shorter time to positivity from the catheter (differential time to positivity)
Purulence or erythema at the exit site, or clinical improvement after line removal, supports the diagnosis but is not always present
Management principles centre on removing or exchanging the catheter, particularly for S. aureus, Candida, Pseudomonas, or complicated infection, alongside targeted antibiotics
Antibiotic lock therapy may allow catheter salvage in selected long-term lines infected with less virulent organisms
S. aureus and candidal line infections mandate a search for metastatic complications such as endocarditis, and echocardiography should be considered
Prevention relies on strict insertion and maintenance bundles, hand hygiene, and prompt removal of unnecessary lines
# Additional differentials
Bloodstream infection from another source (urinary, respiratory, abdominal)
Contaminated blood culture from skin flora
Infective endocarditis
Localised exit-site or tunnel infection without bacteraemia
Non-infective causes of fever in a hospitalised patient
Infection mimics: Blood culture contamination with coagulase-negative staphylococci; Drug fever or transfusion reaction; Thrombophlebitis without infection; Post-operative or malignancy-associated fever.
# Additional investigations
Paired peripheral and catheter-drawn blood cultures for differential time to positivity
Culture of the catheter tip after removal
Echocardiography when S. aureus, candidal, or persistent bacteraemia is present
