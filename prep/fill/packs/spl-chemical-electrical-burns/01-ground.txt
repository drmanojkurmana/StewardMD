Exam grounding notes for electrical, chemical, and cold injuries, written for grounding, not for display. Paraphrased from Plastic Surgery 5E Volume 4 on special burn injuries.

High-voltage electrical injury means contact above 1000 volts. Current passes through deep muscle and bone while the entry skin wound can look deceptively small. Expect extensive muscle necrosis and even osseous injury beneath intact-looking skin, plus cardiac conduction injury along the current path.

Dying muscle releases myoglobin, which precipitates inside renal tubules and causes acute kidney injury. Management keeps urine flowing at 1.5 to 2.0 mL per kg per hour with aggressive fluids and alkalinises the urine with sodium bicarbonate. Dark urine after an electrical injury is treated as myoglobinuria until proven otherwise.

Every high-voltage injury gets continuous electrocardiographic monitoring for at least 24 hours. Ventricular fibrillation and other arrhythmias can follow the current path through the thorax, sometimes with a delayed onset. A normal first tracing does not end surveillance.

Chemical burns are irrigated at once with copious water for a minimum of 30 to 60 minutes. Neutralizing agents are avoided because acid-base reactions release heat and deepen the injury. Brush off dry powder before wet irrigation, and keep irrigating through transfer.

Hydrofluoric acid produces severe liquefactive necrosis and binds calcium and magnesium through fluoride ion chelation. Victims develop life-threatening hypocalcemia and hypomagnesemia with relatively small surface burns. Pain out of proportion to the visible wound is the classic clue.

The specific antidote is calcium gluconate: immediate topical 2.5% calcium gluconate gel massaged into the burn, escalated to subcutaneous or intra-arterial calcium gluconate injection when pain persists or systemic signs appear. Calcium replaces what fluoride has locked away.

Frostbite is rewarmed rapidly in a circulating water bath held at 37 to 42 degrees Celsius for 15 to 30 minutes, until the distal skin turns erythematous and pliable. Refreezing after thaw is forbidden, and blisters are left intact during initial care.

Surgical decisions wait: definitive debridement or amputation for severe frostbite is delayed for 6 to 12 weeks so that viable tissue can demarcate from dead tissue. Early amputation sacrifices length that would have survived.
