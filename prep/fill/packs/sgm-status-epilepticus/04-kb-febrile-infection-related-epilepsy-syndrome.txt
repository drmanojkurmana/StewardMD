# Febrile infection-related epilepsy syndrome
Febrile infection-related epilepsy syndrome, Nervous system, Paediatric neurology.
# Aliases
FIRES
Acute encephalitis with refractory repetitive partial seizures
# Pathophysiology
FIRES is thought to be an immune-mediated encephalopathy triggered by a preceding febrile infection in a genetically susceptible child, rather than a direct central nervous system infection. Activation of innate immunity with excessive pro-inflammatory cytokine signalling, particularly the interleukin-1-beta pathway, is proposed to lower seizure threshold and drive self-sustaining status epilepticus and neuronal injury. A vicious cycle of seizures, neuroinflammation and metabolic stress perpetuates the refractory state, with the hippocampus especially vulnerable.
# Prognosis
Poor overall, with appreciable mortality in the acute phase and a high burden of chronic drug-resistant epilepsy, cognitive impairment and disability among survivors; earlier immunomodulation and ketogenic therapy may improve outcomes.
# Clinical pearls
A catastrophic epileptic encephalopathy in previously healthy children, defined by refractory status epilepticus beginning between 24 hours and 2 weeks after a febrile illness, often with fever absent at seizure onset.
Considered the paediatric subtype of new-onset refractory status epilepticus (NORSE) with an obligatory preceding febrile infection.
Seizures are typically focal with secondary generalisation, extremely frequent and highly drug-resistant, often requiring prolonged anaesthetic coma.
It is a diagnosis of exclusion, as extensive investigation usually fails to identify a specific infective or autoimmune cause.
Emerging evidence implicates innate immune, cytokine-mediated neuroinflammation, notably the interleukin-1 pathway.
Early ketogenic diet and immunotherapy, including interleukin-1 blockade, are increasingly used in the acute phase.
Survivors typically enter a chronic phase of drug-resistant epilepsy with cognitive and behavioural impairment.
MRI may be normal early and later show mesial temporal and diffuse atrophy, while CSF may show mild pleocytosis.
# Additional differentials
Autoimmune or antibody-mediated encephalitis, including anti-NMDA-receptor encephalitis
Viral and bacterial meningoencephalitis
Other causes of NORSE (paraneoplastic, cryptogenic)
Mitochondrial and other metabolic encephalopathies
Genetic epileptic encephalopathies such as POLG- and PCDH19-related disease
Toxic encephalopathy
# Additional investigations
Continuous EEG monitoring
MRI brain, initial and serial
Comprehensive CSF analysis including cell count, viral PCR and autoimmune antibodies
Serum and CSF autoimmune and paraneoplastic antibody panels
Infective screen of blood, CSF and respiratory samples to exclude active infection
Metabolic and mitochondrial workup with genetic testing
Inflammatory markers and cytokine profiling where available
# Red flags
Refractory status epilepticus requiring escalating anaesthesia
Autonomic instability and complications of prolonged critical illness
Rising intracranial pressure or cerebral oedema
Multi-organ complications of prolonged anaesthetic therapy
# Pitfalls
Assuming a treatable central nervous system infection and delaying immunomodulation or ketogenic diet.
Labelling the presentation as ordinary febrile status epilepticus and underestimating its severity.
Delaying the ketogenic diet, which can be effective but requires setup.
Failing to exclude autoimmune encephalitis and metabolic or genetic mimics.
Under-recognising the complications of prolonged therapeutic coma.
# Management
Treat the refractory status epilepticus aggressively with escalating antiseizure medications and anaesthetic burst-suppression using agents such as midazolam, thiopental or ketamine
Introduce the ketogenic diet early in the acute phase
Give immunotherapy with corticosteroids, intravenous immunoglobulin and/or plasma exchange for the presumed immune-mediated encephalopathy
Target the interleukin-1 pathway with anakinra, with tocilizumab or other agents considered in refractory cases
Provide intensive neurocritical supportive care with continuous EEG monitoring
Manage chronic drug-resistant epilepsy and cognitive and neurorehabilitation needs in survivors
Coordinate paediatric neurology, intensive care and immunology multidisciplinary input
