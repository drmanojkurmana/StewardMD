# Pathophysiology
A seizure reflects a shift in the normal balance between excitation and inhibition in the CNS. At the cellular level the hallmark of focal seizure activity is the electrographic spike, generated by near-simultaneous bursting (the 'paroxysmal depolarization shift') of local excitatory neurons - a prolonged membrane depolarization driven by calcium influx that opens voltage-gated sodium channels, followed by a GABA- or potassium-mediated hyperpolarizing afterpotential. Surrounding inhibition normally limits spread; when recruitment overwhelms it (via rising extracellular potassium, presynaptic calcium accumulation with enhanced transmitter release, NMDA-receptor activation, and ephaptic effects), activity propagates through local cortical and long commissural pathways (e.g., corpus callosum). Generalized 3-Hz spike-and-wave discharges of absence seizures arise from thalamocortical oscillatory circuits involving GABA-B receptors, T-type calcium channels, and potassium channels. Many causes converge on three interacting factor classes: endogenous (genetic/developmental seizure-threshold) factors, epileptogenic factors (CNS injury such as severe penetrating head trauma, stroke, infection, or neurodegeneration that, over months to years, transforms a normal network into a chronically hyperexcitable one - 'epileptogenesis'), and precipitating factors (sleep deprivation, stress, hormonal change, toxins, certain drugs, photic stimulation). Many inherited epilepsies are channelopathies due to ion-channel mutations.
# Prognosis
About half of people respond to the first antiseizure drug; roughly 30% have ongoing seizures despite drug combinations (refractory epilepsy). People with epilepsy have a 2-3 times higher mortality than matched controls, mostly driven by the underlying etiology (e.g., tumor, stroke); excess deaths also arise from accidents, status epilepticus, and sudden unexpected death in epilepsy (SUDEP), which typically affects young patients with convulsive seizures and tends to occur at night, possibly via brainstem-mediated cardiorespiratory/arousal effects. Simple febrile seizures do not raise the risk of later epilepsy, whereas complex febrile seizures carry a 2-5% risk. Severe penetrating head trauma carries up to a ~45% (penetrating/depressed fracture/hemorrhage/prolonged coma: 30-50%) risk of subsequent epilepsy, closed head injury with contusion 5-25%, and mild concussion only slightly increased risk. Favorable factors for staying seizure-free after planned drug withdrawal include 1-5 years of control, a single seizure type, normal exam/intelligence, normal EEG, and no family history.
# Severity classification
Seizures are classified (ILAE 2017) by onset: (1) Focal onset (with intact or impaired awareness; motor or nonmotor onset; may evolve to bilateral tonic-clonic); (2) Generalized onset - motor (tonic-clonic, or other such as atonic/myoclonic) or nonmotor (absence); (3) Unknown onset. Status epilepticus is staged by duration/refractoriness in management terms: impending/early (5-30 min), established and early-refractory (30 min to 48 h), and late refractory (>48 h), with subtypes generalized convulsive (GCSE) versus nonconvulsive (NCSE).
# Clinical pearls
A seizure is a transient event from abnormal excessive or synchronous neuronal activity; epilepsy is the distinct condition of recurrent unprovoked seizures from a chronic underlying process. A single seizure, or seizures due to a correctable/avoidable cause, is not by itself epilepsy.
Operationally, epilepsy is defined as two or more unprovoked seizures, though a single seizure with clinical or EEG features portending high recurrence risk can establish the diagnosis.
Seizures are fundamentally classified as focal (network limited to one hemisphere; often structural) versus generalized (rapidly engaging both hemispheres) versus unknown onset; the older terms 'partial', 'simple/complex partial' are no longer used.
Focal seizures are subclassified by awareness (intact vs impaired) and onset (motor vs nonmotor); a focal seizure can evolve to a bilateral tonic-clonic seizure (formerly 'secondary generalization').
Distinguishing a focal seizure that evolves to generalized from a primary generalized tonic-clonic seizure is critical but difficult, because bystanders emphasize the dramatic convulsive phase and overlook the subtle focal onset; a careful history for a preceding aura or focal features, and EEG, are needed because evaluation and treatment differ substantially.
Status epilepticus is continuous seizures, or repetitive discrete seizures with impaired consciousness between them; for generalized convulsive status epilepticus the practical operational threshold is seizures lasting beyond 5 minutes (older definitions used 15-30 min).
Generalized convulsive status epilepticus (GCSE) is a medical emergency: prolonged seizures cause cardiorespiratory dysfunction, hyperthermia, and metabolic derangements that can produce irreversible CNS injury.
Treatment principle for status epilepticus is an escalating sequence beginning with a benzodiazepine, followed by an IV antiseizure drug, with anesthetic agents for refractory cases - alongside attention to airway, oxygenation, glucose, and reversible metabolic causes (no doses given here).
After 30-45 min of uninterrupted GCSE the motor signs can become very subtle (only fine finger twitching or rapid eye movements); if a patient stops overt convulsions yet remains comatose, an EEG is required to exclude ongoing (nonconvulsive) status epilepticus.
If the sole cause of a seizure is a correctable metabolic disturbance (e.g., electrolyte or glucose abnormality), or a precipitant drug/illicit substance, treatment targets the underlying problem and chronic antiseizure drugs are usually unnecessary.
An apparently unprovoked first seizure carries a recurrence risk estimated at roughly 31-71% in the first 12 months; accepted risk factors favoring treatment are prior brain insult (stroke/trauma), epileptiform EEG, a significant imaging abnormality, or a nocturnal seizure.
A serum prolactin rise in the 30 min after most generalized and some focal seizures can support an epileptic cause, but it is unreliable because baseline levels are rarely available and some drugs elevate prolactin.
The clinical range of focal seizure behaviors is extremely broad, so caution is advised before dismissing paroxysmal stereotypic bizarre episodes as non-epileptic.
The age of the patient strongly informs the likely etiology of new seizures - e.g., febrile seizures and genetic/developmental causes in young children versus cerebrovascular disease, tumor, metabolic disturbance and degenerative disease in older adults.
# Additional differentials
Syncope (vasovagal, cardiac arrhythmia, valvular heart disease, cardiac failure, orthostatic hypotension) - the most frequent diagnostic dilemma versus generalized seizure
Transient ischemic attack, particularly basilar artery TIA
Migraine - confusional migraine, basilar migraine
Acute psychosis
Sleep disorders - narcolepsy/cataplexy, benign sleep myoclonus
Movement disorders - tics, nonepileptic myoclonus, paroxysmal choreoathetosis
In children: breath-holding spells, benign paroxysmal vertigo, night terrors, sleepwalking, apnea, abdominal migraine with cyclic vomiting
# Infection mimics
CNS infection (meningitis/encephalitis) presenting with seizures and altered mentation - lumbar puncture indicated if suspected and mandatory in all HIV-infected patients even when asymptomatic
Parasitic CNS infection such as neurocysticercosis as a cause of seizures in adolescents/young adults from endemic areas
# Non infectious mimics
Psychogenic nonepileptic seizures (PNES) - nonepileptic behaviors, often a conversion reaction; clues include side-to-side head turning, ictal eye closure, asynchronous large-amplitude limb shaking, four-limb twitching without loss of consciousness, pelvic thrusting, and prolonged waxing/waning episodes; surface EEG may be normal especially for frontal-lobe-like events, and PNES can coexist with true epilepsy
Convulsive syncope - a brief (1-10 s) burst of convulsive movements at the onset of a faint, especially if the patient stays upright; postictal confusion is much less prominent than after a true seizure
Alcoholic blackouts and delirium tremens
Hypoglycemia and hypoxia as metabolic mimics of seizure
Hyperventilation and panic attack
Effects of psychoactive drugs / hallucinogens
# Additional investigations
Complete blood count, magnesium, liver and renal function tests, and urinalysis in addition to glucose/electrolytes/calcium to find metabolic causes
Toxicology screen of blood and urine, especially when no clear precipitant is identified
Lumbar puncture when meningitis/encephalitis is suspected; mandatory in all HIV-infected patients even without signs of infection
Serum and CSF autoantibody testing when epilepsy onset is fulminant or accompanied by psychiatric/cognitive disturbance (autoimmune/paraneoplastic encephalitis)
MRI preferred over CT for detecting structural lesions; CT performed emergently when MRI is not immediately available or when a mass/infection is suspected
Interictal and ictal EEG, with activating procedures (hyperventilation, photic stimulation, sleep, sleep deprivation); a normal routine interictal EEG does NOT exclude epilepsy - it is normal in 50-80% of known epileptics initially
Continuous video-EEG telemetry for poorly characterized events or seizures that are difficult to control, and to distinguish epileptic from psychogenic events
Genetic testing in infants/children with suspected genetic epilepsy syndromes, and selected older patients with an undiagnosed early-life epilepsy syndrome
# Red flags
Generalized convulsive status epilepticus - immediate emergency due to risk of cardiorespiratory failure, hyperthermia, metabolic derangement, and irreversible CNS injury
Persistent coma after overt convulsions stop - suspect ongoing nonconvulsive status epilepticus and obtain EEG
Focal features, focal neurologic deficit, or abnormal/changing neurologic exam - points to a structural cause (mass, stroke, infection, trauma, degenerative disease) requiring imaging
Fulminant new-onset seizures with psychiatric symptoms and cognitive change - suspect autoimmune/antibody-mediated encephalitis
First seizure in a patient with HIV - lumbar puncture is mandatory even without signs of infection
Atonic ('drop attack') seizures - substantial risk of direct head injury from the fall
# Pitfalls
Failing to exclude a treatable provoking cause (metabolic, toxic, structural, or infective) before labeling a patient as having epilepsy - many such seizures need only correction of the underlying problem, not chronic antiseizure drugs.
Assuming a normal interictal EEG rules out epilepsy - the initial routine EEG is normal in 50-80% of patients with known epilepsy, and EEG has limited sensitivity.
Missing nonconvulsive status epilepticus: in altered consciousness or persistent coma without overt convulsions, NCSE can be present and is only revealed by EEG; intermittent seizures with prolonged postictal states are easily overlooked as causes of unexplained encephalopathy.
Overlooking a focal onset by focusing on the dramatic generalized convulsive phase, thereby misclassifying the seizure and choosing the wrong evaluation/treatment pathway.
Mistaking psychogenic nonepileptic seizures for epilepsy (or vice versa) on clinical grounds alone - even experienced epileptologists err; PNES and epilepsy can coexist, and video-EEG is the arbiter.
Misinterpreting convulsive syncope (brief convulsive jerks at the start of a faint) as a primary seizure.
Relying on headache or incontinence to distinguish seizure from syncope - these features are unreliable.
Forgetting that the postictal Todd's paralysis (focal weakness for minutes to hours after a focal seizure) can mimic an acute structural deficit such as stroke.
Misdiagnosing childhood absence epilepsy as attention deficit disorder or simple 'daydreaming'.

# Steps
Status epilepticus: protect airway, O2, glucose; IV lorazepam (or IM midazolam), repeat once.
Second-line: IV levetiracetam, valproate or phenytoin/fosphenytoin.
Refractory status → ICU, intubation, anaesthetic infusion (propofol/midazolam).
Correct provoking causes (hypoglycaemia, hyponatraemia, alcohol, drugs, infection).
First unprovoked seizure: usually no drug; start AED if high recurrence risk / abnormal EEG-MRI; neurology referral, driving advice.
Status epilepticus: benzodiazepine first, then 2nd-line AED
