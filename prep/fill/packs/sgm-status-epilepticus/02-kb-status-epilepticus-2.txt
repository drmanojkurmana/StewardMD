# Status epilepticus
Status epilepticus, Neurology.
Aliases: status epilepticus; SE; convulsive status epilepticus; non-convulsive status epilepticus; NCSE; refractory status epilepticus; seizure emergency; prolonged seizure.
# Pathophysiology
Status epilepticus arises from a failure of the mechanisms that normally terminate seizure activity, leading to sustained abnormal neuronal hypersynchronization. This results from a relative excess of excitatory (glutamatergic) neurotransmission and depletion or dysfunction of inhibitory (GABAergic) signaling, with progressive receptor trafficking changes that make prolonged seizures increasingly resistant to GABA-acting drugs over time. Prolonged seizure activity can itself cause excitotoxic neuronal injury independent of the underlying cause, and is accompanied by systemic derangements including hyperthermia, acidosis, hypoxia, and autonomic instability that compound neuronal damage.
# Prognosis
Outcome depends heavily on etiology, age, and duration of status before treatment, with prolonged or refractory episodes carrying substantially higher morbidity and mortality. Even with successful seizure termination, patients face risk of cognitive sequelae and recurrent epilepsy, particularly when status is due to an acute severe brain insult.
# Clinical pearls
Defined operationally as a single seizure lasting beyond the time most seizures of that type would normally stop (roughly 5 minutes for convulsive seizures) or recurrent seizures without recovery of consciousness in between.
Treat convulsive status epilepticus as a time-critical emergency — earlier treatment initiation strongly predicts easier termination and better outcome.
Airway, breathing, circulation, glucose, and thiamine should be addressed simultaneously with, not after, anticonvulsant therapy.
Always check a bedside glucose early; hypoglycemia is a rapidly reversible cause that mimics or precipitates status epilepticus.
Non-convulsive status epilepticus should be suspected in any patient with unexplained persistent altered consciousness, especially after a convulsive seizure has been treated but the patient fails to wake up.
Continuous EEG monitoring is essential once convulsions have stopped to confirm electrographic seizure resolution, particularly in patients who remain unresponsive.
Refractory status epilepticus (persisting despite adequate first- and second-line therapy) generally requires escalation to continuous anesthetic infusions with EEG guidance in an ICU setting.
Identifying and treating the underlying etiology is as important as stopping the seizure itself, since ongoing precipitants (e.g., infection, toxin, structural lesion) will otherwise perpetuate the episode.
# Additional differentials
Psychogenic non-epileptic seizures (functional/dissociative attacks)
Syncope with convulsive movements
Movement disorders (e.g., dystonic storm, severe tremor)
Toxic or metabolic encephalopathy without true seizure activity
Rigors or shivering from severe sepsis
Post-anoxic myoclonus
# Infection mimics
Bacterial meningitis or meningoencephalitis
Viral encephalitis (e.g., herpes simplex encephalitis)
Cerebral abscess or CNS tuberculosis with seizures
Neurocysticercosis
# Non infectious mimics
Acute stroke or intracerebral hemorrhage
Severe metabolic derangement (hyponatremia, hypoglycemia, uremia, hepatic encephalopathy)
Drug intoxication or withdrawal (alcohol, benzodiazepine, sedative withdrawal; proconvulsant drug toxicity)
Autoimmune or paraneoplastic encephalitis
Hypoxic-ischemic brain injury
Eclampsia in pregnancy
# Additional investigations
Bedside capillary glucose — rapid identification of reversible hypoglycemic cause
Serum electrolytes, calcium, magnesium, renal and liver function — screen for metabolic precipitants
Anticonvulsant drug levels (if patient on prior therapy) — detect subtherapeutic levels or nonadherence
Toxicology screen — identify intoxication or withdrawal etiology
Continuous EEG monitoring — detect non-convulsive status epilepticus and confirm electrographic seizure termination
Neuroimaging (CT or MRI brain) — identify structural lesion, hemorrhage, or stroke, generally once initially stabilized
Lumbar puncture (after imaging if indicated) — evaluate for CNS infection or inflammation when fever or clinical suspicion present
Arterial blood gas and core temperature — assess for acidosis and hyperthermia from prolonged seizure activity
# Red flags
Seizure activity continuing beyond the expected duration without response to initial therapy — escalate treatment promptly
Persistent unresponsiveness after convulsions have visibly stopped — evaluate for non-convulsive status epilepticus
Hemodynamic instability, hyperthermia, or severe acidosis during prolonged seizure activity
Focal neurologic signs or papilledema suggesting an acute structural intracranial process
Fever with meningismus suggesting an infectious CNS cause requiring urgent antimicrobial coverage
Failure to respond to first- and second-line agents, indicating refractory status epilepticus requiring ICU-level anesthetic therapy
# Pitfalls
Assuming convulsions have stopped means the seizure is over — non-convulsive status epilepticus can persist electrographically with only subtle clinical signs.
Delaying anticonvulsant administration while awaiting imaging or laboratory results, which worsens the chance of terminating the seizure.
Underdosing initial therapy out of caution about respiratory depression, which increases the risk of treatment failure and need for escalation.
Failing to search for and treat the underlying precipitant, allowing the episode to recur despite initial seizure control.
# Management
Secure airway, breathing and circulation, give supplemental oxygen, and treat reversible precipitants immediately with glucose-guided intravenous dextrose plus thiamine and correction of electrolyte disturbance
Abort seizures with a benzodiazepine as first-line therapy, using intravenous lorazepam or intramuscular or buccal midazolam when there is no venous access
If seizures persist, load an intravenous second-line antiseizure agent such as levetiracetam, sodium valproate or fosphenytoin/phenytoin
For refractory status, intubate and induce anaesthesia with a continuous infusion of midazolam, propofol or thiopental titrated to seizure suppression
Establish continuous EEG monitoring to confirm electrographic seizure termination and detect non-convulsive status in patients who fail to wake
Investigate and treat the underlying cause with neuroimaging, drug levels, toxicology and evaluation for CNS infection while managing systemic complications such as hyperthermia and acidosis
Manage in an intensive care setting and refer to neurology for the ongoing antiseizure regimen
