Functional pancreatic NETs: the big picture
- Pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors (pNETs) start in islet-type hormone cells. Most are non-functioning and turn up by chance or because they press on nearby organs.
- Roughly 20 percent of pNETs make enough hormone to cause a recognizable syndrome.
- Frequency order of the functional types: insulinoma first, then glucagonoma and gastrinoma.
- Some pNETs secrete several hormones, and the pattern can change over time.

Insulinoma
- Insulin excess causes hypoglycemia: confusion, sweating, shakiness, and in severe cases seizure or coma.
- Whipple triad: symptoms of low glucose, a low glucose value measured accurately, and relief once glucose is given.
- Confirmation: high fasting insulin with high proinsulin and C-peptide. Exogenous insulin lowers C-peptide, which helps separate factitious use.
- Most insulinomas are small, the majority under 2 cm. Endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) is the best local test.
- Nonmetastatic insulinoma is benign in 95 percent of patients and is usually about 1 cm. Enucleation with the capsule is standard, and nodal dissection is not needed when nothing has spread.

Gastrinoma and Zollinger-Ellison syndrome (ZES)
- Gastrin drives acid secretion, causing refractory peptic ulcers, diarrhea, and reflux.
- Fasting gastrin is usually above 600 pg/mL, and virtually every patient is above 100 pg/mL.
- Gastrin of 1,000 pg/mL or more, with gastric pH of 2 or less, makes the diagnosis.
- Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) raise gastrin and can mislead the test.
- In sporadic disease 50 to 90 percent of gastrinomas sit in the duodenum, usually small.
- Up to 25 percent of ZES patients have multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 (MEN 1). MEN 1 gastrinomas tend to be small and multiple, and partial pancreatectomy risks new tumors in the remaining gland.

Glucagonoma, VIPoma, somatostatinoma
- Glucagonoma: necrolytic migratory erythema in skin folds, glucose intolerance, weight loss. Plasma glucagon is generally above 1,000 pg/mL. Most are large when found.
- VIPoma: watery diarrhea, hypokalemia, achlorhydria, the Verner-Morrison picture. In adults most arise in the pancreas.
- Somatostatinoma: diabetes, gallbladder disease, steatorrhea. Often metastatic at presentation.

Malignant potential
- Most pNETs are malignant. Insulinoma is the main exception.
- Non-insulin pNETs can grow locally and metastasize if left untreated.
- Other pNETs often spread to regional lymph nodes.
- Gastrinoma: more than 60 percent considered malignant (local invasion or metastasis).

Primary aldosteronism (PA)
- Autonomous aldosterone secretion suppresses renin and causes hypertension. Hypokalemia occurs in only 10 to 40 percent.
- Prevalence among hypertensive patients in systematic screening is 5 to 12 percent.
- Screen: aldosterone-to-renin ratio (ARR). Positive when above 750 pmol/L per ng/mL per hour with aldosterone high normal or raised.
- Preparation: stop spironolactone for 4 to 6 weeks and correct hypokalemia.
- Confirmation: saline infusion of 2 liters over 4 hours. Aldosterone still above 170 pmol/L when seated indicates autonomous secretion.
- Fine-cut CT of the adrenals is the imaging method. Most aldosterone-producing adenomas are under 2 cm, and CT can miss lesions under 5 mm.
- Endocrine-inactive adenomas are the most common adrenal mass, at 40 to 70 percent of benign lesions. Aldosterone-producing adenomas are 2 to 5 percent.
- Patients under 35 with a unilateral lesion and no suspicion of a familial form can go to surgery without sampling.
- Patients over 35, or those with bilateral or micronodular disease, need adrenal venous sampling (AVS) first. Cortisol confirms catheter placement. Lateralization needs an aldosterone-to-cortisol ratio at least 2 times higher on one side.
- Medical therapy with spironolactone or eplerenone suits bilateral disease and patients who cannot have surgery.
