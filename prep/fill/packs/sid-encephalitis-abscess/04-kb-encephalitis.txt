# Pathogens

# Pathophysiology
Encephalitis is inflammation of the brain parenchyma, usually viral (or from a primary autoimmune process). Neurotropic viruses tend to injure characteristic CNS regions, producing the clinical pattern. Associated meningeal inflammation (meningoencephalitis) is common, and spinal cord or nerve-root involvement may co-occur (encephalomyelitis, encephalomyeloradiculitis). HSV has a predilection for the inferomedial frontotemporal/limbic regions, producing the characteristic temporal-lobe imaging and EEG changes and a hemorrhagic/necrotizing process; punctate microhemorrhages explain CSF red cells in some cases. Flaviviruses such as West Nile and Japanese encephalitis virus preferentially involve deep grey structures (thalamus, basal ganglia, brainstem). VZV tends to produce a CNS vasculopathy with hemorrhagic and ischemic infarction rather than a true parenchymal encephalitis. Some viral encephalitides (notably HSV, and Japanese encephalitis) can trigger a subsequent antibody-mediated autoimmune encephalitis, e.g. anti-NMDA-receptor antibodies after HSV.
# Prognosis
Outcome varies widely by etiology and host factors. For HSV encephalitis treated with aciclovir, roughly 81% survive but only about half of survivors have no or minor sequelae; outcome is directly related to patient age and to the level of consciousness at the start of therapy - younger patients with good neurologic function do markedly better, while those comatose (GCS ~6) at treatment initiation die or survive with severe deficits. Eastern equine encephalitis carries very high sequelae rates (~80% of survivors severely impaired), whereas EBV, California (La Crosse) and Venezuelan equine encephalitis rarely cause severe sequelae. West Nile survivors frequently have prolonged cognitive impairment, weakness, tremor, myoclonus and parkinsonism, with recovery taking many months. Japanese encephalitis case-fatality in hospitalized patients is high (~20-30%) with common long-term neurologic disability; rabies is almost invariably fatal.
# Clinical pearls
Encephalitis differs from meningitis by the presence of altered brain function itself - altered consciousness (confusion, behavioural change, lethargy to coma) and focal or diffuse neurologic signs - whereas uncomplicated meningitis spares brain parenchymal function.
Seizures (focal or generalized) occur in many patients with encephalitis, and prophylactic anticonvulsant therapy may be considered given the high seizure frequency in severe cases.
Clinical features alone cannot reliably establish the specific viral cause; presentations overlap too much, so etiology rests on CSF PCR, serology, imaging and epidemiologic clues.
Features pointing to inferomedial frontotemporal involvement - prominent olfactory or gustatory hallucinations, anosmia, bizarre behaviour or personality change, and memory disturbance - should specifically raise HSV encephalitis.
HSV is the single most common cause of sporadic encephalitis and the chief treatable cause, so the central diagnostic task once non-viral causes are excluded is to distinguish HSV from other viruses.
A CSF pleocytosis (>5 cells/uL) occurs in >95% of immunocompetent patients with viral encephalitis; severely immunocompromised patients (HIV, glucocorticoids/immunosuppressants, chemotherapy, lymphoreticular malignancy) may fail to mount this response.
CSF cell counts exceed 500/uL in only about 10% of cases; counts >1000/uL should prompt consideration of non-viral infection or other inflammatory processes.
A low CSF glucose is distinctly unusual in viral encephalitis and should suggest bacterial, fungal, tuberculous, parasitic, leptospiral, syphilitic, sarcoid or neoplastic disease instead.
Prominent brainstem involvement suggests flaviviruses (West Nile, St. Louis, Japanese encephalitis), HSV, enterovirus A71, rabies or Listeria; deep grey matter (basal ganglia/thalamus) involvement with movement disorders suggests flavivirus infection.
Persisting CSF neutrophilia should prompt consideration of bacterial infection, leptospirosis, amebic infection, and non-infectious acute hemorrhagic leukoencephalitis.
Empiric aciclovir for suspected HSV should be started immediately on clinical suspicion (especially with focal features) while awaiting diagnostic studies, and is more effective the earlier it is given.
# Additional differentials
Rabies (encephalitic/furious form with hydrophobia and aerophobia, or paralytic form) - bat exposure is the key risk in the US and a bite history may be lacking
Cytomegalovirus, EBV, HHV-6 encephalitis (notably in immunocompromised / allogeneic stem-cell transplant recipients)
Enterovirus A71 / D68 with acute flaccid myelitis-like presentation
Nipah virus encephalitis (Paramyxoviridae; Southeast Asian outbreaks)
SARS-CoV-2 - true viral CNS encephalitis (rare) versus encephalopathy from systemic/hyperinflammatory illness

# Non infectious mimics
Autoimmune anti-NMDA-receptor encephalitis - young women/children, viral-like prodrome then psychiatric symptoms, insomnia, reduced verbal output, seizures, dyskinesias, autonomic instability and hypoventilation; MRI often normal; can follow HSV encephalitis (NMDAR antibodies in up to 25% of HSV recoveries) and may be initially mistaken for primary psychosis
Other antibody-mediated autoimmune encephalitides - LGI1/CASPR2 (anti-VGKC complex), AMPA receptor, GABA receptor, GAD65
Paraneoplastic encephalitis with onconeural antibodies (anti-Hu, Yo, Ma2, amphiphysin, CRMP5/CV2)
Hashimoto's encephalopathy (anti-thyroglobulin / anti-thyroperoxidase antibodies)
Prion disease
Acute hemorrhagic leukoencephalitis
Metabolic/toxic encephalopathy (e.g. hyperammonemia, hepatic failure, intoxication)
# Additional investigations
Diffusion-weighted and FLAIR MRI sequences in addition to standard MRI - increase sensitivity for HSV temporal-lobe lesions (detect ~90% when added)
Repeat CSF HSV PCR if the initial test is obtained within the first ~72 h and clinical suspicion of HSV remains high (early PCR can be falsely negative)
CSF virus-specific IgM / intrathecal antibody synthesis (IgG index) - especially West Nile CSF IgM (diagnostic), and VZV CSF IgM as complementary to PCR; HSV CSF antibody testing useful when illness >1 week and PCR negative
Store frozen CSF (collect ~20 mL, freeze 5-10 mL) for later virus-specific PCR or metagenomic next-generation sequencing, as many neuroinvasive viruses are only transiently present in CSF
Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) of CSF/brain for obscure or immunocompromised-host cases
Autoimmune/paraneoplastic antibody panel in serum and CSF (e.g. NMDAR, LGI1, CASPR2, AMPAR, GABA-R, GAD65; onconeural antibodies) when infectious workup is negative
Brain biopsy - reserved for patients with focal MRI abnormality, negative CSF PCR, no autoimmune serology, who deteriorate despite aciclovir and supportive care
# Red flags
Focal neurologic findings on examination, neuroimaging, or EEG in suspected encephalitis - strongly suggest HSV and mandate immediate empiric aciclovir while testing proceeds
Severely depressed or declining level of consciousness / coma (Glasgow Coma Scale ~6 at start of therapy predicts death or severe sequelae)
Seizures, particularly status or recurrent seizures
Signs of severely raised intracranial pressure (a relative contraindication to lumbar puncture)
Hypothalamic-pituitary dysfunction (temperature dysregulation, diabetes insipidus, SIADH)
Acute flaccid paralysis accompanying encephalitis (West Nile, EV-A71/D68)
# Pitfalls
Do not wait for CSF PCR confirmation before starting aciclovir in suspected HSV encephalitis - early treatment improves outcome, and PCR can be falsely negative in the first ~72 h (start empirically, repeat PCR if needed).
A negative HSV CSF PCR reduces but does not exclude HSV encephalitis; interpret against pretest probability, timing, and prior antiviral use, and consider repeat testing or CSF antibody studies.
Worsening neurologic or psychiatric symptoms in the weeks after recovery from HSV encephalitis are often post-HSV anti-NMDA-receptor autoimmune encephalitis (needing immunotherapy), not aciclovir-resistant HSV - test for NMDAR antibodies.
Detecting NMDAR antibodies does not exclude HSV - they appear in up to 25% of HSV-encephalitis recoveries, so still test and treat for HSV when indicated.
Negative CSF viral culture does not exclude HSV or EBV encephalitis (culture is insensitive; >95% of HSV cases have negative CSF culture).
A positive EBV CSF PCR may be non-specific, reflecting reactivation in lymphocytes entering the CNS during an unrelated process - corroborate before attributing encephalitis to EBV.
Up to ~10% of PCR-proven HSV encephalitis cases have a normal MRI, and CT is normal in 20-35% - normal imaging does not exclude HSV.
Discontinue aciclovir once HSV encephalitis is excluded (with possible exception of severe VZV or EBV encephalitis).
Serum HSV serology alone has no diagnostic value because of high background seroprevalence.
# Deescalation
If CSF HSV PCR is negative and bacterial cultures are negative with a CSF profile inconsistent with bacterial infection, discontinue both acyclovir and antibacterial agents and pursue alternative diagnoses (autoimmune, arboviral, etc.).

# Framework
Do not withhold empiric acyclovir while awaiting CSF HSV PCR — the risk-benefit strongly favors early empiric treatment given HSV encephalitis mortality if delayed.
Discontinue empiric antibacterial coverage promptly once CSF findings make bacterial infection unlikely (e.g., lymphocytic pleocytosis, normal glucose, negative Gram stain/culture).
Stop acyclovir if CSF HSV PCR is negative and an alternative diagnosis is identified, rather than completing a full course by default.
