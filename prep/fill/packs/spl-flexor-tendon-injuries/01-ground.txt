Exam grounding notes for flexor tendon zones and repair, written for grounding, not for display. Paraphrased from Plastic Surgery 5E Volume 6 on flexor injury.

Five anatomical zones divide the flexor system. Zone I lies beyond the FDS insertion near the fingertip. Zone II runs from the A1 pulley to the FDS insertion and is nicknamed no mans land for its poor results. Zone III crosses the palm, Zone IV the carpal tunnel, and Zone V the forearm.

The digital sheath carries five annular pulleys labelled A1 to A5 and three cruciform pulleys C1 to C3. The A2 and A4 pulleys over the proximal and middle phalanges are biomechanically essential: cutting them lets the tendon bowstring away from bone and destroys the moment arm.

Tendons feed through two routes. Longitudinal vincula, the brevia and longa vessels, perfuse segments directly, while the surrounding synovial fluid nourishes by diffusion. Repairs must preserve both the vincula and the smooth sheath gliding surface.

Strength comes from multi-strand cores. Modern repairs use 4 strand or 6 strand core patterns such as the modified Kessler, cruciate, or Strickland designs in 3-0 or 4-0 braided or monofilament suture. More strands crossing the repair mean higher load to failure.

A continuous running epitendinous suture in 5-0 or 6-0 material tidies the repair edge. It smooths gliding through the pulleys and contributes a further 20 percent to 50 percent of tensile strength. Skipping it leaves a catching, weaker repair.

Motion starts early. Early active motion programmes, exemplified by the Manchester and Saint John protocols, begin within 3 to 5 days after repair. Controlled glide prevents the restrictive peritendinous adhesions that freeze an immobilised tendon.

Jersey finger is the classic closed avulsion: the flexor digitorum profundus tendon tears off the distal phalanx base when a grabbed jersey yanks the finger straight. The ring finger suffers this in 75 percent of cases because it protrudes furthest in grip.

Leddy and Pack sort Jersey injuries by retreat. Type I pulls back into the palm with disrupted vincula and needs surgery within days. Type II stalls at the PIP joint. Type III carries a bony fragment that jams at the A4 pulley and can wait slightly longer.
