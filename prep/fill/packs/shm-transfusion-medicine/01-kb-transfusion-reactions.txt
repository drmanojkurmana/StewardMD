# Transfusion Reactions
Transfusion Reactions, Hematology / Transfusion.
Aliases: hemolytic transfusion reaction; TRALI; TACO.
# Pathophysiology
Adverse reactions to blood components arise via immune or nonimmune mechanisms. Immune-mediated acute haemolysis occurs when recipient preformed antibodies (anti-A/anti-B IgM/IgG, or alloantibodies to Rh, Kell, Duffy) lyse transfused donor RBCs, with complement activation; ABO incompatibility classically results from clerical/identification error. Delayed haemolysis reflects an anamnestic alloantibody response (neoformed allogeneic IgG, C3b-mediated opsonisation and ADCC-mediated phagocytosis) detectable 1-2 weeks later. FNHTR is driven by proinflammatory cytokines accumulated in the component or recipient antibodies against donor leukocyte antigens. Allergic/anaphylactic reactions relate to plasma proteins and soluble mediators triggering histamine/serotonin release (IgA-deficient recipients may react to IgA). TRALI results from donor anti-HLA (class II, also class I) or anti-HNA antibodies binding recipient antigen, or cytokine/chemokine priming, causing neutrophil aggregation in pulmonary vasculature and release of inflammatory mediators producing noncardiogenic pulmonary oedema. TACO is hydrostatic circulatory overload from excessive volume/rate relative to cardiac/renal reserve. Transfusion-associated GVHD is mediated by engrafted donor T lymphocytes attacking host HLA antigens.
# Prognosis
Most adverse reactions are non-life-threatening, though serious reactions can present with mild symptoms. An average of ~37 transfusion-associated fatalities (possible-to-definite imputability) were reported yearly to the FDA between 2017-2021 among ~15 million transfused components, with leading causes of death being TACO (32%), TRALI (21%), haemolysis (21%) and microbial contamination (13%). Acute haemolysis is often self-limiting but can be fatal (DIC, acute renal failure, shock). Transfusion-associated GVHD is fatal in over 90% of cases and highly resistant to treatment.
# Severity classification
Warning-sign based recognition of transfusion adverse reactions: fever of at least 38 degrees C (rise of 1-2 degrees C within 4 h favours FNHTR or TRALI; rise over 2 degrees C favours bacterial infection/haemolysis); hypotension (systolic drop of at least 30 mmHg) suggesting haemolytic, anaphylactic or septic shock; dyspnoea (TRALI within 6 h, TACO within 12 h, severe allergy within 4 h); haemoglobinuria (intravascular haemolysis); rash (minor allergy if under two-thirds body, severe if over two-thirds, anaphylaxis if with dyspnoea/shock within 5 min); icterus (delayed haemolysis); rash/diarrhoea/fever 2 days-6 weeks (GVHD); gum bleeding/purpura 5-12 days (posttransfusion purpura). Reactions are also categorised as immune-mediated vs nonimmunologic, and by timing (acute vs delayed).
# Clinical pearls
Acute immune-mediated haemolytic reactions occur when recipient preformed antibodies lyse transfused donor RBCs, during or up to 24 h after transfusion; anti-A/anti-B antibodies cause most of the most severe (potentially fatal) reactions, though alloantibodies against Rh, Kell, and Duffy antigens can also cause severe haemolysis.
The most dramatic acute haemolytic reactions are usually caused by a failure in product or patient identification, erroneous blood grouping, or unidentified recipient anti-RBC alloimmunisation - i.e. a clerical/identification error.
Acute haemolytic reactions may present with hypotension, tachypnoea, tachycardia, fever (rise of 1-2 degrees C), chills, chest and back/flank pain, haemoglobinuria, and haemoglobinemia; in the most severe cases DIC, acute renal failure, shock, and death may occur.
RED FLAG: when an adverse reaction is suspected the transfusion must be STOPPED, the recipient's clinical status assessed, and supportive care initiated; a thorough clerical check is crucial to prevent a possible second misidentified transfusion.
Management of acute immune haemolytic reaction is mainly supportive: prompt interruption of transfusion, biological workup, vigorous isotonic saline hydration with diuretics to maintain urine output; may require forced alkaline diuresis, electrolyte correction, and pressor support, and in DIC with bleeding, platelet/plasma/cryoprecipitate or fibrinogen.
Diagnosis of transfusion-associated haemolysis relies on persistent/worsening anaemia, depleted haptoglobin, haemoglobinemia and haemoglobinuria, and elevated LDH and unconjugated bilirubin; the direct antiglobulin test (DAT/direct Coombs) is most often positive, as is the indirect antiglobulin test (IAT).
Delayed haemolytic reactions present with icterus and persisting or worsening anaemia and result from an anamnestic response in previously sensitised patients who had a negative alloantibody screen at transfusion (low antibody levels); the alloantibody becomes detectable 1-2 weeks after transfusion.
FNHTR (febrile non-haemolytic transfusion reaction) is the most frequent reaction to cellular components (up to 300 per 100,000), characterised by chills, rigors and a rise of at least 1 degree C, caused by proinflammatory cytokines in the component or recipient antibodies against donor antigens; it is a diagnosis of exclusion once infection and haemolysis are ruled out.
Leukocyte reduction (especially prestorage) can prevent FNHTR; premedication with antipyretics has generally proven ineffective and may mask relevant clinical symptoms.
Most allergic transfusion reactions are mild (rash, pruritus, urticaria, localised oedema) and relate to plasma proteins; rarely they are severe/anaphylactic with bronchospasm, respiratory distress, hypotension, nausea, vomiting and shock - anaphylaxis can occur after only a few millilitres of component.
TRALI is characterised by occurrence or worsening of hypoxia and NONcardiogenic pulmonary oedema with bilateral interstitial infiltrates on chest x-ray during or within 6 h of transfusion (delayed cases up to 72 h); it is among the most common causes of transfusion-related fatalities and treatment is supportive only.
TRALI usually results from donor plasma containing high-titre anti-HLA class II antibodies binding recipient cognate antigen (anti-HLA class I and anti-HNA antibodies may also be involved); a cytokine/chemokine-mediated mechanism without HLA interaction can also occur.
TACO (transfusion-associated circulatory overload) is common and underrecognised and is now the main cause of transfusion-related death since TRALI risk was mitigated; it presents with dyspnoea, hypoxia, bilateral and predominantly ALVEOLAR infiltrates on chest x-ray, frequent systolic hypertension, and elevated BNP (fever may also occur).
TRALI vs TACO distinction: TRALI gives noncardiogenic oedema with predominantly interstitial infiltrates within 6 h, whereas TACO is volume overload with predominantly alveolar infiltrates (within 12 h), systolic hypertension and elevated BNP; TACO risk factors include older age, renal failure, preexisting fluid overload, cardiac dysfunction, large transfusion volume and excessive transfusion rate.
Transfusion-related GVHD is extremely rare but fatal in over 90% of cases; mediated by engrafted donor T lymphocytes in a recipient unable to reject them, it presents 5-10 days post-transfusion with cytopenia, fever, characteristic skin rash, diarrhoea and liver function abnormalities, and is prevented by irradiation (minimum 25 Gy) of cellular components in at-risk patients.
Transfusion-transmitted bacterial infection remains a significant concern, notably with platelet concentrates stored at room temperature; some gram-negatives such as Yersinia grow at 4 degrees C and can contaminate RBC units - recipients may develop abrupt fever and chills deteriorating to septic shock, DIC and death, and after culture sampling broad-spectrum antibiotics should be started promptly.
Iron overload: each RBC concentrate contains 200-250 mg of iron; in frequently transfused recipients untreated accumulation impairs endocrine, hepatic and cardiac function (death may occur from cardiac failure or arrhythmia), assessed by serum ferritin, MRI and liver biopsy, and prevented/treated by monitoring and iron chelation.
Acute hypotensive transfusion reactions are an abrupt drop in systolic blood pressure of more than 30 mmHg early after starting transfusion that resolves once transfusion is stopped; they are more likely in patients taking ACE inhibitors (impaired bradykinin metabolism) and the same product should NOT be restarted.

# Infection mimics
Transfusion-transmitted bacterial contamination/sepsis presenting with abrupt fever, chills, septic shock and DIC can mimic an acute haemolytic reaction (fever over 2 degrees C is a warning sign favouring bacterial contamination/haemolysis)
Septic shock from a contaminated component can mimic anaphylactic or haemolytic shock
# Non infectious mimics
TRALI may be difficult to distinguish from other causes of hypoxia, particularly circulatory overload (TACO)
G6PD/enzymatic RBC defects and underlying haemolytic conditions exacerbated by transfusion can mimic immune-mediated haemolytic transfusion reaction
Hyperhaemolysis in sickle cell disease can mimic and be exacerbated by transfusion
# Additional investigations
Direct antiglobulin test (DAT / direct Coombs) detecting immunoglobulin and possibly complement (C3d) on recipient RBCs
Indirect antiglobulin test (IAT / indirect Coombs) detecting anti-RBC alloantibody in serum, with antibody elution to identify the culprit alloantibody
Plasma haptoglobin (depleted), LDH and unconjugated bilirubin (elevated), haemoglobinemia and haemoglobinuria
Repeat ABO determination and thorough clerical check; alloantibody screen and crossmatch
Bacterial culture of the component and recipient before starting broad-spectrum antibiotics when bacterial contamination suspected
Chest x-ray (bilateral interstitial infiltrates in TRALI; predominantly alveolar infiltrates in TACO) and brain natriuretic peptide (elevated in TACO)
Serum ferritin, MRI and liver biopsy to assess iron overload in frequently transfused patients
Test for IgA deficiency in patients with anaphylactic or repeated allergic reactions
# Red flags
STOP the transfusion immediately when any adverse reaction is suspected
Fever, flank/back pain and haemoglobinuria suggesting acute haemolytic (ABO-incompatible) reaction - perform an urgent clerical/identification check to prevent a second misidentified transfusion
Hypotension (systolic drop of at least 30 mmHg) - haemolytic, anaphylactic or septic shock
Dyspnoea within 6 h (TRALI) or within 12 h (TACO) of transfusion
Temperature rise over 2 degrees C - favours transfusion-transmitted bacterial infection or haemolysis
Rash over two-thirds of body within 5 minutes with dyspnoea and shock - anaphylaxis
Development of DIC, acute renal failure or shock during/after transfusion
# Pitfalls
Premedication with antipyretics for FNHTR is generally ineffective and may mask relevant clinical warning symptoms
FNHTR must not be diagnosed until infection and haemolysis are excluded
Failing to perform a clerical/identification check risks a second misidentified (incompatible) transfusion
TRALI and TACO are easily confused; both cause hypoxia and bilateral infiltrates - TACO is now the leading cause of transfusion death and is underrecognised
In acute hypotensive reactions the same blood product should not be restarted; consider switching ACE inhibitor for patients needing further transfusion
Delayed haemolytic reactions occur despite a negative pretransfusion alloantibody screen (low antibody levels) - icterus and worsening anaemia 1-2 weeks later
Iron overload is frequently underreported in chronically transfused patients
Further RBC transfusion in hyperhaemolytic reaction (sickle cell disease) typically exacerbates ongoing haemolysis
# Management
Stop the transfusion immediately at any suspected reaction, maintain venous access, and perform a rigorous clerical and identity check to prevent a second misidentified transfusion
For acute haemolytic reactions give vigorous isotonic saline to maintain urine output with diuretics as needed, support blood pressure with vasopressors, and correct electrolytes
Manage haemolysis-associated disseminated intravascular coagulation with platelets, plasma, cryoprecipitate or fibrinogen when there is bleeding
Treat febrile non-haemolytic reactions with antipyretics after excluding haemolysis, and allergic reactions with antihistamines, escalating to adrenaline and corticosteroids for anaphylaxis
When bacterial contamination is suspected, culture the component and recipient and start broad-spectrum antibiotics
Send diagnostic samples including direct and indirect antiglobulin tests, repeat ABO grouping and crossmatch, and report to the transfusion service
Prevent recurrence with appropriate component selection using leucoreduced units, washed or IgA-deficient components for IgA-deficient recipients, and premedication where indicated
