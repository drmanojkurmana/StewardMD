# Epilepsy with myoclonic absences
Epilepsy with myoclonic absences, Nervous system, Paediatric neurology.
Aliases: Myoclonic absence epilepsy; Tassinari syndrome.
# Pathophysiology
Epilepsy with myoclonic absences is a generalised epilepsy in which thalamocortical oscillatory circuits generate rhythmic 3 Hz spike-wave discharges. The distinctive rhythmic myoclonias arise as the motor cortex is entrained by these generalised discharges, producing a positive motor phenomenon with a superimposed tonic contraction, demonstrable on combined EEG-EMG polygraphy. Underlying genetic or, in some, structural and chromosomal abnormalities contribute to the network dysfunction and the associated cognitive impairment.
# Prognosis
Variable and often guarded; seizures may be difficult to control and cognitive impairment is common, although isolated cases without other seizure types can remit. Evolution to other epileptic encephalopathies occurs in some.
# Clinical pearls
A generalised epilepsy defined by absence seizures accompanied by rhythmic bilateral myoclonic jerks, predominantly of the shoulders and arms, with a tonic component.
Onset is around 7 years, spanning early childhood to pre-adolescence, with a male predominance.
Seizures are frequent, occurring many times a day, and can be triggered by hyperventilation.
EEG shows bilateral synchronous rhythmic 3 Hz spike-wave discharges time-locked to the myoclonias, confirmed by polygraphic EMG.
Consciousness is variably impaired during events.
Associated intellectual disability is common and other seizure types, including generalised tonic-clonic seizures, may occur.
A proportion have an identifiable cause such as a chromosomal abnormality or structural lesion.
Treatment response is often incomplete, and valproate combined with ethosuximide is a common regimen.
Additional differentials: Childhood and juvenile absence epilepsy; Doose syndrome (myoclonic-atonic epilepsy); Juvenile myoclonic epilepsy; Lennox-Gastaut syndrome; Eyelid myoclonia with absences (Jeavons); Progressive myoclonic epilepsy.
# Additional investigations
Video-EEG with polygraphic EMG to demonstrate the time-locked myoclonias
MRI brain to exclude a structural cause
Chromosomal microarray and genetic testing
Metabolic screen if progressive features emerge
Neuropsychological assessment
Hyperventilation-activated EEG
# Red flags
Cognitive decline or regression
Emergence of tonic or atonic seizures suggesting evolution to Lennox-Gastaut syndrome
Drug-resistant, very frequent seizures
Abnormal neurological examination or imaging
# Pitfalls
Mistaking the myoclonic absences for simple typical absences and under-treating.
Not performing polygraphic EEG-EMG to confirm the time-locked myoclonias.
Overlooking an underlying chromosomal or structural cause.
Using drugs that aggravate generalised seizures.
# Management
Treat with broad-spectrum antiseizure medication, using valproate often combined with ethosuximide, and add lamotrigine for incomplete control
Avoid seizure-aggravating agents such as carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine and phenytoin that can worsen generalised seizures
Manage any coexisting generalised tonic-clonic seizures within the same regimen
Provide neuropsychological assessment and educational support, as intellectual disability commonly accompanies the epilepsy
Advise on seizure precautions and note that hyperventilation can provoke attacks
Monitor for treatment response and for evolution into other epileptic encephalopathies, with regular paediatric neurology follow-up
