Penile cancer is overwhelmingly one histology with one viral driver. Squamous cell carcinoma accounts for over 95 percent of penile malignancies, and high-risk human papillomavirus genotypes 16 and 18 are implicated in 30 to 50 percent of cases. The uncircumcised penis with phimosis and chronic inflammation carries the highest risk, while neonatal circumcision is strongly protective.

Premalignancy is visible before invasion. Erythroplasia of Queyrat affects the glans and prepuce while Bowen disease affects the penile shaft, and both are carcinoma in situ, Tis, with intact basement membrane. Because they are visible and accessible, biopsy of any persistent red or scaly patch catches the disease before it invades, and topical or ablative therapy can cure it.

Small invasive cancers keep the organ. Organ-sparing surgery by wide local excision, glansectomy with skin graft, or Mohs micrographic surgery gives excellent control for low-risk T1 tumours, provided clear surgical margins of 3 to 5 mm are achieved. Margin-controlled excision preserves appearance and function, with surveillance catching the minority that recur.

Bigger tumours need partial amputation with a working stump. Partial penectomy with a spatulated neo-meatus is indicated for invasive T2 tumours where at least 2 to 3 cm of functional stump can be preserved for upright micturition. Length decides between organ-sparing and partial approaches, and the patient must accept the functional trade before surgery.

Nodes decide survival, not palpation. Inguinal lymph node dissection is mandatory for intermediate and high-risk primaries, T1G3 or T2 and above, because clinical palpation misses occult micrometastases in 20 to 25 percent. Removing the inguinal basins both stages the disease and cures micrometastatic patients, which observation cannot do.

Sentinel biopsy stages the groin with less harm. Dynamic sentinel node biopsy with 99mTc-nanocolloid plus patent blue or indocyanine green maps the first draining node accurately while cutting the 30 to 50 percent wound morbidity of full dissection. A positive sentinel node leads to full dissection, while a negative one allows surveillance of the groin.

Pelvic nodes follow positive groins. Bilateral pelvic lymphadenectomy is indicated when two or more inguinal nodes are positive on histology, or with extracapsular nodal extension staged pN2 or pN3. The pelvis is the next landing zone after the groin, and clearing it extends the curative operation upward in one sequence.

Bulky nodes get chemotherapy first. Neoadjuvant combination chemotherapy with paclitaxel, ifosfamide, and cisplatin, the TIP regimen, is recommended for fixed, ulcerated, or bulky inguinal nodal metastases before consolidation surgery. Shrinking unresectable disease converts palliation into potential cure, with surgery removing whatever viable tumour remains.
