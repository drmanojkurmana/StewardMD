# cement dermatitis
cement dermatitis, occupational medicine.
Aliases: bricklayer's dermatitis; cement eczema.
# Pathophysiology
Cement injures skin by several mechanisms. Its high alkalinity (calcium hydroxide and related species when hydrated) saponifies lipids and denatures keratin, producing irritant dermatitis and, with prolonged contact, alkaline caustic burns; the abrasive mineral particles and desiccating properties add mechanical and drying injury that further disrupts the barrier. Superimposed on this is allergic contact dermatitis: hexavalent chromium and cobalt present as soluble contaminants penetrate the compromised epidermis, bind proteins to form haptens and elicit a delayed (type IV) T-cell-mediated hypersensitivity, giving a chronic spongiotic eczema. Barrier damage from the irritant effects enhances allergen penetration and perpetuates sensitisation, so the two processes reinforce each other. Reducing hexavalent to trivalent chromium (for example with added ferrous sulfate) lowers the allergenic load and the risk of new sensitisation.
# Prognosis
Irritant cement dermatitis often improves with barrier protection and good skin care, but allergic (chromate) cement dermatitis is frequently chronic and may persist even after leaving the trade because of established sensitisation and ubiquitous exposure. Alkaline burns can cause significant scarring.
# Clinical pearls
Cement causes two overlapping problems: an irritant contact dermatitis from its strong alkalinity, abrasive particles and hygroscopic drying effect, and an allergic contact dermatitis, chiefly to hexavalent chromium and cobalt.
Wet cement is highly alkaline and can produce not only eczema but full-thickness alkaline (caustic) burns, classically on the knees, feet and lower legs of kneeling concrete workers.
Chromate is the dominant allergen in cement dermatitis; adding ferrous sulfate to reduce hexavalent to trivalent chromium has markedly lowered the incidence where mandated.
The eruption typically affects the hands, wrists and forearms, and any skin in prolonged contact with wet concrete through clothing or boots.
Allergic cement dermatitis tends to be chronic and persistent, often continuing despite avoidance because of the ubiquity of chromate and the entrenched sensitisation.
A history of an itchy hand eczema that flares at work and settles on holiday, in a construction or masonry worker, is highly suggestive.
