# Rheumatoid arthritis
Rheumatoid arthritis, Rheumatology.
# Red flags
Septic arthritis can complicate a known RA joint
# Investigations
RF, anti-CCP, ESR/CRP

# Pathophysiology
RA is a chronic, systemic, symmetric erosive polyarthritis driven by an interaction of genetic predisposition (e.g., the HLA shared epitope) and environmental triggers such as cigarette smoking. Antigen-presenting cells activate synovial CD4+ T cells (via TCR-MHC class II and CD28-CD80/86 co-stimulation), which differentiate into TH1 and TH17 subsets. These activate B cells that mature into plasma cells producing autoantibodies (RF and anti-CCP/ACPA); immune complexes form within the joint and activate complement, amplifying inflammation. T effector cells stimulate synovial macrophages and fibroblasts to release pro-inflammatory cytokines, with TNF-alpha playing a central role by upregulating endothelial adhesion molecules and driving IL-1, IL-6 and GM-CSF production. TNF-alpha also disrupts the bone balance: it raises DKK-1 (inhibiting Wnt-driven osteoblastic bone formation) and promotes osteoclastogenesis through M-CSF and RANKL signalling, with osteoprotegerin acting as a decoy that limits bone loss. The net result is pannus formation, cartilage and bone erosion, and generalised inflammatory bone loss.
# Prognosis
Most patients show persistent, waxing-and-waning progressive disease; about 10% (particularly seronegative) remit spontaneously within 6 months, while a minority follow either a relapsing explosive course or an aggressively erosive course (now less common with modern therapy). Early disability is driven mainly by joint inflammation (potentially reversible) and later by cumulative irreversible joint damage. Overall mortality is roughly twice that of the general population, with excess deaths from respiratory illness, cardiovascular disease and infection; median life expectancy is shortened by about 7 years in men and 3 years in women. Worse survival is associated with systemic extraarticular involvement, low functional capacity, low socioeconomic status, low education and chronic prednisone use. Cardiovascular disease is the most common cause of death. Seropositivity for RF and especially anti-CCP predicts worse outcomes.
# Severity classification
Diagnosis is supported by the 2010 ACR-EULAR classification criteria (for patients with at least one joint of definite clinical synovitis not better explained by another disease), scored 0-10 across four domains - joint involvement (0-5), serology (RF/ACPA, 0-3), acute-phase reactants (CRP/ESR, 0-1), and symptom duration (0-1); a score of 6 or more fulfils requirements for definite RA. Disease activity (the main driver of treatment decisions) is tracked with composite indices: ACR 20/50/70 improvement criteria (mainly for trials) and continuous measures including DAS, SDAI, CDAI and RAPID3 used in practice. Functional disability is measured with the Health Assessment Questionnaire (HAQ).
# Clinical pearls
Early-morning joint stiffness lasting more than 1 hour that improves with activity is characteristic; the earliest joints involved are typically the small joints of the hands and feet.
Wrists, MCP and PIP joints are most often affected; isolated DIP involvement usually reflects coexistent osteoarthritis rather than RA itself.
Flexor tendon tenosynovitis is a frequent hallmark, producing reduced range of motion, weakened grip and trigger fingers, and can lead to tendon rupture (most commonly flexor pollicis longus).
Anti-CCP (ACPA) and RF are useful both diagnostically and as prognostic biomarkers; anti-CCP carries the most value for predicting worse outcomes.
Anti-CCP has sensitivity similar to RF but much higher specificity (approaching 95%), so a positive anti-CCP in early inflammatory arthritis helps distinguish RA from other arthritides; testing both RF and anti-CCP adds incremental yield because some patients are positive for only one.
About three-quarters of RA patients are seropositive (RF and/or ACPA) and one-quarter are seronegative; seronegative RA is a clinical diagnosis.
A fever above 38.3C (101F) at any point in the course should raise suspicion of systemic vasculitis or superimposed infection rather than being attributed to RA activity alone.
Approximately 30% of RA patients are ANA-positive and some have p-ANCA, but anti-MPO and anti-PR3 are not expected and their presence points to an ANCA-associated vasculitis instead.
Synovial fluid in RA is inflammatory (typically 5,000-50,000 WBC/uL, neutrophil-predominant) versus under 2,000 WBC/uL in osteoarthritis; aspiration is most useful to confirm inflammation and to exclude infection or crystal arthritis.
# Additional differentials
Primary Sjogren's syndrome - polyarthralgia and mild synovitis can mimic RA, and ~50% are RF-positive; distinguished by predominant sicca symptoms, ANA positivity and anti-Ro/SSA in about two-thirds.
Spondyloarthropathies (psoriatic arthritis, enteropathy-associated arthritis) - distinguished by sacroiliitis, enthesopathy, and presence of psoriasis or inflammatory bowel disease.
Polymyalgia rheumatica in elderly patients - can resemble seronegative RA; PMR centres on neck/shoulder and hip girdle, whereas RA predominantly affects wrists/hands and ankles/feet, especially at onset.
Remitting seronegative symmetrical synovitis with pitting edema (RS3PE) - features prominent distal-limb pitting edema (unusual in RA) and responds promptly to low-dose prednisone.
Paraneoplastic syndromes - may be confused with early RA.
# Infection mimics
Virus-related polyarthritis syndromes - can mimic RA and cause transient RF production, but usually resolve within 2-3 weeks.
Hepatitis C-related arthropathy - often involves small joints of the hands and is RF-positive in about half of cases, but generally anti-CCP negative.
Subacute bacterial endocarditis and chronic hepatitis B/C - chronic infections that can produce a positive RF and confound interpretation.
# Non infectious mimics
Chronic tophaceous gout - can mimic severe RA, and tophi may be mistaken for rheumatoid nodules.
Pseudogout (CPPD crystal arthritis) - inflammatory arthritis excluded via synovial fluid crystal analysis.
Osteoarthritis - non-inflammatory; DIP involvement and synovial fluid under 2,000 WBC/uL favour OA over RA.
# Additional investigations
Anti-CCP (ACPA) antibodies - high specificity (~95%) for RA; useful diagnostically and prognostically.
Synovial fluid aspiration with cell count and crystal/microbiology analysis - confirms inflammatory arthritis and excludes infection or crystal disease.
MRI - greatest sensitivity for synovitis, effusions and early bone/bone-marrow changes; bone marrow edema predicts subsequent erosions.
Musculoskeletal ultrasound with power Doppler - detects synovitis, increased joint vascularity and erosions, often more than plain films; portable and radiation-free.
ANA and ANCA - may be positive in RA (~30% ANA, occasional p-ANCA), but anti-MPO/anti-PR3 should be negative.
High-resolution chest CT - to detect and characterise interstitial lung disease (UIP vs NSIP patterns) when respiratory symptoms arise.
# Red flags
Fever above 38.3C (101F) during the course should prompt evaluation for systemic vasculitis or infection rather than being ascribed to RA activity.
RA patients have the highest incidence of septic (most often S. aureus) arthritis due to chronically inflamed joints, glucocorticoid therapy and breakdown of nodules/vasculitic ulcers; TNF inhibitors add risk of mycobacterial and other infections.
Atlantoaxial (C1-C2) subluxation can cause compressive myelopathy; neurologic signs may evolve slowly with progressive instability (now seen in under 10% of patients).
New-onset numbness, tingling or focal weakness (mononeuritis multiplex) suggests systemic rheumatoid vasculitis.
Two- to fourfold increased risk of lymphoma (most often diffuse large B-cell), greatest with high disease activity or Felty's syndrome.
# Pitfalls
The 2010 ACR-EULAR criteria are classification (not diagnostic) criteria intended to identify early patients likely to progress; a positive RF or anti-CCP alone does not establish the diagnosis without supporting clinical features.
A negative RF does not exclude RA - IgM RF is found in only ~75% of patients, and RF also occurs in other connective tissue diseases, chronic infections and 1-5% of healthy people.
Accelerated growth of subcutaneous nodules can occur in up to 10% of patients on long-term methotrexate and should not automatically be read as worsening disease.
Rheumatoid nodules and chronic tophaceous gout tophi can be confused with one another.
Periarticular osteopenia, the classic earliest x-ray finding, is difficult to appreciate on plain (especially digitised) films, so early radiographs may appear normal.
# Rheumatoid arthritis: treatment
Symmetrical small-joint polyarthritis with early-morning stiffness >30 min; RF/anti-CCP, raised ESR/CRP, erosions, Rheumatology referral.
Early DMARD (methotrexate) + steroid bridge + symptom relief
# Steps
Early referral to rheumatology — 'treat to target' (remission/low disease activity).
Start a DMARD early (methotrexate first-line) ± short-term steroid bridge.
Escalate to combination DMARDs or a biologic/targeted synthetic DMARD if inadequate response.
NSAID for symptom relief; monitor DMARD toxicity (FBC, LFTs).
Manage cardiovascular risk, bone health and vaccination.
