# Infectious Arthritis
Infectious Arthritis, Musculoskeletal.
Aliases: septic arthritis; bacterial arthritis; pyogenic arthritis; joint infection.
# Pathophysiology
Bacteria reach the joint hematogenously, from a contiguous bone or soft-tissue focus, or by direct inoculation during surgery, injection, bite, or trauma. In hematogenous infection, organisms escape from synovial capillaries, which lack a limiting basement membrane, and within hours provoke neutrophilic infiltration of the synovium. Neutrophils and bacteria enter the joint space and bacteria adhere to articular cartilage. Cartilage degradation begins within about 48 hours owing to increased intraarticular pressure, release of proteases and cytokines from chondrocytes and synovial macrophages, and invasion of cartilage by bacteria and inflammatory cells. Synovial proliferation forms a pannus over the cartilage, and thrombosis of inflamed synovial vessels develops; abscesses may extend into synovium, cartilage, and subchondral bone. S. aureus surface adhesins promote cartilage adherence and endotoxins drive chondrocyte-mediated cartilage breakdown. Gonococcal arthritis-dermatitis and viral arthritides are believed to be largely immune-mediated, reflecting reaction to circulating organisms and immune-complex deposition rather than direct joint invasion.
# Prognosis
Prompt systemic antibiotics with timely joint drainage can prevent cartilage destruction, postinfectious degenerative arthritis, joint instability, and deformity, so outcome depends heavily on early recognition and adequate decompression. Radiographic joint-space narrowing and bony erosions indicate advanced disease and a poorer outcome. Gonococcal and Lyme arthritis generally respond well to therapy, and most reactive arthritis resolves within about 6 months, though recurrent disease is more common after chlamydial urethritis. Untreated Lyme arthritis leaves about 10% with chronic erosive synovitis, and certain viral arthropathies (e.g., parvovirus B19) occasionally become chronic.
# Severity classification
No single formal grading system; synovial fluid cell count guides likelihood and severity. Normal synovial fluid contains fewer than 180 cells/uL (predominantly mononuclear). Acute bacterial infection averages about 100,000 cells/uL (range roughly 25,000-250,000/uL) with over 90% neutrophils. Crystal-induced, rheumatoid, and other noninfectious inflammatory arthritides usually show under 30,000-50,000 cells/uL. Mycobacterial and fungal infections typically show 10,000-30,000 cells/uL with 50-70% neutrophils. Gonococcal DGI synovial fluid usually contains only 10,000-20,000 leukocytes/uL, whereas true gonococcal septic arthritis contains over 50,000/uL.
# Clinical pearls
Staphylococcus aureus, streptococci, and Neisseria gonorrhoeae are the most common causes of infectious arthritis, though mycobacteria, spirochetes, fungi, and viruses can also seed joints.
Acute bacterial infection can rapidly destroy articular cartilage, so every inflamed joint warrants prompt evaluation; cartilage degradation can begin within about 48 hours.
Roughly 90% of acute septic arthritis presents in a single joint, most often the knee, then the hip, and less often shoulder, wrist, or elbow.
In IV drug users, infections of the spine, sacroiliac joints, and sternoclavicular joints are more common than those of the appendicular skeleton.
Acute bacterial infection typically causes mono- or oligoarthritis; subacute/chronic monoarthritis suggests mycobacterial or fungal disease, and acute polyarthritis suggests viral, immunologic (endocarditis, rheumatic fever, DGI), or underlying rheumatoid disease.
Synovial fluid Gram stain is positive in roughly 30-50% of cases overall; organisms are seen on smear in nearly three-quarters of S. aureus and streptococcal infections but only 30-50% of gram-negative and other bacterial infections.
Synovial fluid cultures are positive in over 90% of nongonococcal bacterial arthritis cases, but in gonococcal arthritis synovial cultures are frequently negative and blood cultures positive in fewer than 45%.
Disseminated gonococcal infection most commonly presents as an arthritis-dermatitis syndrome with fever, chills, hemorrhagic pustular rash, tenosynovitis, and migratory arthritis; women are 2-3 times more likely than men to develop DGI, with peak risk during menses and pregnancy.
Lyme disease causes arthritis in up to 60% of untreated persons; serologic IgG to B. burgdorferi is positive in over 90% of Lyme arthritis, and NAAT detects Borrelia DNA in synovial fluid in about 85%.
Tuberculous arthritis is usually a chronic granulomatous monoarthritis of large weight-bearing joints (hips, knees, ankles); synovial fluid acid-fast staining is positive in under one-third of cases but synovial tissue culture is positive in about 90%.
Parvovirus B19 causes polyarthritis or polyarthralgia in about 60% of infected women and 10% of children; rubella, mumps, hepatitis B, and arboviruses (Zika, chikungunya, Ross River) are other notable viral causes.
Prosthetic joint infection complicates roughly 0.5-2% of total joint replacements and is more common after knee than hip arthroplasty; indolent infection with coagulase-negative staphylococci, Cutibacterium, enterococci, or diphtheroids may persist for months to years without constitutional symptoms.
# Additional differentials
Crystal-induced arthritis (gout, pseudogout) - can clinically mimic and may coexist with septic arthritis
Cellulitis
Bursitis
Acute osteomyelitis
Hemarthrosis or fracture
Monoarticular flare of rheumatoid arthritis
Reactive/postinfectious arthritis
Acute rheumatic fever
Osteoarthritis
# Infection mimics
Gonococcal disseminated infection presenting as arthritis-dermatitis (versus true septic monoarthritis)
Meningococcemia with dermatitis-arthritis or purulent monoarthritis
Poncet's disease (reactive polyarthritis of disseminated tuberculosis, with no organisms in the joint)
