# Intestinal tuberculosis and differentiation from Crohn disease
# Source: StewardMD gastroenterology knowledge base / Sleisenger 11e Ch. 110

## Definition, epidemiology and localization
Intestinal tuberculosis (ITB) affects the ileocecal region in > 70% of cases, presenting with mucosal ulcerations, strictures, or inflammatory pseudotumours resulting from Mycobacterium tuberculosis ingestion or hematogenous dissemination.

## Endoscopic and histological differentiation
Endoscopic differentiation reveals that intestinal TB characteristically features transverse circular ulcers oriented perpendicular to the long bowel axis with patulous ileocecal valve, whereas Crohn disease features longitudinal linear ulcers and cobblestoning.
Histopathologic hallmarks distinguishing intestinal TB from Crohn disease are large confluent (> 400 micrometres) caseating granulomas, multiple granulomas per biopsy (> 5), and granulomas located in the lamina propria or submucosa, compared to small Crohn granulomas (< 200 um).

## Microbiological confirmation and radiology
Acid-fast bacilli (AFB) on Ziehl-Neelsen staining or positive GeneXpert MTB/RIF assay on mucosal biopsies provides rapid microbiological confirmation of intestinal tuberculosis and detects resistance to rifampicin.
CT enterography in intestinal TB typically demonstrates necrotic mesenteric lymph nodes with peripheral rim enhancement and central hypoattenuation, a radiological finding rarely seen in Crohn disease.

## Therapeutic trial and treatment regimen
A therapeutic trial of antitubercular therapy (ATT) is indicated in diagnostic ambiguity; mucosal healing on repeat colonoscopy at 8 to 12 weeks confirms the diagnosis of intestinal tuberculosis and excludes Crohn disease.
Standard curative medical management of intestinal tuberculosis requires a 6-month regimen of antitubercular therapy comprising 2 months of HRZE (isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide, ethambutol) followed by 4 months of HR (isoniazid, rifampicin).
Misdiagnosing intestinal tuberculosis as Crohn disease and inadvertently administering anti-TNF or immunosuppressive therapy can provoke catastrophic disseminated tuberculosis, bowel perforation, and septic death.

## Additional clinical details from Sleisenger
Gastrointestinal tuberculosis is caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex.
Ascites with high protein and elevated adenosine deaminase indicates tuberculous peritonitis.
In contrast, Crohn disease features aphthous ulcers, eccentric bowel wall thickening, comb sign of engorged vasa recta, and fibrostenotic strictures.
