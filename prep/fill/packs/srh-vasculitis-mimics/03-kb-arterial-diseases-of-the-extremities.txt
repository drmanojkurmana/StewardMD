# Arterial Diseases of the Extremities
Arterial Diseases of the Extremities, Cardiovascular (peripheral vascular).
Aliases: Peripheral artery disease; PAD; Peripheral arterial disease; Intermittent claudication; Chronic limb-threatening ischemia; Acute limb ischemia.
# Pathophysiology
In atherosclerotic PAD, segmental plaques with calcium deposition, medial thinning, patchy loss of muscle and elastic fibers, fragmentation of the internal elastic lamina, and platelet-fibrin thrombi narrow large and medium vessels, preferentially at branch points where turbulence, altered shear stress, and intimal injury occur. Principal sites are the abdominal aorta/iliac arteries (about 30% of symptomatic patients), femoral/popliteal arteries (80-90%), and tibial/peroneal vessels (40-50%); distal disease predominates in the elderly and in diabetics. Hemodynamically significant stenoses lower distal systolic pressure (reflected in a reduced ABI). During exercise, vasodilation distal to a stenosis drops perfusion pressure below interstitial tissue pressure generated by working muscle, producing claudication. Buerger disease shows early neutrophilic infiltration of vessel walls with a preserved internal elastic lamina and a cellular inflammatory luminal thrombus, evolving to mononuclear/giant-cell infiltration, then perivascular fibrosis, organized thrombus, and recanalization. Raynaud phenomenon reflects digital arterial vasospasm; inherited erythromelalgia involves gain-of-function SCN9A (Nav1.7 sodium channel) mutations in sensory/sympathetic nerves.
# Prognosis
PAD natural history is driven largely by coexisting coronary and cerebrovascular disease: roughly one-third to one-half of symptomatic patients have clinical/ECG evidence of CAD and over half have significant disease on coronary angiography. PAD carries a 15-25% 5-year mortality and a two- to fourfold increased risk of cardiovascular death, with the highest mortality in those with most severe disease. Over 5 years the ABI worsens in nearly 40% and symptoms progress in about 20-25%; roughly 11% of symptomatic patients ultimately develop chronic limb-threatening ischemia, and without revascularization about 25% of those with chronic limb-threatening ischemia undergo amputation within a year. Prognosis is worse in continued smokers and diabetics. In thromboangiitis obliterans the outlook is worse in continued smokers and remains discouraging even after cessation. Primary Raynaud disease is generally mild, with fewer than 1% losing part of a digit, spontaneous improvement in about 15%, and progression in about 30%; primary acrocyanosis and benign livedo reticularis have favorable outcomes without ulceration or gangrene.
# Severity classification
PAD severity is graded hemodynamically by ABI: normal 1.00-1.40, borderline 0.91-0.99, abnormal/diagnostic at 0.90 or below, and noncompressible above 1.40 (toe-brachial index 0.70 or below abnormal). Clinically the spectrum runs from asymptomatic disease, to intermittent claudication, to chronic limb-threatening ischemia (rest pain, ulceration, gangrene). Acute limb ischemia is assessed for limb viability by presence of sensation, motor function, and Doppler-detectable arterial and venous flow; loss of all three defines a nonviable limb.
# Clinical pearls
Peripheral artery disease (PAD) means stenosis or occlusion of the aorta or limb arteries; atherosclerosis is the dominant cause in patients over 40, while thrombosis, embolism, vasculitis, fibromuscular dysplasia, entrapment, cystic adventitial disease, and trauma account for the remainder.
Risk factors mirror those for coronary and cerebral atherosclerosis: cigarette smoking, diabetes mellitus, hypercholesterolemia, elevated lipoprotein(a), hypertension, and renal insufficiency; prevalence is similar between sexes but greater in Black than non-Hispanic white persons and in lower socioeconomic groups.
More than half of PAD patients are asymptomatic; the classic symptom is intermittent claudication (pain, ache, cramp, numbness, or fatigue in muscle that appears with exertion and resolves with rest), and the symptomatic muscle group lies distal to the obstructing lesion (e.g., buttock/hip/thigh with aortoiliac disease, calf with femoral-popliteal disease).
Chronic limb-threatening ischemia produces rest pain or a cold/numb foot that worsens at night when legs are horizontal and improves with dependency; signs include hair loss, thickened nails, shiny atrophic skin, coolness, pallor or cyanosis, and ultimately ulcers or gangrene.
Ankle-brachial index (ABI) is the key noninvasive test: normal 1.00-1.40, borderline 0.91-0.99, and abnormal/diagnostic of PAD at 0.90 or below; values above 1.40 indicate noncompressible, calcified vessels (as in diabetes or chronic kidney disease).
When the ABI is unreliable because of medial calcification, a toe-brachial index of 0.70 or below is considered abnormal and supports the diagnosis.
A drop in ABI immediately after treadmill exercise helps confirm PAD when resting findings are equivocal; MRA, CTA, and catheter angiography are reserved for revascularization planning, not routine diagnosis.
Fibromuscular dysplasia is a non-inflammatory hyperplastic arteriopathy of medium and small (sometimes large) arteries, predominant in women, classically renal and carotid/vertebral, with a multifocal 'string of beads' or focal pattern, and can cause stenosis, dissection, aneurysm, or thrombosis.
Thromboangiitis obliterans (Buerger disease) is an inflammatory occlusive disorder of distal small and medium arteries and veins, strongly linked to tobacco, typically in men under 40, presenting with a triad of distal claudication, Raynaud phenomenon, and migratory superficial thrombophlebitis; brachial and popliteal pulses are normal while distal pulses are reduced or absent.
Acute limb ischemia is sudden cessation of limb blood flow most often from cardiac embolism (atrial fibrillation, MI, ventricular aneurysm, endocarditis, prosthetic valves, atrial myxoma), in-situ thrombosis, dissection, or trauma; emboli lodge at bifurcations, most commonly the femoral artery in the leg.
Atheroembolism (cholesterol/fibrin/platelet debris from proximal plaque or aneurysm) classically spares large vessels, so distal pulses remain palpable; it causes the painful 'blue toe' syndrome, livedo reticularis, and may show cholesterol crystals on skin or muscle biopsy.
Raynaud phenomenon is episodic digital ischemia with sequential pallor (vasospasm), cyanosis (deoxygenated blood in dilated capillaries), and rubor (reactive hyperemia) after cold or emotional stress; over half of cases are primary/idiopathic, with women affected roughly five times more often and onset typically between ages 20 and 40.
