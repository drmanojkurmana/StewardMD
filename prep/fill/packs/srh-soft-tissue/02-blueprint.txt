Regional and soft tissue rheumatism: rotator cuff tendinopathy, epicondylitis, De Quervain tenosynovitis, trochanteric pain, frozen shoulder, pes anserine pain, plantar fasciitis.

Rotator cuff tendinopathy most commonly involves the supraspinatus tendon at its critical hypovascular zone about 1 cm proximal to the greater tuberosity insertion. Painful arc between 60 and 120 degrees of abduction with a painful drop arm sign indicates a large tear. Neer impingement test (forced forward flexion with scapular stabilization) and Hawkins-Kennedy test (shoulder flexed 90 degrees with forced internal rotation) both elicit subacromial pain. Ultrasound or MRI confirms partial versus full thickness tears.

Lateral epicondylitis, called tennis elbow, involves microtrauma and angiofibroblastic tendinosis predominantly at the origin of the extensor carpi radialis brevis (ECRB) tendon on the lateral epicondyle. Pain is elicited by resisted wrist extension with elbow extended and by resisted third-digit extension, known as the Maudsley test. Medial epicondylitis, called golfer's elbow, targets the common flexor tendon and pronator teres origin on the medial epicondyle, provoked by resisted wrist flexion and forearm pronation with the elbow held extended. Both are tendinopathies, not inflammatory, so NSAIDs give only short symptom relief while eccentric loading and activity change drive recovery; glucocorticoid injection with triamcinolone plus lidocaine rescues stubborn cases.

De Quervain tenosynovitis involves stenosing tenosynovitis of the first dorsal extensor compartment at the radial styloid, enclosing the abductor pollicis longus (APL) and extensor pollicis brevis (EPB) tendons. The pathognomonic Eichhoff test, often called the Finkelstein test, reproduces radial wrist pain when the thumb is clasped in the fist with ulnar deviation of the wrist. Thumb spica splinting with a single triamcinolone injection into the sheath settles most cases; release of the first compartment is reserved for refractory disease.

Trochanteric pain syndrome, also called greater trochanteric pain syndrome or GTPS, is primarily caused by gluteus medius and minimus tendinopathy or tear rather than isolated trochanteric bursitis. Lateral hip pain on lying on the affected side with tenderness over the greater trochanter defines it, and pain provoked by single-leg standing for at least 30 seconds is the bedside confirmation. MRI shows peritrochanteric edema with tendon splits. Load management with gluteal strengthening beats repeated bursal steroid shots.

Adhesive capsulitis, called frozen shoulder, progresses through freezing (painful, months 0 to 9), frozen (stiff, months 9 to 15), and thawing (recovery, months 15 to 24) stages, characterized pathologically by dense glenohumeral capsular contracture with synovial fibrosis. The hallmark is loss of both active and passive external rotation with greater than 50 percent restriction compared with the other side. Diabetes and thyroid disease predispose. Intra-articular glucocorticoid injection shortens the painful phase while hydrodilatation and graded physiotherapy restore motion.

Pes anserine bursitis and tendinopathy causes focal tenderness about 5 cm distal to the medial tibial joint line at the conjoined insertion of the sartorius, gracilis, and semitendinosus (SGS) tendons, remembered as say grace before tea. It is common in elderly obese females with knee osteoarthritis and valgus knees. Weight reduction with quadriceps work and a short NSAID course settles most cases.

Plantar fasciitis presents with intense stabbing heel pain upon the first morning weight-bearing steps after rest, easing with activity then aching again by evening, localized to the medial calcaneal tuberosity insertion of the fascia. The windlass test exacerbates pain on passive dorsiflexion of the first metatarsophalangeal joint with the ankle neutral. Calf stretching, silicone heel cups, night splints, and weight control form the base; a single ultrasound-guided steroid shot helps resistant heels while repeated shots risk rupture.
