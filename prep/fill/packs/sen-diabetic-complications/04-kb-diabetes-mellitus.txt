# Diabetes Mellitus
Diabetes Mellitus, Endocrine / Metabolic.
Aliases: type 1 diabetes; type 2 diabetes; DM.
# Pathophysiology
Hyperglycemia results from reduced insulin secretion, decreased peripheral glucose utilization, and/or increased hepatic glucose production, with the relative contribution depending on the DM type. Insulin is produced by pancreatic beta cells (from preproinsulin -> proinsulin -> insulin + C-peptide); glucose is the key secretagogue: it enters the beta cell via GLUT transporters, glucokinase phosphorylation is rate-limiting, glycolysis generates ATP that closes the ATP-sensitive K+ channel (target of sulfonylureas/meglitinides), causing depolarization, calcium influx, and insulin release; incretins (GLP-1, GIP) amplify glucose-stimulated secretion only when glucose is above fasting. Type 1 DM arises from interacting genetic (HLA region accounts for ~50% of risk; DR3-DQ2 and DR4-DQ8 predisposing), environmental, and immunologic factors leading to CD8+ T cell-mediated beta cell destruction and absolute insulin deficiency; alpha-cell dysfunction adds fasting/postprandial hyperglucagonemia with impaired glucagon response to hypoglycemia. Type 2 DM combines impaired insulin secretion, insulin resistance (post-receptor defects in skeletal muscle, liver, fat; lipid intermediate accumulation; low-grade metabolic inflammation), excessive hepatic glucose production (failure to suppress gluconeogenesis), abnormal fat metabolism (increased free fatty acids, altered adipokines, reduced adiponectin), giving the characteristic dyslipidemia (high triglycerides, low HDL, small dense LDL); beta cell function is reduced ~50% at onset and declines progressively. Complications stem from chronic hyperglycemia driving advanced glycosylation end products (AGEs), increased mitochondrial reactive oxygen species, epigenetic changes ("metabolic memory"), and growth factors such as VEGF-A in proliferative retinopathy.
# Prognosis
DM is a major cause of mortality (8th leading cause of death in the US, 2021; likely underreported) and was responsible for ~6.7 million deaths worldwide in 2021 (12.2% of global all-cause mortality in adults 20-79); about one-third of diabetes-related deaths occur before age 60. Cardiovascular disease is the main cause of morbidity and mortality, with ASCVD events and mortality 2-4 times greater in type 2 DM. In the US, DM is the leading cause of end-stage renal disease, nontraumatic lower-extremity amputation, and adult blindness; ~20-40% of patients develop diabetic nephropathy. Intensive glycemic control has durable benefit ("metabolic memory"/legacy effect lasting >=10 years): DCCT/EDIC showed reductions in retinopathy (47%), albuminuria (39%), clinical nephropathy (54%), neuropathy (60%), later a 57% reduction in cardiovascular events and 33% reduction in mortality; UKPDS showed each 1% HbA1c reduction lowered microvascular complications by 35%, and blood pressure control reduced complications even more than glycemic control. Diagnosis at younger age worsens prognosis (~3-4 years of reduced life expectancy per decade of earlier diagnosis).
# Severity classification
Glucose homeostasis is a spectrum: normal -> impaired (IFG/IGT/HbA1c 5.7-6.4%, i.e., prediabetes) -> diabetes mellitus. DKA severity by ADA: mild (pH 7.25-7.30, bicarbonate 15-18 meq/L, normal mental status), moderate (pH 7.0-7.25, bicarbonate 10-15 meq/L, mildly reduced mental status), severe (pH <7.0). Diabetic retinopathy: nonproliferative (microaneurysms, blot hemorrhages, cotton-wool spots) vs proliferative (neovascularization) +/- clinically significant macular edema. Albuminuria: moderately increased UACR 30-299 mg/g vs severely increased >300 mg/g creatinine. Neuropathy categories: distal symmetric polyneuropathy, autonomic neuropathy, mononeuropathy, and radiculopathy/polyradiculopathy.
# Clinical pearls
DM is a group of metabolic disorders sharing the phenotype of hyperglycemia; it is defined as the level of glycemia at which a diabetes-specific complication (e.g., retinopathy) occurs, not as a deviation from a population mean.
Diagnostic criteria (any one, confirmed by repeat testing on a different day unless unequivocal hyperglycemia/acute metabolic decompensation): HbA1c >=6.5% (>=48 mmol/mol); fasting plasma glucose (FPG) >=7.0 mmol/L (126 mg/dL, fasting = no calories >=8 h); 2-h plasma glucose >=11.1 mmol/L (200 mg/dL) on a 75-g OGTT; or a random plasma glucose >=11.1 mmol/L (200 mg/dL) PLUS classic symptoms (polyuria, polydipsia, weight loss).
Prediabetes (each identifies different individuals via different biologic mechanisms): HbA1c 5.7-6.4%; impaired fasting glucose (IFG) FPG 5.6-6.9 mmol/L (100-125 mg/dL) per ADA; impaired glucose tolerance (IGT) 2-h glucose 7.8-11.0 mmol/L (140-199 mg/dL). WHO defines IFG as FPG >6.1 mmol/L (110 mg/dL). All three confer increased risk of type 2 DM and cardiovascular disease.
Type 1 DM = autoimmune beta cell destruction with insulin deficiency; 25-50% present with DKA; ~40% of new type 1 DM now has onset after age 30 and is often initially misdiagnosed as type 2. Islet cell autoantibodies (ICAs: insulin, GAD, IA-2, ZnT8) are present in >85% at new onset and define a 3-stage model: stage 1 = >=2 autoantibodies + normoglycemia, stage 2 = autoimmunity + dysglycemia, stage 3 = overt hyperglycemia.
Type 2 DM = heterogeneous: insulin resistance + impaired insulin secretion + increased hepatic glucose production; >=80% of patients are obese (central/visceral). Insulin resistance usually precedes the secretory defect, but diabetes appears only once secretion becomes inadequate for the degree of resistance.
Type 2 DM may be asymptomatic for up to a decade before diagnosis, so some patients already have diabetes-specific complications at the time of diagnosis.
HbA1c can be misleading with hemoglobinopathies and varies by ancestry; OGTT is reserved mainly for pregnancy/GDM and cystic fibrosis-related diabetes screening, not routine care.
Symptoms of hyperglycemia: polyuria, polydipsia, weight loss, fatigue, weakness, blurred vision (lens water-content change, resolves with control), recurrent superficial infections (vaginitis, fungal skin infections), slow wound healing.
Microvascular complications usually do not appear until the second decade of hyperglycemia; macrovascular (ASCVD) risk, driven by insulin resistance and dyslipidemia, may develop before hyperglycemia is established.
Distal symmetric polyneuropathy (DSPN) is the most common diabetic neuropathy and occurs in ~50% of long-standing DM; up to 50% are asymptomatic, so annual screening for loss of protective sensation (LOPS) is required - LOPS is a major risk factor for foot ulceration and amputation.
# Additional differentials
Monogenic diabetes / MODY (autosomal dominant, onset usually <25 y, impaired secretion; HNF-4alpha, glucokinase, HNF-1alpha/1beta mutations; glucokinase MODY = mild stable hyperglycemia; HNF-1alpha MODY often responds to sulfonylureas)
Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) - glucose intolerance arising in 2nd/3rd trimester; diabetes in 1st trimester is classified as pre-existing pregestational diabetes
Atypical diabetes (features of both type 1 and type 2, not single-gene; includes ketosis-prone diabetes) and latent autoimmune diabetes of adults (5-10% of phenotypic type 2 have GAD/ICA)
Pancreatogenic (type 3c) DM from pancreatitis, pancreatectomy, cystic fibrosis (CF-related diabetes prevalence ~50%), hemochromatosis
Endocrinopathy-related hyperglycemia: acromegaly, Cushing's syndrome, glucagonoma, pheochromocytoma, somatostatinoma, hyperthyroidism
Drug/chemical-induced diabetes: glucocorticoids, calcineurin/mTOR inhibitors, pentamidine, nicotinic acid, statins, HIV/protease-inhibitor therapy, thiazides, second-generation antipsychotics, immune checkpoint inhibitors
Neonatal diabetes (onset <6 months; ATP-sensitive K+ channel subunit mutations may respond to sulfonylureas)
# Non infectious mimics
Stress/illness-related transient hyperglycemia from counterregulatory hormone excess (surgery, infection, critical illness) - hyperglycemia in a hospitalized patient need not indicate established DM
Other causes of increased anion-gap acidosis when DKA is suspected: starvation ketosis and alcoholic ketoacidosis (bicarbonate usually >15 meq/L)
# Additional investigations
HbA1c (lab method NGSP-aligned to DCCT reference) for diagnosis and monitoring; affected by hemoglobinopathies and ancestry
Fasting plasma glucose and 75-g OGTT (OGTT mainly for pregnancy/GDM and CF-related diabetes screening)
Islet cell autoantibodies (GAD, IA-2, ZnT8, insulin) to classify type 1 vs type 2 and identify presymptomatic type 1 DM
C-peptide as a marker of endogenous insulin secretion (distinguishes endogenous vs exogenous insulin)
Spot urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) plus estimated GFR annually for nephropathy (screen 5 y after type 1 onset and at type 2 diagnosis); albuminuria = UACR >30 mg/g, severe >300 mg/g
Annual dilated retinal exam (or retinal photography) - nondilated primary-care exams are inadequate
Annual foot exam with 10-g monofilament, 128-Hz vibration, ankle reflexes, pedal pulses; annual DSPN screening for LOPS
In DKA/HHS: serum/plasma beta-hydroxybutyrate (preferred over nitroprusside which detects acetoacetate), glucose, electrolytes with corrected sodium, anion gap, bicarbonate, BUN/creatinine
# Red flags
Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA): hyperglycemia (glucose >13.9 mmol/L [250 mg/dL]; may be near-normal = euglycemic DKA, especially on SGLT-2 inhibitors), ketosis, anion-gap metabolic acidosis (bicarbonate <15-18 mmol/L); nausea/vomiting, severe abdominal pain (may mimic acute pancreatitis), Kussmaul respirations, fruity breath, volume depletion, lethargy progressing to coma; cerebral edema is a serious complication mainly in children
Hyperglycemic hyperosmolar state (HHS): older patient, more pronounced dehydration, marked hyperglycemia (often 33.3-66.6 mmol/L [600-1200 mg/dL]) with mental status changes; mortality up to 15% (higher than DKA)
Hypoglycemia (<3.0 mmol/L, <54 mg/dL); hypoglycemia unawareness from autonomic neuropathy raises risk of severe events and can prolong QTc - sudden death has been attributed to cardiac autonomic neuropathy
New blindness risk: proliferative retinopathy with neovascularization and vitreous hemorrhage; clinically significant macular edema
Loss of protective sensation / foot ulceration predisposing to lower-extremity amputation
Silent (painless) myocardial ischemia is common - absence of chest pain does not exclude coronary disease; consider cardiac evaluation before major surgery
# Pitfalls
Do not over-rely on classic features (age of onset, obesity, insulin need) to classify DM - many adult-onset type 1 cases are misdiagnosed as type 2, and many new type 1 patients are now overweight/obese; measure ICAs when type 1 features are present.
Euglycemic DKA can occur (notably with SGLT-2 inhibitors) - a near-normal glucose does not exclude DKA; check ketones and acid-base.
In DKA, hyperamylasemia is usually salivary in origin and does NOT diagnose pancreatitis; obtain serum lipase if pancreatitis is suspected.
Measured serum sodium is falsely lowered by hyperglycemia (correct ~1.6 mmol/L per 5.6 mmol/L [100 mg/dL] glucose rise); a normal sodium in DKA signals a profound water deficit.
Serum potassium may be normal or high at DKA presentation despite a large total-body potassium deficit (acidosis/volume depletion shift).
Nitroprusside ketone tests detect acetoacetate, not the predominant beta-hydroxybutyrate, and give false positives with captopril, penicillamine, or valproic acid - prefer serum beta-hydroxybutyrate.
Glycemic control may transiently worsen established retinopathy in the first 6-12 months of improvement, and GLP-1 receptor agonists with marked glycemic improvement have been associated with worsening retinopathy.
CKD without retinopathy in type 1 DM should prompt a search for an alternative (non-diabetic) cause of kidney disease.
Very strict glycemic control in acutely/critically ill patients (NICE-SUGAR) increased mortality and severe hypoglycemia - avoid overly tight targets in hospital.
# Management
Type 1 diabetes: lifelong insulin replacement with basal-bolus regimens or an insulin pump, carbohydrate counting and structured glucose monitoring
Type 2 diabetes: lifestyle modification with diet, weight loss and physical activity, metformin first-line, adding SGLT2 inhibitors or GLP-1 receptor agonists especially where cardiovascular or renal disease coexists
Individualised glycaemic targets guided by HbA1c and continuous or capillary glucose monitoring with time-in-range
Structured patient education and self-management support with hypoglycaemia avoidance
Cardiovascular risk reduction through blood-pressure control, statin therapy and smoking cessation
Regular complication surveillance with dilated retinal examination, urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio and eGFR, and foot and neuropathy screening
Multidisciplinary care involving diabetes specialist nurses, dietitians, podiatry and ophthalmology
Escalate to insulin or specialist input for uncontrolled hyperglycaemia or acute metabolic decompensation
