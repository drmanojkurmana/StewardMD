# Diabetic Foot Infection
Diabetic Foot Infection, Skin & Soft Tissue.
# Pathogens
Mild superficial diabetic foot infections are usually monomicrobial with gram-positive cocci, while deeper, chronic, or previously treated wounds become polymicrobial, incorporating gram-negatives and anaerobes — empiric spectrum should match wound chronicity and depth.
Very likely: Staphylococcus aureus; Streptococcus species.
# Likely
Enterobacteriaceae (E. coli, Klebsiella, Proteus) — especially in chronic or previously treated wounds
# Possible
Pseudomonas aeruginosa (if wound exposed to water/soaking)
Anaerobes (if deep, necrotic, or malodorous wound)
# Investigations
Deep tissue/bone culture (avoid superficial swab alone)
Superficial swabs poorly correlate with the true infecting organism; deep culture better guides targeted therapy.
Plain X-ray of the foot, Screens for osteomyelitis and gas in soft tissue.
MRI (if osteomyelitis suspected and X-ray inconclusive), More sensitive for early osteomyelitis than plain films.
HbA1c and glycemic control assessment, Poor glycemic control impairs healing and infection clearance; relevant to overall management.
Vascular assessment (ABI/Doppler)
Peripheral arterial disease impairs healing and antibiotic delivery to tissue — relevant to overall treatment plan and need for revascularization.
Antibiotics recommended

# Pathophysiology
Diabetic foot infection arises from the interaction of peripheral sensory, motor, and autonomic neuropathy with abnormal foot biomechanics, peripheral arterial disease, and impaired wound healing. Sensory neuropathy removes protective pain sensation, allowing unrecognized repetitive or major trauma; impaired proprioception causes abnormal weight-bearing and callus/ulcer formation. Motor and sensory neuropathy produce structural deformities (hammer/claw toe, prominent metatarsal heads, Charcot joint). Autonomic neuropathy causes anhidrosis with dry, fissured skin. Peripheral arterial disease and poor healing then allow minor skin breaks to enlarge and become infected. Foot osteomyelitis is almost always acquired by the exogenous (contiguous) route, progressing from skin/soft-tissue infection into bone, and can also follow deep wound infection after foot surgery.
# Prognosis
Diabetes is the leading cause of nontraumatic lower-extremity amputation. Diabetic foot osteomyelitis increases amputation risk; a foot ulcer (or subsequent ulceration) carries a 14-24% risk of eventual amputation. Adequate management of early-stage infection lowers the amputation rate, and wound debridement combined with antibiotics makes amputation unnecessary in roughly two-thirds of patients.
Infections are described broadly as mild/non-limb-threatening versus more severe.
# Clinical pearls
A wound that probes to bone (rock-hard, gritty base without intervening soft tissue) strongly predicts underlying osteomyelitis; the probe-to-bone test has a positive predictive value of ~90% in high-pretest-probability populations.
In a hospitalized diabetic patient with a chronic deep foot ulcer, the pretest probability of osteomyelitis is high (~65%), so a positive probe-to-bone test can often establish the diagnosis clinically without imaging.
A negative probe-to-bone test lowers the likelihood of osteomyelitis (likelihood ratio ~0.2).
An infected ulcer is a clinical diagnosis; superficial swab cultures recover multiple organisms of uncertain significance and correlate poorly with bone biopsy (only ~24% concordance in one study).
Culture-pathogen concordance between deep samples and bone is best for S. aureus (40-50%) and poorest for coagulase-negative staphylococci, anaerobes, and gram-negative bacilli.
About 34% of people with diabetes develop a foot ulcer in their lifetime; among those with an ulcer, ~5% per year progress to foot osteomyelitis.
Gas gangrene-like findings may occur in a diabetic foot wound even in the absence of clostridial infection.
# Additional differentials
Charcot neuroarthropathy (neuropathic joint disease) — can mimic or coexist with foot osteomyelitis
Purely neuropathic ulcer without infection
Cellulitis without underlying ulceration
# Non infectious mimics
Charcot (neuropathic) arthropathy with bony deformity, which can radiographically and clinically resemble osteomyelitis
Callus or pressure changes from abnormal foot biomechanics
# Additional investigations
Probe-to-bone test with a blunt sterile instrument as a bedside clinical assessment
