# Idiopathic intracranial hypertension
Idiopathic intracranial hypertension, Neurology.
Red flags: Progressive visual loss needs urgent treatment.
# Investigations
Fundoscopy (papilloedema)
MRI + MR venography
LP with opening pressure
# Pathophysiology
Raised CSF pressure without a space-occupying lesion, occurring in the setting of obesity, female sex, and occasionally pregnancy; partial obstruction of the cerebral venous sinuses is found in a small number of cases. Stenosis at the transverse-sigmoid sinus junction is commonly present, providing the rationale for venous sinus stenting. Vitamin A excess is a recognised cause of pseudotumor cerebri.
# Prognosis
Headache often improves within weeks of starting medical therapy. However, unremitting papilloedema leads to progressive peripheral field loss and optic atrophy, and fulminant cases can cause rapid blindness, so visual loss is the feared outcome that drives urgent intervention.
# Clinical pearls
Headache is typically generalized, present on awakening, eases through the day, and worsens on lying down.
Transient visual obscurations are a classic feature: brief (usually seconds), in one or both eyes, often triggered by a postural shift, and most frequent when headache is at its worst.
Obscurations that are prolonged or spontaneous indicate more threatening papilloedema.
The syndrome can occur without visual symptoms, especially when the fundi look normal, so it must be considered even when funduscopy is unremarkable.
Persistently raised intracranial pressure can itself trigger a chronic migraine syndrome.
Visual acuity is usually preserved in papilloedema unless it is severe, longstanding, or complicated by macular oedema and haemorrhage; field loss precedes acuity loss.
Unilateral or bilateral abducens (6th nerve) palsy is a classic false-localising sign of raised intracranial pressure and is corroborated by papilloedema on fundoscopy.
# Additional differentials
Optic disc drusen, which produce pseudopapilloedema and can be mistaken for true papilloedema.
Obstructive sleep apnoea and poorly controlled hypertension, which also cause morning or nocturnal headache.
# Non infectious mimics
Space-occupying intracranial lesion causing raised CSF pressure headache.
Dural venous sinus thrombosis.
Arteriovenous (dural) shunt.
# Additional investigations
MRI as the initial study, including MR venography, to look for a mass and to search for dural venous sinus thrombosis or an AV shunt.
Lumbar puncture performed while the patient is symptomatic, measuring opening pressure in the lateral decubitus position; both the pressure and the symptomatic response to removing 20-30 mL of CSF can be assessed.
Formal visual field testing, performed even when there is no overt ophthalmic involvement.
B-mode ocular ultrasound to detect buried optic disc drusen (hyperechoic due to calcium) when pseudopapilloedema is suspected.
# Red flags
Prolonged or spontaneous transient visual obscurations signal more threatening papilloedema.
Insidious peripheral visual field loss with progressive optic atrophy under unremitting papilloedema; in this setting a reduction in disc swelling is an ominous sign of a dying nerve, not resolution.
Fulminant papilloedema can cause rapid-onset blindness and constitutes an emergency.
# Pitfalls
Inaccurate CSF opening-pressure readings at lumbar puncture are a common pitfall.
Failing to exclude a dural venous sinus thrombosis before attributing raised pressure to IIH.
Mistaking buried optic disc drusen (pseudopapilloedema) for true papilloedema, prompting an unnecessary work-up.
Assuming a normal fundus excludes the diagnosis; IIH can occur without funduscopic changes, so formal perimetry is still warranted.
# Confirm
Headache + visual disturbance with papilloedema, typically a young obese woman; normal imaging; LP opening pressure raised with normal CSF.
Refer neurology + ophthalmology.
Acetazolamide + weight loss
# Steps
MRI + MR venography (exclude mass/venous sinus thrombosis), then LP to confirm raised opening pressure with normal CSF.
Weight loss is central; consider a GLP-1 receptor agonist if unable to lose weight by diet.
Acetazolamide first-line (250-500 mg bid) to lower CSF production; topiramate is the next agent if ineffective.
Formal perimetry and urgent ophthalmology if visual loss; severe/progressive loss needs CSF diversion (lumboperitoneal shunt) or optic-nerve-sheath fenestration.
Stop precipitants (tetracyclines, vitamin A, retinoids).
Acetazolamide + weight loss; urgent ophthalmology if visual loss
