# Rheumatological overlap syndrome
Rheumatological overlap syndrome, Rheumatology / Vascular, Rheumatology / Vascular Medicine.
Aliases: overlap syndrome; connective tissue overlap; anti-PM/Scl.
# Pathophysiology
Rheumatological overlap syndromes arise when autoimmune mechanisms characteristic of more than one defined connective tissue disease coexist in a single patient. Shared genetic susceptibility and dysregulated adaptive immunity generate autoantibodies and tissue inflammation spanning several disorders, sometimes marked by antibodies such as anti-PM/Scl or anti-Ku that associate with combined phenotypes. The resulting clinical picture blends the vasculopathy, fibrosis, synovitis, and myositis of the component diseases, and organ involvement reflects the summed pathology.
# Prognosis
Outcome is determined by the combination and severity of component diseases and their organ involvement; some overlaps behave indolently while others, particularly those with significant lung, cardiac, or renal disease, carry substantial morbidity. Tailored immunosuppression can control inflammation across the overlapping conditions.
# Clinical pearls
Patients simultaneously fulfil criteria for two or more distinct connective tissue or autoimmune diseases
Common combinations include systemic sclerosis with inflammatory myositis (scleromyositis) or lupus with rheumatoid arthritis
Specific autoantibodies such as anti-PM/Scl and anti-Ku help characterise particular overlap phenotypes
Unlike mixed connective tissue disease, overlap syndromes meet full criteria for each component disorder rather than sharing a single defining antibody
Management is directed at the dominant and most threatening organ involvement across the combined diseases
Careful serological and clinical mapping guides prognosis and treatment intensity
The overlapping features may appear together or accrue sequentially over time
Additional differentials: Mixed connective tissue disease; Systemic lupus erythematosus; Systemic sclerosis; Idiopathic inflammatory myopathy; Rheumatoid arthritis; Undifferentiated connective tissue disease.
# Additional investigations
Antinuclear antibody with a broad extractable nuclear antigen and myositis-specific/associated antibody panel
Anti-PM/Scl and anti-Ku testing when scleromyositis is suspected
Creatine kinase and inflammatory markers for myositis
Transthoracic echocardiography and pulmonary function tests for cardiopulmonary involvement
High-resolution CT for interstitial lung disease
Renal function and urinalysis to detect nephritis
# Red flags
Severe myositis with respiratory or bulbar involvement
Progressive interstitial lung disease or pulmonary hypertension
Lupus-type nephritis with declining renal function
Digital or systemic ischaemia from active vasculopathy
# Pitfalls
Treating only the most obvious component and neglecting concurrent organ-threatening disease
Confusing an overlap syndrome with mixed connective tissue disease and mislabelling the serology
Missing evolving features of a second disorder over time
Under-recognising myositis-associated interstitial lung disease
# Management
Direct treatment at the dominant and most organ-threatening manifestation across the coexisting connective tissue diseases rather than at a single label
Use corticosteroids with a steroid-sparing immunosuppressant such as methotrexate, azathioprine or mycophenolate for the inflammatory arthritis and myositis components
Escalate to potent agents such as cyclophosphamide or rituximab for severe organ involvement including nephritis or progressive interstitial lung disease
Manage scleroderma-spectrum features with Raynaud measures including a calcium-channel blocker, and treat pulmonary arterial hypertension where present
Monitor across systems with creatine kinase, renal function and urinalysis, pulmonary function tests, echocardiography and high-resolution CT
Coordinate care through rheumatology with respiratory, nephrology and cardiology input, adjusting immunosuppression intensity to the combined phenotype
