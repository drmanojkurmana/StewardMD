# Adult cardiac arrest (ALS/ACLS)
Adults
Emergency department, ward, ICU, prehospital
High-quality CPR with minimal interruptions, early defibrillation for VF/pVT, adrenaline 1 mg every 3 to 5 minutes (as soon as possible for non-shockable rhythms, after failed initial shocks for shockable rhythms), amiodarone or lidocaine for refractory VF/pVT, and an active search for reversible causes.
Aliases: cardiac arrest; ACLS; ALS; CPR; code blue; VF; pulseless VT; PEA; cardiopulmonary resuscitation; 4 Hs and 4 Ts.
# Recognise and call for help
Unresponsive with absent or abnormal (agonal) breathing: treat as cardiac arrest. Take no more than 10 seconds to feel for a central pulse; if in doubt, start CPR.
Call the resuscitation team (code blue), bring a defibrillator and note the time of collapse.
Many in-hospital arrests have warning signs: use a track-and-trigger score (for example NEWS2) and act on deterioration early.
# High-quality CPR and defibrillation
Compress the centre of the chest at 100 to 120/min, depth at least 5 cm (not more than 6 cm), allow full recoil; change the compressor every 2 minutes or sooner if tired.
No advanced airway: 30:2 with bag-mask and 100% oxygen. With a tracheal tube or supraglottic airway: 10 breaths/min (1 every 6 seconds) with continuous compressions. Avoid hyperventilation.
Antero-lateral pads (lateral pad directly below the armpit in the mid-axillary line). Keep compressing while charging, keep the pre-shock pause under 5 seconds and resume compressions straight after the shock without a pulse check.
Energy: biphasic per manufacturer (AHA: usually 120 to 200 J; ERC: first shock at least 150 J), same or higher for later shocks; use the maximum if the setting is unknown. Monophasic 360 J.
Rhythm check every 2 minutes. Check a pulse only if an organised rhythm appears or there are signs of ROSC (sudden ETCO2 rise, arterial waveform, purposeful movement).
Witnessed, monitored VF/pVT with a defibrillator already attached (cath lab, CCU): up to 3 stacked shocks may be given; count them as the first shock for drug timing.
# Drugs, access and airway
Access: attempt IV first; use IO if IV fails after 2 attempts or is not feasible. The tracheal route is no longer recommended for drugs.
Non-shockable rhythm (asystole, PEA): adrenaline 1 mg IV or IO as soon as access is obtained, then every 3 to 5 minutes.
Shockable rhythm (VF/pVT): adrenaline 1 mg IV or IO after the 3rd shock (ERC/RCUK) or after the 2nd shock (AHA algorithm), then every 3 to 5 minutes. Amiodarone 300 mg after 3 shocks and a further 150 mg after 5 shocks; lidocaine is an alternative when amiodarone is unavailable.
Refractory VF (persisting after 3 or more failed shocks): recheck pad position and consider changing to anterior-posterior pads (vector change). Double sequential external defibrillation has limited evidence (one RCT, DOSE-VF 2022); use it only where local protocol allows (RCUK 2025 does not recommend routine use).
Airway: start with bag-mask; progress to a supraglottic airway or tracheal tube only if skilled (compression pause under 5 seconds). Confirm tube position with a sustained waveform capnography trace.
Do not give calcium, sodium bicarbonate, magnesium or steroids routinely; reserve them for specific causes (hyperkalaemia, sodium channel blocker toxicity, torsades de pointes).
# Reversible causes (4 Hs and 4 Ts)
Hypoxia, hypovolaemia, hypo/hyperkalaemia and other metabolic causes (including hypoglycaemia and acidosis), hypothermia; thrombosis (coronary or pulmonary), tension pneumothorax, tamponade, toxins.
Hyperkalaemic arrest (ERC): calcium chloride 10% 10 mL IV by rapid bolus; soluble insulin 10 units with glucose 25 g (50 mL of 50% glucose) IV by rapid injection, then monitor blood glucose for hypoglycaemia; sodium bicarbonate 8.4% 50 mmol IV if there is severe acidosis or renal failure. Give calcium and bicarbonate through separate lines or flush between them. Consider dialysis or ECPR if CPR fails.
Suspected or confirmed pulmonary embolism: give thrombolysis and continue CPR for 60 to 90 minutes afterwards.
Point-of-care ultrasound can show tamponade, pneumothorax or hypovolaemia but must not lengthen pauses; right ventricular dilatation alone does not diagnose PE.
India: think of poisoning (organophosphates, aluminium phosphide, yellow oleander), snakebite, hyperkalaemia in CKD and electrolyte loss from diarrhoeal illness.
# Monitoring during CPR
Waveform capnography: ETCO2 below 10 mmHg suggests poor CPR quality or poor prognosis; above 10 and ideally above 20 mmHg is associated with ROSC. An abrupt sustained rise suggests ROSC.
With an arterial line (physiology-guided CPR, ERC): aim for diastolic pressure 30 mmHg or more and ETCO2 25 mmHg (3.3 kPa) or more. In highly monitored arrests, adrenaline may be given in 50 to 100 microgram increments first.
Record times of shocks and drugs aloud; use a timer and CPR feedback device if available.
# Special situations
Pregnancy (fundus at or above the umbilicus): continuous manual left uterine displacement, IV access above the diaphragm, stop IV magnesium and give calcium if on magnesium, and prepare resuscitative delivery aiming for 5 minutes if no ROSC.
Hypothermia, drowning and anaphylaxis: follow the specific protocols (modified shock and drug rules below 30 degrees C; 5 initial rescue breaths in drowning).
CPR-induced consciousness: small doses of sedative or analgesic (for example fentanyl, ketamine, midazolam) may be used; never give a neuromuscular blocker alone to a conscious patient.
