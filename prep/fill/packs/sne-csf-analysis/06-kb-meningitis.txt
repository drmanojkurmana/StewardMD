# Acute Bacterial Meningitis
Acute Bacterial Meningitis, Central Nervous System.
# Pathogens
S. pneumoniae and N. meningitidis are the leading causes of community-acquired bacterial meningitis across most age groups. Listeria is specifically added in neonates, those over 50, pregnant patients, and the immunocompromised, since standard empiric regimens do not cover it.
Very likely: Streptococcus pneumoniae; Neisseria meningitidis.
Likely: Listeria monocytogenes (neonates, elderly, immunocompromised, pregnant).
Possible: Haemophilus influenzae (unvaccinated populations); Gram-negative bacilli (post-neurosurgery, immunocompromised).
Red flags: Immediate empiric antibiotics required.

# Pathophysiology
Bacterial meningitis is an acute purulent infection of the subarachnoid space accompanied by a CNS inflammatory reaction that can cause decreased consciousness, seizures, raised intracranial pressure and stroke; meninges, subarachnoid space and brain parenchyma are often jointly involved (meningoencephalitis). The leading organisms (S. pneumoniae, N. meningitidis) first colonize the nasopharynx, cross or breach the epithelium into the bloodstream, evade neutrophil phagocytosis and complement killing via their polysaccharide capsule, and reach the CSF through the choroid plexus or cerebral capillary endothelium. They multiply readily in CSF because normal CSF has few leukocytes and little complement/immunoglobulin, so opsonization and phagocytosis are poor. Much of the neurologic injury comes from the host inflammatory response rather than direct bacterial damage, so injury can progress even after antibiotics sterilize the CSF. Lysis of bacteria releases cell-wall components (gram-negative LPS; pneumococcal teichoic acid and peptidoglycan) that drive production of TNF-alpha, IL-1beta, chemokines and other mediators by microglia, astrocytes, monocytes, endothelium and CSF leukocytes. These cytokines increase blood-brain barrier permeability (vasogenic edema and protein leakage), the proteinaceous/leukocytic exudate obstructs CSF flow and resorption (obstructive and communicating hydrocephalus, interstitial edema), and neutrophil degranulation plus reactive oxygen/nitrogen species and excitatory amino acids produce cytotoxic edema and cell death (notably in the hippocampal dentate gyrus). Cerebral blood flow rises early then falls with loss of autoregulation; vasculitis and thrombosis of arteries, cortical veins and dural sinuses cause ischemia/infarction. The combined interstitial, vasogenic and cytotoxic edema raises ICP and can lead to coma and herniation.
# Prognosis
Case-fatality varies by organism: roughly 3-7% for H. influenzae, N. meningitidis or group B streptococcal meningitis, about 15% for Listeria, and around 20% for pneumococcal meningitis. Risk of death rises with decreased consciousness on admission, seizures within 24 h, signs of raised ICP, age (infancy or >50 years), comorbidities such as shock or need for mechanical ventilation, and delay in starting treatment. A markedly low CSF glucose (<40 mg/dL) and very high CSF protein (>300 mg/dL) predict worse outcomes in some series. About 25% of survivors have moderate-to-severe sequelae, commonly decreased intellectual function, memory impairment, seizures, sensorineural hearing loss with dizziness, and gait disturbance.
# Severity classification
For distinguishing bacterial from aseptic meningitis in children, the prospectively validated Bacterial Meningitis Score is cited: very low probability (NPV ~99.7%) when all of negative CSF Gram stain, CSF neutrophils <1000/uL, CSF protein <80 mg/dL, peripheral ANC <10,000/uL, and no seizures are met.
# Clinical pearls
The classic triad of fever, headache and nuchal rigidity is useful but each component occurs in over 80% of adult cases while the full triad is frequently incomplete, so its absence does not exclude bacterial meningitis.
A reduced level of consciousness, ranging from lethargy to coma, is present in more than three-quarters of patients; nausea, vomiting and photophobia are also common.
Nuchal rigidity is the characteristic sign of meningeal irritation (neck resists passive flexion), but Kernig's and Brudzinski's signs have uncertain sensitivity/specificity and may be absent in the very young, the elderly, the immunocompromised or those with depressed mental status.
Coexisting cervical spine disease in older patients can produce false-positive neck stiffness.
