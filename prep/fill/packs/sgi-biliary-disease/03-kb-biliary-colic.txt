# Biliary colic / cholelithiasis
Biliary colic / cholelithiasis, Gastroenterology.
Red flags: Fever/jaundice → cholecystitis/cholangitis (infective).
# Investigations
Abdominal ultrasound
LFTs
# Pathophysiology
Biliary colic results from transient obstruction of the cystic duct (or common bile duct) by a gallstone migrating out of the gallbladder. The obstruction raises intraluminal pressure and distends the viscus; because repetitive biliary contractions cannot overcome or relieve the blockage, a steady visceral pain is generated. When the stone disimpacts or falls back, pressure normalizes and the pain resolves spontaneously, so an uncomplicated episode is self-limited and is NOT accompanied by inflammation, fever, or sustained cholestasis. Underlying stones are usually cholesterol stones (>90% in Western populations), which form when bile becomes supersaturated with cholesterol, cholesterol monohydrate crystals nucleate, and gallbladder hypomotility allows stasis and stone growth; gallbladder emptying is frequently impaired in stone formers. Distinct mechanisms (mechanical, chemical from lysolecithin, and bacterial) convert simple obstruction into acute cholecystitis when the cystic duct stays blocked.
# Prognosis
Uncomplicated biliary colic is self-limited; the individual attack resolves once the transient obstruction clears. The natural history of the underlying gallstones is generally favorable: 60-80% of asymptomatic stone carriers stay symptom-free for up to 25 years, the chance of developing symptoms is only ~2-4% per year in the first 5 years after diagnosis (falling to 1-2% thereafter), and the yearly incidence of complications is only ~0.1-0.3%. The cumulative risk of death from gallstone disease under expectant management is small. However, once biliary pain has occurred, the likelihood of recurrent attacks and of complications requiring intervention rises substantially; younger patients at diagnosis are more likely to become symptomatic than those over 60.
Acute cholecystitis is itself triaged by complications (e.g., empyema, gangrene, perforation, Mirizzi's syndrome).
# Clinical pearls
Biliary colic is the most specific symptom of gallstone disease, yet 'colic' is a misnomer: the pain is steady rather than waxing-and-waning, reflecting non-relievable distention of the gallbladder/duct against a stone obstructing the cystic duct or CBD.
A typical episode begins abruptly and lasts roughly 30 minutes to 5 hours before easing; pain that persists beyond ~5 hours should prompt suspicion of acute cholecystitis rather than uncomplicated colic.
Pain is characteristically a severe steady ache or fullness in the epigastrium or right upper quadrant, often radiating to the interscapular region, right scapula, or shoulder.
Attacks may be triggered by a fatty meal, a large meal after prolonged fasting, or even a normal meal, and are frequently nocturnal, occurring within a few hours of going to bed.
Nausea and vomiting commonly accompany the pain, but fever/chills are NOT features of uncomplicated colic and signal a complication.
Vague short-lived epigastric fullness, dyspepsia, belching, or flatulence after fatty food are nonspecific and occur with or without gallstones; they should not be equated with true biliary pain.
Most asymptomatic gallstones stay silent: 60-80% remain symptom-free over follow-up up to 25 years, so incidentally found stones without referable symptoms generally do not warrant intervention.
Once colic occurs, the disease has 'declared' itself; complications requiring surgery are far more common in patients who have developed biliary pain than in the asymptomatic group, and most patients who develop complications had prior warning symptoms.
Sudden awakening from sleep by pain favors an organic cause such as biliary colic over a functional gut-brain disorder.
# Additional differentials
Acute cholecystitis (pain >5 h, fever, Murphy's sign) — represents biliary pain that fails to resolve as the cystic duct stays obstructed
Choledocholithiasis with cholangitis — CBD stone causing biliary pain plus jaundice and spiking fevers/chills (Charcot's triad)
Gallstone pancreatitis — stone-related obstruction at the ampulla; pain is severe and persists for days, not hours
Peptic ulcer disease / functional dyspepsia — ulcer pain is intermittent over weeks-months and may be relieved by eating, unlike the sudden hours-long onset of colic
Irritable bowel syndrome and other disorders of gut-brain interaction — common causes of abdominal pain, exacerbated by stress rather than fatty meals
Sphincter of Oddi dysfunction / biliary dyskinesia — biliary-type pain without stones, may follow cholecystectomy
Acalculous cholecystopathy — disordered gallbladder motility producing recurrent biliary pain in the absence of stones
Right-lower-lobe pneumonia, mesenteric ischemia, or right renal colic as non-biliary causes of RUQ/flank pain
# Non infectious mimics
Functional dyspepsia / non-ulcer dyspepsia presenting with post-prandial epigastric discomfort
Gastroesophageal reflux / pill esophagitis (biliary colic itself can be mistaken for unexplained upper-GI symptoms)
Irritable bowel syndrome (most common cause of abdominal pain alongside functional dyspepsia)
Sphincter of Oddi dysfunction and acalculous cholecystopathy (biliary-type pain without calculi)
Cardiac/precordial referral — gallbladder pain may occasionally be referred to the precordium
# Additional investigations
Right-upper-quadrant ultrasound is the procedure of choice: rapid, not limited by jaundice or pregnancy, and >95% accurate for gallstones, identifying calculi as small as ~1.5 mm by their acoustic shadowing and gravity-dependent movement
Ultrasound also distinguishes biliary sludge (low-echogenicity layer that shifts with position but casts NO acoustic shadow) from true stones, and can assess gallbladder emptying
Serum bilirubin and/or alkaline phosphatase elevation during a biliary attack suggests a common-duct stone rather than simple colic
Cholescintigraphy (HIDA/DISIDA scan): non-visualization of the gallbladder indicates cystic-duct obstruction and supports acute cholecystitis
MRCP, EUS, or ERCP for suspected choledocholithiasis; ERCP is the gold standard and also therapeutic, MRCP often the initial test
Plain abdominal film detects only the radiopaque minority of stones (~10-15% of cholesterol, ~50% of pigment) and has low yield
# Red flags
Pain persisting beyond ~5 hours — suspect acute cholecystitis
Fever, chills, or rigors with biliary pain — implies a complication (cholecystitis, cholangitis, or pancreatitis), not uncomplicated colic
Jaundice and/or rising bilirubin or alkaline phosphatase — suggests common-bile-duct stone or obstruction
Charcot's triad (biliary pain + jaundice + spiking fever/chills) — acute cholangitis
Murphy's sign (inspiratory arrest on RUQ palpation) with RUQ tenderness, fever, and leukocytosis — acute cholecystitis
Severe pain persisting for days, with confusion/sepsis features — suggests pancreatitis or suppurative cholangitis
# Pitfalls
Treating the term 'colic' literally and expecting intermittent crampy pain — biliary colic is steady, and waiting for it to fluctuate can delay recognition.
Attributing nonspecific fatty-food dyspepsia, belching, or bloating to gallstones; these symptoms are equally common in people without stones and do not establish a biliary cause.
Assuming an incidental gallstone explains a patient's symptoms — most stones are silent, and a coexisting non-biliary disorder (e.g., reflux, peptic ulcer, IBS) may be the true cause.
Missing that fever or jaundice has converted 'colic' into a complication requiring different (often urgent) management.
Relying on a plain abdominal film, which misses most cholesterol stones, instead of ultrasound.
Overlooking acalculous causes of biliary-type pain (sphincter of Oddi dysfunction, acalculous cholecystopathy) when ultrasound shows no stones.
# Confirm
Postprandial RUQ/epigastric pain ± radiation to scapula, lasting <6 h, with gallstones on US and no fever/inflammation.
Outpatient; surgical referral.
Analgesia + antiemetic for the acute episode
# Steps
Analgesia (NSAID/opioid) and antiemetics for the acute episode.
USS to confirm gallstones; check LFTs (exclude CBD stone/cholecystitis).
Low-fat dietary advice while awaiting surgery.
Elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy for symptomatic gallstones.
Escalate if fever, persistent pain or jaundice (cholecystitis/cholangitis).
Analgesia; refer for elective cholecystectomy
