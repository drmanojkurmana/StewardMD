# Pathophysiology
Peptic ulcers are mucosal breaks >5 mm extending into the submucosa, occurring in the stomach (gastric ulcer, GU) or duodenum (duodenal ulcer, DU); >95% of DUs lie in the first part of the duodenum, within about 3 cm of the pylorus. The disease reflects an imbalance between aggressive factors (acid, pepsin) and mucosal defence. H. pylori and NSAIDs cause the majority of ulcers but act differently: H. pylori-associated benign GUs show antral gastritis, whereas NSAID-related GUs typically lack chronic active gastritis and instead show a chemical gastropathy (foveolar hyperplasia, lamina propria oedema). Duodenal ulcer patients tend to have increased basal and nocturnal acid secretion (with substantial overlap with controls) and reduced duodenal bicarbonate, both potentially influenced by H. pylori; gastric acid output in GU patients is usually normal or reduced, implying impaired mucosal defence. H. pylori is a gram-negative microaerophilic urease-producing rod living in the gastric mucous layer; virulence factors such as the cag pathogenicity island (delivering CagA), VacA, and urease determine the clinical outcome among colonisation, PUD, MALT lymphoma, and gastric adenocarcinoma.
# Prognosis
More than 90% of both gastric and duodenal ulcers heal with conventional therapy; a GU not healed by 12 weeks or a DU not healed by 8 weeks is considered refractory. Eradicating H. pylori has cut duodenal ulcer recurrence by >80%. GI bleeding complicates roughly 15% of PUD with a 30-day mortality of about 2.5-10% (most deaths from non-bleeding causes such as multiorgan failure, pulmonary complications, and malignancy). Perforation occurs in ~6-7% of patients with 30-day mortality >20%, and penetration carries comparable mortality; gastric outlet obstruction is least common (~1-2%). Complications and mortality rise in patients over 60, partly reflecting greater NSAID use.
# Clinical pearls
Around two-thirds of patients with peptic ulcer disease have no abdominal pain, and up to ~87% of those with NSAID-related mucosal injury first present with a complication (bleeding, perforation, or obstruction) without any preceding symptoms.
Classic duodenal ulcer pain comes on roughly 90 minutes to 3 hours after eating and is typically eased by food or antacids; pain that wakes the patient between midnight and 3 a.m. is the most discriminating symptom and is reported by about two-thirds of duodenal ulcer patients.
Gastric ulcer pain can instead be provoked by eating, and nausea and weight loss are commoner with gastric than duodenal ulcers.
Elderly and immunosuppressed patients are less likely to have typical pain and may present directly with bleeding or perforation.
H. pylori and NSAIDs are the dominant risk factors (US odds ratios ~3.7 and ~3.3); NSAIDs are now described as the most common cause of PUD, with only ~one-quarter of duodenal and ~one-sixth of gastric ulcers now attributable to H. pylori.
More than half of patients with ulcer-related haemorrhage bleed without any preceding warning signs or symptoms.
Endoscopy detects an ulcer in fewer than 30% of patients investigated for dyspepsia, reflecting how poorly symptoms predict an actual ulcer.
# Additional differentials
Functional (non-ulcer/essential) dyspepsia - the commonest cause of upper abdominal discomfort, subdivided into postprandial distress syndrome and epigastric pain syndrome; up to ~80% of patients evaluated for dyspepsia have a negative work-up.
Proximal gastrointestinal tumours.
Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease.
Pancreaticobiliary disease - biliary colic, chronic pancreatitis.
Gastroduodenal Crohn's disease.
Vascular (mesenteric/ischaemic) disease presenting as upper abdominal pain.
# Infection mimics
Cytomegalovirus-related ulceration (a non-Hp, non-NSAID ulcer cause).
Herpes simplex virus-related ulceration.
Helicobacter heilmannii infection.
# Non infectious mimics
Functional/non-ulcer dyspepsia closely mimics ulcer symptoms without an actual ulcer.
Drug- or toxin-induced ulceration not from NSAIDs/H. pylori - bisphosphonates, immune checkpoint inhibitors, chemotherapy, clopidogrel, crack cocaine, glucocorticoids combined with NSAIDs, mycophenolate mofetil, potassium chloride.
Infiltrative/inflammatory mimics - Crohn's disease, sarcoidosis, eosinophilic infiltration, amyloidosis, lymphoma.
Ischaemia and radiation-induced mucosal injury.
Idiopathic acid-hypersecretory states distinct from Zollinger-Ellison syndrome.
# Additional investigations
Endoscopy is the most sensitive and specific test, allowing direct visualisation, biopsy to exclude malignancy (GU) and to test for H. pylori, and identification of a bleeding source.
Barium studies of the upper GI tract can document an ulcer but are now rarely used as a first test.
Biopsy urease tests (e.g. CLOtest, PyloriTek, Hpfast, Pronto Dry) with sensitivity/specificity >90-95% for H. pylori.
Non-invasive H. pylori tests - serology, 13C-urea breath test, and monoclonal stool antigen test; the urea breath test or monoclonal stool antigen test is preferred to document eradication, performed off antisecretory drugs for at least 7 days (serology is not useful for confirming eradication).
Fasting serum gastrin and gastric acid analysis, or secretin stimulation testing, in complicated or refractory ulcers to exclude Zollinger-Ellison syndrome.
Screening for surreptitious aspirin/NSAID use (blood or urine) in refractory H. pylori-negative PUD.
Multiple biopsies of any gastric ulcer at the index endoscopy, with repeat endoscopy at 8-12 weeks to document healing and re-biopsy if the ulcer persists.
# Red flags
Alarm features warranting prompt endoscopy rather than empirical test-and-treat - nausea, vomiting, weight loss, evidence of GI bleeding, or age >=60 years with new dyspepsia.
Constant pain no longer relieved by food/antacids and radiating to the back suggests a penetrating ulcer (e.g. into the pancreas).
Sudden severe generalised abdominal pain with a board-like, rigid abdomen (the triad of acute pain, tachycardia, and rigidity) suggests perforation.
Pain worsening with meals plus vomiting of undigested food, early satiety, weight loss, or a succussion splash suggests gastric outlet obstruction.
Tarry stools or coffee-ground emesis indicate bleeding; tachycardia and orthostasis suggest hypovolaemia from blood loss or vomiting.
# Pitfalls
Every gastric ulcer must be biopsied (multiple samples) at discovery because a GU may be malignant, and healing on therapy does not exclude cancer - about 70% of GUs ultimately found malignant show significant (usually incomplete) healing; repeat endoscopy with biopsy at 8-12 weeks is needed if the ulcer persists.
Abdominal pain has poor predictive value for an ulcer, and a normal exam or absent pain does not exclude PUD - many patients, especially the elderly or those on NSAIDs, present first with a complication.
Right-sided epigastric tenderness in about 20% of patients can mislead the examiner; epigastric tenderness overall has low predictive value.
Widespread PPI use can mask and delay the diagnosis of Zollinger-Ellison syndrome and increase false-positive gastrin results.
Consider gastrinoma/ZES when ulcers occur in unusual locations (second part of duodenum or beyond), are multiple, are refractory, recur after acid-reducing surgery, present with frank complications, or arise without H. pylori or NSAID use.
# Peptic ulcer disease / upper GI bleed: treatment
Epigastric pain ± upper-GI bleed (melaena/haematemesis). Risk-stratify bleed (Glasgow-Blatchford); OGD diagnostic and therapeutic, Admit; ICU/HDU if unstable bleed.
Resuscitate + endoscopy + IV PPI infusion
# Steps
Active bleed: resuscitate (IV access, fluids/blood, transfuse to Hb ~70 g/L threshold); correct coagulopathy.
Urgent endoscopy (within 24 h) for haemostasis; IV PPI infusion for high-risk stigmata.
Test and treat H. pylori; stop NSAIDs; continue PPI.
Repeat endoscopy/IR embolisation or surgery for rebleeding.
Risk score (Glasgow-Blatchford/Rockall) guides admission and timing.
