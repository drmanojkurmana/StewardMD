Undescended testis is common at birth and rare after infancy. Cryptorchidism affects about 3 percent of full-term and up to 30 percent of premature male newborns, because descent completes late in gestation. Spontaneous descent after 3 to 6 months is rare, so a testis still undescended at 6 months needs referral rather than further waiting.

Surgery has a clock. Definitive orchidopexy is performed between 6 and 12 months of age and completed by 18 months, catching the window when germ cells still mature normally. Operating in this window preserves spermatogenesis and Leydig cell function, while delay into childhood progressively trades fertility and raises malignancy risk.

The non-palpable testis is scoped, not scanned. Diagnostic laparoscopy is the gold standard investigation and treatment combined: it finds blind-ending spermatic vessels proving a vanishing testis, or locates an intra-abdominal testis for same-session mobilization. Ultrasound and MRI add nothing when nothing is felt, and they delay the definitive look inside.

High testes need vessel division. Fowler-Stephens orchiopexy, in one or two stages, divides the testicular vessels and relies on collateral flow from the deferential vasal artery and cremasteric vessels. The testis survives on its secondary supply and reaches the scrotum without tension, at the price of some atrophy risk that two-staging reduces.

Undescended testes turn malignant more often. The lifetime risk of testicular cancer rises 4- to 8-fold, with seminoma the commonest type, and orchidopexy does not remove the risk. What surgery changes is detectability: a scrotal testis can be felt by the patient, so tumours present earlier and smaller, and lifelong self-examination remains mandatory.

A hernia sac almost always accompanies the undescended testis. Patent processus vaginalis is present in over 90 percent, so every orchidopexy includes high dissection and ligation of the hernia sac during open or laparoscopic repair. Missing the sac leaves an indirect hernia that presents months later through the same groin.

Two absent testes with normal male genitalia is an emergency until proven otherwise. Bilateral non-palpable testes in a normally virilized newborn demand immediate evaluation for 46,XX congenital adrenal hyperplasia with its salt-wasting crisis risk, plus karyotype and hCG stimulation testing. Missing salt-wasting CAH kills within weeks, so steroids and fluids start on suspicion.

Hormones are not the answer. Hormonal therapy with hCG or GnRH agonists is not routinely recommended because durable descent occurs in under 15 percent, and treatment may harm future germ cells. Surgery remains the standard: hormones are reserved for highly selected retractile cases, never as a substitute for timely orchidopexy.
