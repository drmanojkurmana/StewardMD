# Delirium (adult)
Adults
Emergency department, ward, care home
Think delirium with any acute change in cognition or behaviour; assess with the 4AT (CAM-ICU in ICU), find and treat the causes, use non-drug care, give short-term low-dose haloperidol only for severe distress or risk, avoid benzodiazepines except for alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal, and follow up for dementia.
Aliases: acute confusional state; acute confusion; 4AT; CAM; hypoactive delirium; hyperactive delirium; haloperidol delirium; confusion in the elderly.
# Think delirium
Acute (hours to days) and fluctuating disturbance of attention and awareness with altered cognition, perception or behaviour.
At risk: age 65 or over, dementia or cognitive impairment, current hip fracture, severe illness; also frailty, sensory impairment, polypharmacy, recent surgery, urinary catheter and alcohol misuse.
Hypoactive delirium (withdrawn, slow, sleepy, poor intake) is common and often missed; hyperactive delirium brings agitation and hallucinations; mixed forms occur.
Observe people in hospital or long-term care at least daily for new changes in cognition, perception, function or behaviour, and take reports from family seriously.
# Assess with the 4AT
4AT (alertness, AMT4, months backwards, acute change or fluctuation): 4 or more suggests delirium; 1 to 3 suggests cognitive impairment; 0 makes delirium unlikely. In ICU or recovery use CAM-ICU or ICDSC.
A clinician with the right expertise confirms the diagnosis; document it in the notes and the discharge letter.
If delirium, dementia or delirium superimposed on dementia cannot be distinguished, treat as delirium first.
Check at once: capillary glucose, oxygen saturation, vital signs and sepsis screen, pain, urinary retention (bladder scan), constipation, and recent drug changes or withdrawal.
# Find the causes
Often multifactorial, and in up to 30% no cause is found. Consider infection, drugs, dehydration, metabolic and electrolyte disturbance, hypoxia, retention, constipation, pain, alcohol or drug withdrawal, stroke and head injury.
Tests guided by history and examination: CBC, urea and electrolytes, liver function, glucose, CRP, calcium, phosphate, thyroid function, blood gas or oxygen saturation, ECG, chest X-ray, urinalysis and cultures.
Urgent CT head if anticoagulated, after head injury, with focal neurological signs, or for persistent unexplained delirium.
Medication review: opioids, benzodiazepines, antipsychotics, anticholinergics, antihistamines, antiepileptics, corticosteroids, tricyclics, digoxin and antiparkinsonian drugs. Avoid abrupt withdrawal of dependence-forming drugs.
India: in febrile delirium consider malaria, scrub typhus, dengue, enteric fever and encephalitis; also consider anticholinergic (dhatura) poisoning.
# Non-drug management
Treat each cause found. Reorientate (explain where the person is, who you are and your role); provide clocks, calendars, lighting, glasses and hearing aids; involve family and relax visiting times.
Keep familiar staff and avoid ward or bed moves; protect sleep (no night procedures, low noise); encourage mobility, fluids and nutrition; manage bowels; avoid unnecessary catheters.
For distress, use verbal and non-verbal de-escalation first; avoid physical restraint where possible.
# Drugs for severe distress or risk
Only if the person is distressed or a risk to themselves or others and de-escalation has failed: short-term haloperidol, usually for 1 week or less (NICE); review daily and stop as soon as possible, typically within 1 to 2 days (SIGN).
Haloperidol 0.5 to 1 mg orally or 0.5 mg IM, maximum 2 mg in 24 hours in older people (Scottish Delirium Association pathway); start low and titrate cautiously. Younger patients may need higher doses.
Before haloperidol: ECG (avoid with prolonged QTc or other QT-prolonging drugs) and correct electrolytes; monitor for extrapyramidal effects, swallowing problems and postural hypotension (MHRA).
Alternative: risperidone 0.25 mg orally, maximum 1 mg in 24 hours.
Parkinson's disease or Lewy body dementia: do not use haloperidol; if a drug is unavoidable, lorazepam 0.5 to 1 mg orally (maximum 2 mg in 24 hours) and seek specialist advice.
Avoid benzodiazepines otherwise, as they worsen delirium; they are the treatment for alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal (see Alcohol withdrawal and delirium tremens, including thiamine).
# Monitoring and follow-up
Repeat the delirium assessment until 2 successive daily assessments are negative.
Review any antipsychotic daily and stop it before discharge where possible; if it must continue, set a clear review date.
Delirium can persist for weeks or months: if it does not resolve, re-evaluate the causes and assess for dementia.
Explain to the person and family that delirium is common and usually temporary, and record the episode in the primary care record because it raises the risk of future delirium and dementia.
# Prevention in people at risk
Within 24 hours of admission, assess people at risk and give a tailored multicomponent package (NICE): orientation, hydration, oxygenation, infection control, mobility, pain control, medication review, nutrition, sensory aids and sleep.
Avoid unnecessary catheters, bed moves, and sedative or anticholinergic drugs.
Do not give antipsychotics to prevent delirium (SIGN: insufficient evidence).
# Pitfalls
Missing hypoactive delirium or labelling it as depression or dementia.
Sedating with benzodiazepines, which prolongs and worsens delirium (except in alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal).
Giving haloperidol to someone with Parkinson's disease or Lewy body dementia.
Blaming a positive urine dipstick and missing the real cause.
Physical restraint and urinary catheters, which make delirium worse.
# Drugs
Haloperidol
# Dose
0.5 to 1 mg orally, or 0.5 mg IM; maximum 2 mg in 24 hours in older people
Only for severe distress or risk. ECG first; contraindicated with Parkinson's disease, Lewy body dementia and prolonged QTc. Review daily; shortest course.
Risperidone, 0.25 mg orally; maximum 1 mg in 24 hours, Alternative antipsychotic; avoid in Parkinson's disease and Lewy body dementia.
Lorazepam
0.5 to 1 mg orally; maximum 2 mg in 24 hours
Only when antipsychotics are contraindicated (Parkinson's disease, Lewy body dementia) or for alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal. Can worsen delirium; respiratory depression.
