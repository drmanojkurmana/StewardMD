# Pathophysiology
Delirium reflects diffuse rather than focal brain dysfunction; the attentional deficit localizes broadly to brainstem, thalamus, prefrontal cortex and parietal lobes, and most cases arise from widespread cortical and subcortical disturbance rather than a single lesion. Acetylcholine deficiency is thought to be central (explaining the potency of anticholinergic drugs), with additional roles for excess dopamine, proinflammatory mediators and individual susceptibility. The concept is that of an insult acting on a vulnerable, low-reserve brain. For most metabolic causes (hypoglycaemia, hyponatraemia, hyperosmolarity, hypercapnia, hypercalcaemia, hepatic and renal failure) the brain shows little or no structural damage and the effects are largely reversible, attributed to impaired energy supply, altered ion fluxes across neuronal membranes and neurotransmitter abnormalities. In hepatic encephalopathy, raised ammonia is proposed to drive astrocytic glutamine synthesis and osmotic swelling, mitochondrial energy failure, reactive nitrogen/oxygen species, increased inhibitory GABA tone and 'false' neurotransmitters, with chronic disease producing diffuse astrocytosis. Uraemic encephalopathy is multifactorial (urea itself is not the cause): accumulated neurotoxins, catecholamine depletion, altered glutamate/GABA tone, raised brain calcium and blood-brain-barrier disruption. Importantly, the degree of neurologic change in metabolic encephalopathies depends heavily on the rapidity with which the serum derangement develops, not just its absolute level. In sepsis-associated encephalopathy, circulating inflammatory mediators (TNF, IL-1, IL-2, IL-6) appear to cause diffuse cerebral dysfunction that is not simply explained by the metabolic derangements of organ failure.
# Prognosis
Once viewed as benign and transient, delirium is now recognized as a marker of serious illness with substantial morbidity and mortality: in-hospital mortality is roughly 25-33% (similar to sepsis), and delirious patients have about a fivefold higher mortality in the ensuing months versus matched non-delirious inpatients, plus longer stays, more nursing-home discharge, higher readmission and recurrent episodes. Most metabolic and toxic encephalopathies are reversible with no or minimal structural brain damage, and drug/toxin-induced coma typically resolves fully provided hypoxia or severe hypotension has not occurred. However, some episodes persist for weeks to months or longer, delirium can cause lasting cognitive decline, and lingering effects (ranging from amnesia for the episode to PTSD-like re-experiencing) are common. Sepsis-associated encephalopathy severe enough to cause coma carries a mortality approaching 50%, though this mainly reflects severity of the underlying critical illness rather than the encephalopathy itself.
# Severity classification
No formal grading scale is described; severity is gauged clinically by the level of consciousness (a continuum from hyperarousal through lethargy to coma) and by the depth of the attentional deficit (e.g., forward digit span). The EEG correlates with severity, showing progressive generalized background slowing as the encephalopathy deepens.
# Clinical pearls
Delirium is an acute, relatively rapid decline in cognition (hours to days) that characteristically fluctuates; the cardinal neuropsychological deficit is inattention, though memory, executive function, visuospatial skills and language are variably affected.
It is a purely clinical bedside diagnosis. The validated Confusion Assessment Method (CAM) requires (1) acute onset with fluctuating course and (2) inattention, plus either (3) disorganized thinking or (4) altered level of consciousness.
Recognize two ends of a spectrum: a hyperactive subtype (classic example: delirium tremens, with agitation, hallucinations, tremor, autonomic instability) and a hypoactive subtype (e.g., benzodiazepine intoxication, with apathy and psychomotor slowing); hypoactive cases are frequently overlooked, especially on wards and in the ICU.
Bilateral asterixis and multifocal myoclonus on motor exam are non-focal signs that, while nonspecific, usually point to a metabolic or toxic encephalopathy / drug intoxication rather than a structural lesion.
A simple bedside test of attention is forward digit span: healthy adults manage 5-7 digits, while a span of 4 or fewer suggests an attentional deficit; many delirious patients can repeat only 3 or fewer.
Worsening in the evening (sundowning) is typical but not required; because the course fluctuates, a single morning assessment can be falsely reassuring and serial examinations are needed.
About one-third of delirium cases are drug-related, especially agents with anticholinergic or sedative properties, narcotics and benzodiazepines; nearly any drug can precipitate delirium in a predisposed patient.
Delirium can act as a 'stress test for the brain' - a minor insult (UTI, elective surgery, hospitalization) that unmasks delirium in a previously well person should prompt later outpatient screening for an unrecognized underlying neurodegenerative or cerebrovascular disorder.
The MMSE is unreliable in delirium because tasks such as spelling 'world' backward and serial subtraction are corrupted by the underlying attentional deficit.
# Additional differentials
Dementia and pre-existing cognitive impairment - the major predisposing condition; at least two-thirds of delirium occurs in patients with underlying dementia, and the two coexist and overlap.
Dementia with Lewy bodies - fluctuating course, prominent visual hallucinations and parkinsonism can closely mimic hyperactive delirium.
Nonconvulsive status epilepticus and recurrent seizures with prolonged postictal confusion.
Focal stroke or haemorrhage in nondominant (right) parietal lobe or medial dorsal thalamus - rare structural cause of an acute confusional state.
Hypertensive encephalopathy / posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome (PRES) and eclampsia.
Diffuse neoplastic disease - multiple brain metastases, diffuse glioma, CNS lymphoma, carcinomatous meningitis.
Postoperative encephalopathy from a shower of microemboli, particularly after cardiac/cardiopulmonary bypass surgery.
Acute primary psychiatric disturbance, depression and catatonia - must be distinguished, particularly from the quiet hypoactive subtype.
# Infection mimics
Systemic infections - urinary tract infection, pneumonia and other respiratory infections, skin/soft tissue infection and frank sepsis; a UTI precipitating acute confusion in an elderly patient with baseline dementia is a classic scenario.
CNS infections - bacterial meningitis, encephalitis (including herpes simplex) and brain abscess; less common causes but high morbidity/mortality if missed.
Sepsis-associated ('septic') encephalopathy - diffuse cytokine-mediated brain dysfunction occurring in the majority of patients with sepsis and multiorgan failure.
Severe systemic infections causing coma - septicaemia, typhoid fever, malaria (including cerebral malaria), Waterhouse-Friderichsen syndrome.
# Non infectious mimics
Anticholinergic, opioid, sedative-hypnotic and other prescription drug toxicity, and drugs of abuse (alcohol, opiates, MDMA/ecstasy, GHB, ketamine/PCP, cocaine, 'bath salts', synthetic cannabinoids).
Alcohol and benzodiazepine withdrawal - to be considered in all delirium including the elderly, since even modest daily drinkers can withdraw on admission.
Poison/toxin exposure - carbon monoxide, ethylene glycol, inhalants, pesticides.
Endocrinopathies - hyper- or hypothyroidism, hyperparathyroidism, adrenal insufficiency / Addisonian crisis.
Hypothermia and hyperthermia.
Global cerebral hypoperfusion from hypotension - heart failure, septic shock, severe dehydration or anaemia.
Autoimmune and paraneoplastic disorders - CNS vasculitis, cerebral lupus, autoimmune encephalitis (anti-NMDA-receptor encephalitis shows a characteristic 'extreme delta brush' EEG).
Sedative drug overdose mimicking structural brainstem disease or even brain death (e.g., barbiturates) - toxic causes must be excluded before diagnosing brain death.
# Additional investigations
Complete blood count and full electrolyte panel including magnesium and phosphorus.
Serum/urine toxicology screen - performed earlier in younger patients.
TSH and free T4, and serum cortisol for suspected endocrinopathy.
Vitamin levels - B12, folate, thiamine.
EEG - essential when seizures are suspected or when no cause is found after initial workup; shows nonspecific diffuse symmetric slowing in encephalopathy, frontal high-voltage slow or triphasic waves in metabolic (e.g., hepatic) coma, and can reveal nonconvulsive status epilepticus.
Brain imaging - MRI with diffusion and gadolinium is the preferred study (CT often used first for speed/availability) to exclude structural causes; note imaging is frequently unrevealing in pure metabolic delirium.
Lumbar puncture (after neuroimaging) when CNS infection, autoimmune, inflammatory or neoplastic disease is suspected, or in any delirium with a negative workup.
Inflammatory and autoimmune serologies - ESR, ANA, complement, p-/c-ANCA, and paraneoplastic/autoimmune encephalitis panels in serum and CSF when indicated.
ECG and infectious serologies (RPR, HIV; fungal/viral if high suspicion) as second-tier tests.
# Red flags
New focal neurologic deficit, meningismus, fever or papilloedema - point away from a simple metabolic cause toward a structural, infectious or raised-ICP process needing urgent imaging/LP.
Suspected nonconvulsive status epilepticus - if suspicion is high, EEG should be obtained immediately as it is treatable and easily missed in a fluctuating patient.
Sodium <125 mmol/L (especially if acute) causing confusion, and <119 mmol/L acutely associated with coma and convulsions.
In-hospital delirium carries a 25-33% mortality (comparable to sepsis) and a roughly fivefold higher mortality in following months - it signals serious underlying illness, not a benign transient state.
Delirium in the ICU should be regarded as organ dysfunction on a par with liver, kidney or heart failure, and is associated with worse outcomes.
# Pitfalls
Treating the symptoms of delirium pharmacologically while neglecting the underlying cause merely prolongs the confused state and can mask important diagnostic clues.
Missing nonconvulsive status epilepticus - its fluctuating presentation mimics ordinary delirium, and only EEG will reveal it.
Diagnosing delirium without knowledge of the patient's cognitive baseline, so that dementia or long-standing depression is mistaken for an acute confusional state - collateral history is essential.
Overlooking the quiet hypoactive subtype, which is commonly missed and may be wrongly attributed to depression or sedation.
Attributing coma to incidental imaging findings (small subdural, old stroke) when the true cause is intoxication; a normal CT does not exclude an anatomic lesion, and most coma/confusion is metabolic or toxic in origin.
Forgetting that the severity of metabolic encephalopathy depends on how rapidly the derangement developed, so a 'mildly' abnormal value reached acutely can produce marked impairment.
Diagnosing brain death without first excluding sedative/toxic causes (e.g., barbiturates) that can mimic its findings.
# Metabolic encephalopathy: treatment
Diffuse alteration of consciousness without focal deficit; asterixis, myoclonus. Identify the metabolic cause, Admit; ICU if airway risk.
Correct the metabolic cause + supportive care
# Steps
Find and correct the cause — glucose, Na, Ca, uraemia, hypercapnia, ammonia, drugs, hypoxia, sepsis.
Support airway, oxygenation and circulation.
Empirical thiamine in at-risk patients; naloxone if opioid suspected.
Treat the underlying organ failure; review/stop offending drugs.
Monitor neurological status.
