# Dementia: assessment and management
Adults
Primary care, memory clinic, hospital ward
Take a history from the person and an informant, use a brief cognitive test, exclude delirium, depression, sensory loss, anticholinergic drugs and metabolic causes, image to exclude structural causes, offer a cholinesterase inhibitor or memantine by subtype, and use antipsychotics only for severe distress or risk of harm.
Aliases: Alzheimer's disease; AD; memory loss; cognitive impairment; vascular dementia; Lewy body dementia; DLB; frontotemporal dementia; BPSD; behavioural and psychological symptoms of dementia.
# Suspect and assess
Suspect dementia with progressive decline in memory or other cognitive domains that affects daily function. Take a history from the person and from someone who knows them well (IQCODE or FAQ can help).
Use a validated brief cognitive test (for example Mini-Cog, 6CIT, Memory Impairment Screen, 10-point cognitive screener); a normal score does not rule out dementia.
Exclude delirium first: acute onset, fluctuation and inattention suggest delirium (use the 4AT). If unsure, treat as delirium.
Rapidly progressive dementia (weeks to months): refer urgently to neurology for MRI and CSF tests (Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease, autoimmune encephalitis).
Subtype clues: Alzheimer's (early episodic memory loss); vascular (stepwise course, focal signs, vascular risk); Lewy body (fluctuation, visual hallucinations, parkinsonism, REM sleep behaviour disorder); frontotemporal (early behaviour or language change).
# Look for reversible causes
Bloods: CBC, renal and liver function, calcium, glucose or HbA1c, thyroid function, vitamin B12 and folate; syphilis and HIV serology if there are risk factors.
Review medicines for anticholinergic burden (oxybutynin, tricyclics, chlorphenamine, promethazine and others), benzodiazepines, opioids and sedatives; reduce or replace where possible.
Check for depression, hearing and visual impairment, alcohol misuse and sleep apnoea.
Structural imaging (MRI, or CT if MRI is unavailable) to exclude tumour, subdural haematoma, normal pressure hydrocephalus and stroke and to help subtype, unless dementia is established and the subtype is clear.
Further tests (FDG-PET or perfusion SPECT, CSF biomarkers, DaTscan for suspected Lewy body dementia) only if the subtype is uncertain and knowing it would change management.
# Drug treatment by subtype (NICE NG97)
Mild to moderate Alzheimer's disease: donepezil, galantamine or rivastigmine (start with the lowest-cost option unless another suits better).
Memantine: moderate Alzheimer's if cholinesterase inhibitors are not tolerated or contraindicated, or severe Alzheimer's. Already on a cholinesterase inhibitor: consider adding memantine in moderate and offer it in severe disease.
Lewy body dementia: offer donepezil or rivastigmine. Parkinson's disease dementia: offer a cholinesterase inhibitor. Vascular dementia: only if mixed with Alzheimer's or Lewy body disease. Frontotemporal dementia: do not use these drugs.
Do not stop cholinesterase inhibitors because of disease severity alone. Check pulse, and ECG if there is bradycardia, syncope, heart block or rate-slowing drugs, before starting.
Anti-amyloid antibodies (lecanemab, donanemab) are licensed in some countries for early Alzheimer's with confirmed amyloid and need MRI monitoring for ARIA; NICE has not recommended them for routine NHS use.
Offer group cognitive stimulation therapy and consider cognitive rehabilitation or occupational therapy in mild to moderate dementia. Do not offer ginseng, vitamin E, herbal formulations or acupuncture to treat dementia.
# Distress, agitation and psychosis
First do a structured assessment for causes: pain, delirium, infection, constipation, urinary retention, hunger or thirst, sensory loss, the environment or the way care is given. Use personalised activities and psychosocial measures first.
Offer an antipsychotic only if the person is at risk of harming themselves or others, or has agitation, hallucinations or delusions causing severe distress. Discuss the increased risk of stroke and death.
If used: lowest dose for the shortest time (risperidone is licensed for up to 6 weeks for persistent aggression in Alzheimer's); reassess at least every 6 weeks and stop if there is no clear benefit.
Lewy body and Parkinson's disease dementia: antipsychotics can worsen motor symptoms and cause severe sensitivity reactions; avoid haloperidol and seek specialist advice.
Do not use valproate for agitation. Do not routinely use antidepressants for mild to moderate depression in mild to moderate dementia; consider psychological treatment. Do not use melatonin for insomnia in Alzheimer's.
# Ongoing care and support
Give a named care coordinator, information and carer support. Discuss driving (inform the licensing authority), work, finances and legal planning (for example power of attorney) early.
Start advance care planning while the person has capacity, and offer flexible, needs-based palliative care from diagnosis.
Hospital admission raises the risk of delirium, falls and prolonged stay: weigh benefits and harms, and keep familiar people and routines.
Assess pain with an observational tool when self-report is unreliable; manage falls, continence, nutrition and swallowing; eye test every 2 years.
India: most people with dementia are cared for at home by family, so involve and support carers early; consider B12 deficiency (common in vegetarians), hypothyroidism, neurosyphilis and HIV as treatable contributors.
# Risk reduction
Lancet Commission 2024: about 45% of dementia is potentially preventable by addressing 14 risk factors across life.
Treat hypertension, diabetes and high LDL cholesterol; support stopping smoking, weight control, physical activity and limiting alcohol.
Treat depression, offer hearing aids for hearing loss and correct vision loss; reduce social isolation; prevent head injury; reduce exposure to air pollution.
Avoid long-term anticholinergic and sedative drugs in older adults.
# Pitfalls
Diagnosing dementia during delirium or untreated depression.
Missing Lewy body dementia and giving haloperidol or another typical antipsychotic.
Starting a cholinesterase inhibitor in someone with bradycardia or syncope without an ECG.
Continuing antipsychotics for months without review.
Dismissing memory problems in younger people (consider frontotemporal dementia, alcohol, HIV and autoimmune causes).
# Drugs
Donepezil
# Dose
5 mg orally once daily at night for at least 1 month, then 10 mg once daily if tolerated (max 10 mg)
Nausea, diarrhoea, vivid dreams (give in the morning), bradycardia, syncope.
Rivastigmine (transdermal patch)
# Dose
4.6 mg/24 h, increase after at least 4 weeks to 9.5 mg/24 h; may increase to 13.3 mg/24 h after a further 4 weeks
Useful in Parkinson's disease dementia and Lewy body dementia. Re-titrate from 4.6 mg/24 h if more than 3 days are missed. Oral capsules also exist.
Galantamine (modified-release)
# Dose
8 mg orally once daily for 4 weeks, then 16 mg once daily; max 24 mg daily after at least 4 weeks at 16 mg
Moderate hepatic or renal impairment: max 16 mg daily. Take with food.
Memantine
# Dose
5 mg orally once daily, increase by 5 mg each week to 20 mg once daily
CrCl 30 to 49 mL/min: 10 mg daily, may increase to 20 mg after 7 days if tolerated; CrCl 5 to 29 mL/min: 10 mg daily. Dizziness, headache, constipation.
Risperidone
# Dose
0.25 mg orally twice daily; adjust by 0.25 mg twice daily no more often than every other day; usual 0.5 mg twice daily, max 1 mg twice daily; no longer than 6 weeks
Only for persistent aggression with risk of harm in moderate to severe Alzheimer's. Increased stroke and mortality risk; avoid in Lewy body dementia.
