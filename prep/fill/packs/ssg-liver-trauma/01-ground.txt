The American Association for the Surgery of Trauma grades liver injury from grade I capsular tears and subcapsular haematoma under 10 percent surface through deeper lacerations to grade VI hepatic avulsion, with grade III marking major injury once parenchymal depth exceeds 3 cm and vascular damage accumulates.

Stable patients stay out of theatre. Haemodynamically stable blunt injury without peritonitis gets nonoperative management with bed rest, serial haemoglobin and computed tomography follow-up, succeeding in over 85 percent of cases across modern trauma centres.

A blush changes the plan only with instability. Contrast blush with haemodynamic instability goes to angioembolisation of the bleeding branch, while a blush in a stable patient without peritonitis can be watched with rescanning and transfusion thresholds.

Packing saves the dying. Major bleeding in damage control surgery gets perihepatic packing with laparotomy pads compressing the liver against the diaphragm, with relook and pack removal at 24 to 48 hours once coagulopathy, cold and acidosis are corrected.

Veins kill faster than arteries. Juxtahepatic venous injury with retrohepatic caval bleeding needs atriocaval shunting or total vascular exclusion to isolate the segment, carrying mortality over 50 percent even in expert hands.

Delayed bleeding has a signature. Delayed haemobilia with melena and right upper quadrant pain days after trauma comes from an arteriobiliary fistula, and is diagnosed and treated by hepatic angiography with embolisation of the culprit branch.

Bile finds its own path. Bile leak and biloma after trauma get endoscopic sphincterotomy with stenting to lower ductal pressure and encourage internal drainage, while major ductal disruption needs hepaticojejunostomy reconstruction.

Penetrating wounds go straight in. Penetrating injury with peritonitis or evisceration gets immediate laparotomy, and tractotomy with selective vessel ligation beats formal lobectomy for through-and-through wounds, preserving maximum functioning liver.
