Exam grounding notes for digital replantation, written for grounding, not for display. Paraphrased from Plastic Surgery 5E Volume 6 on replantation.

Replantation is absolute for thumb amputations at any level, for multiple digits, for transmetacarpal losses, for any amputation in a paediatric patient, and for wrist or forearm amputations. Function gained outweighs any operative burden in these patterns.

Relative contraindications temper enthusiasm: severe crush or avulsion with mangled vessels, multiple-level amputations, severe psychiatric illness that threatens rehabilitation, and prolonged warm ischaemia beyond 6 hours in proximal muscle-containing parts. Each factor lowers the odds of a useful hand.

Time limits differ by tissue. A digit on cold storage tolerates up to 24 hours of ischaemia because it carries no muscle. Warm ischaemia in a proximal muscle-containing part must not exceed 6 hours before muscle dies and reperfusion turns toxic.

Field preservation decides viability. The part is wrapped in saline-moistened sterile gauze, sealed inside a waterproof plastic bag, and floated in an ice-water slurry. Direct contact with ice freezes tissue and must be prevented.

Theatre follows a fixed sequence: bone shortening with rigid skeletal fixation, extensor tendon repair, flexor tendon repair, digital arterial anastomosis, digital nerve repair, and finally venous anastomosis. Bone first gives a stable platform for the delicate joins.

Outflow needs redundancy. A vein-to-artery ratio of 2 to 1, meaning two veins repaired for every one artery, provides optimal venous outflow and minimises postoperative congestion. One vein alone invites failure.

When venous repair proves impossible, Hirudo medicinalis leeches salvage the congested digit. The medicinal leech secretes hirudin, a potent thrombin inhibitor, while draining blood. Ciprofloxacin prophylaxis covers Aeromonas hydrophila from the leech gut.

Tamai zones map the fingertip. Zone I lies distal to the lunula base of the nail bed, and Zone II spans lunula to DIP joint. Zone I repairs demand supermicrosurgery on submillimetre vessels and survive less often than more proximal work.
