# Upper Respiratory Tract Infection (common cold)
Upper Respiratory Tract Infection (common cold), Respiratory.
# Pathogens
URTI is almost universally viral. Bacterial superinfection (sinusitis, otitis media) is uncommon and is a separate diagnosis to be made on its own merits if symptoms evolve.
Very likely: Rhinovirus; Coronavirus (non-SARS-CoV-2 strains); Influenza virus; Adenovirus.
# Investigations
None required, Diagnosis is clinical and investigations rarely change management in uncomplicated URTI.
Antibiotics NOT indicated
# Reason
Clinical findings (coryza, nasal congestion, mild sore throat, absence of high fever or focal findings) are consistent with a viral upper respiratory tract infection. This is a self-limiting condition and antibiotics provide no benefit.
# Pathophysiology
Nonspecific URI is an acute infection of the respiratory tract above the vocal cords, most commonly caused by respiratory viruses acquired through direct contact with infected people, contaminated surfaces, and large and small respiratory droplets. Susceptibility depends on prior exposure and immunity, general health, genetics, microbiome factors, and psychosocial factors including stress. Because immunity is serotype-specific (rhinovirus, adenovirus) or weak/short-lived (coronaviruses, parainfluenza, RSV, metapneumovirus), reinfection is frequent.
# Prognosis
Nonspecific URI is self-limited. Symptoms peak early and generally resolve within 5-10 days, though some last up to 14 days. Although the cold can occasionally be complicated by otitis media or bacterial sinusitis, for any individual patient an antibiotic is more likely to cause an adverse reaction than to prevent a complication.
# Clinical pearls
The nonspecific URI ('common cold') is defined as a respiratory tract infection in which no single symptom predominates, distinguishing it clinically from more localized syndromes such as pharyngitis, sinusitis, or otitis media.
The overwhelming majority of acute respiratory infections are caused by common respiratory viruses and are self-limited; antibiotics neither speed resolution nor prevent complications, which is why at least half of ambulatory antibiotic prescriptions for these infections are inappropriate.
Nasal mucus in a viral URI can range anywhere from watery to frankly purulent, so purulent or discolored nasal discharge by itself does not indicate bacterial infection or warrant antibiotics.
Symptoms typically begin gradually after a 2-8 day incubation, peak early, and usually last 5-10 days but may persist up to 14 days; patients remain infectious as long as symptoms are present.
Adults average 2-4 URIs per year and children 6-10 per year; reinfection is common because immunity to the many rhinovirus and adenovirus serotypes is serotype-specific and immunity to coronaviruses, parainfluenza, RSV, and metapneumovirus is weak or short-lived.
Myalgias may occur but are usually less prominent than in influenza, a useful bedside discriminator from influenza.
Making a specific viral diagnosis is not practical, cost-effective, or necessary; multiplex RT-PCR panels may be overly sensitive and yield false positives from recent prior infection.
URI can be reduced by hand hygiene, physical distancing, masking, isolation of ill persons, and environmental cleaning.
# Additional differentials
Influenza (more prominent myalgias and systemic symptoms)
COVID-19 (SARS-CoV-2 can cause virtually any upper respiratory symptom; new loss of taste or smell appears specific for COVID-19)
Measles (cough, coryza, and conjunctivitis)
Acute HIV infection (sore throat and rash often predominate)
Allergic, inflammatory, autoimmune, or neoplastic causes of upper respiratory symptoms
Infection mimics: Influenza; COVID-19 (SARS-CoV-2); Acute HIV infection; Measles; Streptococcal pharyngitis; Acute bacterial sinusitis; Acute otitis media.
# Non infectious mimics
Allergic rhinitis / allergic upper respiratory symptoms
Other inflammatory upper respiratory conditions
Autoimmune causes of upper respiratory symptoms
Neoplastic causes of upper respiratory symptoms
# Additional investigations
No specific viral testing is needed for diagnosis; multiplex RT-PCR panels exist but are often unnecessary and may be falsely positive due to recent prior infection
# Red flags
Fever above 102 degrees F (38.9 C)
Chest pain not attributable to a pulled muscle
Shortness of breath
Dizziness
Confusion
New ear or sinus pain (suggesting otitis media or bacterial sinusitis complication)
Symptoms lasting longer than 14 days
New loss of taste or smell where SARS-CoV-2 is circulating (suggests COVID-19)
# Pitfalls
Prescribing antibiotics for a viral URI: they do not shorten illness or prevent complications and risk adverse drug effects, microbiome disruption, C. difficile infection, increased cost, and antibiotic resistance.
Interpreting purulent nasal discharge as evidence of bacterial infection; viral URI mucus normally ranges from watery to purulent.
Drivers of inappropriate prescribing include time pressure, fear of missing a rare bacterial diagnosis, concern about a rare complication, low salience of antibiotic harms, and the mistaken belief that patients demand antibiotics.
Delayed antibiotic prescriptions are conceptually flawed for self-limited respiratory infections, shift decision-making to patients, and send a mixed message; they should be avoided.
Procalcitonin testing has been ineffective and CRP testing not durable for reducing inappropriate ambulatory antibiotic use.
Many popular cold remedies are ineffective, of questionable benefit, or harmful, including echinacea, zinc, inhaled steam, vitamin C, vitamin D, garlic, antihistamines, intranasal corticosteroids, saline nasal irrigation, and antiviral drugs.
Cough and cold medicines should not be used in children under 6 years because of the risk of adverse effects.
# Stewardship
Not applicable — no antibiotics started.
# Framework
Viral syndrome — antibiotics are not indicated regardless of symptom duration up to 10 days.
Educate patient that colored nasal discharge does not indicate bacterial infection.
Reassess only if symptoms worsen after initial improvement (suggesting secondary bacterial sinusitis) or persist beyond 10 days.
Antibiotics NOT indicated
