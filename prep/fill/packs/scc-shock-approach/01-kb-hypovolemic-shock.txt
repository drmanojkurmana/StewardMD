# Hypovolemic / haemorrhagic shock
Hypovolemic / haemorrhagic shock, Critical care.
Red flags: GI bleed / occult haemorrhage.
# Investigations
Identify bleeding source
Crossmatch
Resuscitate
# Pathophysiology
Hypovolemic shock arises when a fall in intravascular volume reduces preload, lowering stroke volume and cardiac output and therefore systemic oxygen delivery; CVP and PCWP are low and systemic vascular resistance rises as a compensatory response. Inadequate oxygen delivery forces cells into anaerobic metabolism, so pyruvate is converted to lactate with markedly less ATP generation; failure of ATP-dependent ion pumps disrupts ionic and pH homeostasis, and calcium influx activates phospholipases/proteases causing cell swelling and death, with leaked intracellular contents driving inflammation and microvascular injury. In acute haemorrhage, hypovolaemia dominates first and threatens high-flow organs (brain, kidneys); the haemoglobin is initially normal and only falls over hours as fluid shifts from the extravascular to the intravascular space (haemodilution), so the haematocrit does not reflect the volume lost early on.
# Prognosis
Hypovolemic shock carries a lower mortality than septic or cardiogenic shock, which have the highest death rates among shock types. Organ dysfunction in early (compensated) shock is often reversible if perfusion and oxygen delivery are restored, but untreated shock progresses through a decompensated phase to an irreversible phase with permanent multisystem organ dysfunction and death.
# Severity classification
The shock index (HR/SBP; normal 0.5-0.7) is a bedside severity marker, with values >0.9 indicating critical bleeding and likely transfusion need.
# Clinical pearls
Hypovolemic shock is haemodynamically a low-output state: reduced cardiac output and oxygen delivery driven by low preload, with a compensatory rise in systemic vascular resistance and low CVP and PCWP from depleted intravascular volume.
Divide causes into haemorrhagic (external/trauma or internal, most often upper or lower GI bleeding) and non-haemorrhagic fluid loss (profuse vomiting/diarrhoea; renal losses such as osmotic diuresis in DKA or diabetes insipidus; skin losses from severe burns or Stevens-Johnson syndrome).
On examination it presents as a cold, low-output shock: cool/clammy peripheries, narrow pulse pressure, delayed capillary refill and a reduced JVP (<8 cm) - the heart is 'empty' - in contrast to the warm peripheries and wide pulse pressure of distributive shock.
Tachycardia and tachypnoea are early compensatory signs, but hypotension may be a late finding; younger patients in particular can compensate for an extended period before blood pressure falls.
Signs of vascular instability appear once roughly 25% of blood volume is lost; the usual sympathetic compensation (tachycardia, vasoconstriction, regional flow shift) fails beyond ~30% loss, and loss of >40% (>2 L in an average adult) produces overt shock (confusion, dyspnoea, diaphoresis, hypotension, tachycardia).
Among hypovolemic causes, haemorrhage is the most common; non-haemorrhagic GI, renal and skin fluid losses are also recognised causes.
# Additional differentials
Distributive shock (e.g. sepsis, anaphylaxis, neurogenic, adrenal crisis) - high cardiac output, low SVR, warm peripheries
Cardiogenic shock - low output but with elevated CVP/PCWP and raised JVP
Obstructive shock (tension pneumothorax, cardiac tamponade, pulmonary embolism, aortic dissection) - low output with raised filling pressures/JVP
Mixed shock - more than one mechanism coexisting (e.g. hypovolemic plus septic)
# Non infectious mimics
Cardiogenic shock - shares cool peripheries and narrow pulse pressure (low output) but is distinguished by raised JVP, peripheral oedema, an S3/S4 gallop and pulmonary crackles, indicating high filling pressures rather than volume depletion
Obstructive shock - low-output state but typically with an elevated JVP; tamponade (pulsus paradoxus, raised JVP, low-voltage ECG with electrical alternans) and tension pneumothorax (absent breath sounds, tracheal deviation) mimic the hypoperfusion of hypovolemia
Distributive shock unmasked after fluid loss (e.g. adrenal insufficiency with aldosterone-deficient hypovolaemia)
# Additional investigations
Serial serum lactate for diagnosis, risk stratification and to gauge response to resuscitation - elevated lactate/lactic acidosis correlates with worse outcome
Haemoglobin/haematocrit - reduced in haemorrhagic loss, but may be normal early in active bleeding before haemodilution occurs
Coagulation profile (PT, PTT, INR) as part of the undifferentiated shock workup, relevant before/around transfusion
Arterial or venous blood gas to quantify the degree of metabolic acidosis
Urine electrolytes with FENa/FEUrea, which may indicate hypovolaemia or reduced effective circulating volume
Point-of-care ultrasound (RUSH/ACES/SESAME protocols): a collapsible IVC at end-expiration in a spontaneously breathing patient and focused views of abdomen/pelvis suggest reduced intravascular volume and can identify intra-abdominal haemorrhage
Dynamic fluid-responsiveness assessment - passive leg raise, or >12% stroke-volume/pulse-pressure variation in suitable mechanically ventilated patients - to predict response to volume
Pregnancy test (to detect ruptured ectopic pregnancy as a haemorrhagic source)
Localising signs of occult internal bleed: Grey Turner sign (flank ecchymosis) and Cullen sign (umbilical ecchymosis) suggest retro-/intraperitoneal haemorrhage; rectal exam may reveal GI bleeding
# Red flags
Overt features of severe blood-volume loss (>40%): confusion, dyspnoea, diaphoresis, hypotension and tachycardia indicating critical vital-organ hypoperfusion needing immediate volume replacement
New altered mental status or new oliguria (<0.5 mL/kg/h) - signs the compensatory mechanisms have been overwhelmed (decompensated shock)
Occult bleeding sites - retroperitoneal haemorrhage, long-bone fracture haematoma, intra-abdominal/intrapleural bleeding - that may not be externally apparent
Elevated shock index (HR/SBP >0.9) flagging critical bleeding and likely transfusion need in haemorrhagic shock
# Pitfalls
Compensated shock can present with a normal blood pressure: relying on an absolute SBP/MAP threshold misses early shock, since young patients may maintain pressure with tachycardia, narrow pulse pressure and only a mildly raised lactate before decompensating.
Do not be falsely reassured by tachycardia without hypotension, nor exclude shock because the heart rate is normal - patients on nodal-blocking drugs or with conduction disease may have a blunted or absent tachycardic response.
A normal initial haemoglobin/haematocrit does NOT exclude significant active haemorrhage; values fall only later as haemodilution develops, and the haematocrit does not reflect the true volume lost early on.
Misclassifying low-output shock: an elevated JVP, peripheral oedema, gallop or crackles point to cardiogenic/obstructive rather than hypovolemic shock and warn against indiscriminate large-volume resuscitation.
Over-resuscitation is harmful - targeted, reassessed fluid therapy is the goal rather than maximal volume.
Estimates of external blood loss at a bloody scene are often overestimated, while large internal bleeds may be clinically inapparent - examine carefully for localising signs.
# Hypovolemic / haemorrhagic shock: treatment
Hypotension, tachycardia, cool peripheries, low JVP with a source of fluid/blood loss, Resuscitation area → ICU/theatre.
Resuscitate with balanced crystalloid / blood + control the source
# Steps
Two large-bore IV lines; rapid balanced crystalloid resuscitation.
Haemorrhagic: activate major haemorrhage protocol — transfuse blood/products, tranexamic acid early.
Identify and control the source (surgery, endoscopy, IR, compression).
Titrate to perfusion (MAP, urine output, lactate clearance) — avoid over-resuscitation in active bleeding.
Keep warm; correct coagulopathy and calcium.
Antifibrinolytic + correct coagulopathy/calcium
Resuscitate + control haemorrhage

FBC, cross-match, coagulation, lactate
Source imaging (CT/endoscopy)
