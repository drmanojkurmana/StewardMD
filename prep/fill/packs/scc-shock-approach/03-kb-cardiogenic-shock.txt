# Cardiogenic shock
Cardiogenic shock, Cardiology / Critical care.
Red flags: Revascularisation/inotropes — not antibiotics.
# Investigations
ECG, troponin
Echocardiogram
Lactate
# Pathophysiology
A profound fall in myocardial contractility initiates a self-perpetuating spiral: reduced cardiac output and stroke volume lower blood pressure and coronary perfusion, worsening ischaemia and further depressing contractility, which left untreated leads to death. Ineffective stroke volume is the inciting event, but inadequate circulatory compensation also contributes. Early peripheral vasoconstriction may transiently improve coronary and peripheral perfusion but raises afterload and can worsen ischaemia. Over time, systemic inflammation from acute cardiac injury induces pathologic vasodilatation; inflammatory cytokines and inducible/endothelial nitric oxide synthase increase NO and its by-product peroxynitrite, which are negatively inotropic and cardiotoxic. Lactic acidosis and hypoxaemia compound the cycle because severe acidosis blunts the efficacy of endogenous and exogenous catecholamines. Bleeding and transfusion during ICU care can further trigger inflammation. Left-ventricular failure accounts for roughly 80% of cardiogenic shock complicating acute MI, with acute severe mitral regurgitation, ventricular septal rupture, predominant RV failure, and free-wall rupture/tamponade making up the remainder.
# Prognosis
In-hospital mortality ranges from roughly 40-60% depending on shock severity and underlying cause; cardiogenic and septic shock carry the highest mortality among shock types (90-day mortality around 52% for cardiogenic shock in one ED cohort). Higher lactate, elevated admission glucose, hepatic hypoperfusion and progressive renal impairment are adverse markers. Untreated shock progresses through compensated (preshock), decompensated, and finally irreversible stages with permanent organ dysfunction and multi-organ failure. Reperfusion and modern care have reduced the incidence of shock complicating acute MI to about 5-10%.
# Severity classification
SCAI shock classification (2019, updated): Stage A 'at risk' — no current signs/symptoms; Stage B 'beginning'/preshock — relative hypotension or tachycardia without hypoperfusion; Stage C 'classic' — manifest hypoperfusion requiring intervention (inotropes, vasopressors or MCS, excluding ECMO) beyond volume; Stage D 'deteriorating' — like C but worsening and failing initial therapy; Stage E 'extremis' — cardiac arrest with ongoing CPR and/or ECMO. The updated definition adds a three-axis model: (1) shock severity, (2) phenotype/aetiology, (3) risk modifiers such as cardiac arrest. Lactate >8 mmol/L marks stage E.
# Clinical pearls
Defined haemodynamically as a low cardiac-output state producing life-threatening end-organ hypoperfusion: persistent SBP <90 mmHg (or MAP <60-65 mmHg) unresponsive to volume and/or requiring vasopressors, plus hypoperfusion features such as lactate >2 mmol/L.
Objective parameters (cardiac index, pulmonary capillary wedge pressure) help confirm a cardiac cause but are not mandatory for the diagnosis.
Compensatory rise in systemic vascular resistance is typical; left-ventricular processes elevate PCWP whereas right-ventricular processes elevate CVP.
Occasionally BP is maintained by very high SVR despite established shock, so normotension does not exclude cardiogenic shock.
Only about 10% of patients present with shock on admission; roughly 90% develop it during hospitalisation, so serial reassessment is essential.
Shock complicating a first inferior MI should prompt a search for a mechanical complication or right-ventricular involvement.
A new systolic murmur after MI should trigger urgent echocardiography to look for VSR, papillary muscle rupture or free-wall rupture; a murmur may be absent in up to 50% of acute MR cases.
Pericardial thrombus on imaging after MI strongly suggests myocardial rupture and is a surgical emergency.
Patients who develop shock typically have severe multivessel disease with 'piecemeal' necrosis extending beyond the original infarct zone.
# Additional differentials
Acute fulminant myocarditis
End-stage / decompensated cardiomyopathy
Takotsubo (apical ballooning) syndrome as a transient cause
Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with severe LV outflow obstruction
Critical valvular disease (critical aortic/mitral stenosis; acute severe aortic or mitral regurgitation)
Aortic dissection with aortic insufficiency or tamponade
Refractory sustained tachyarrhythmia or bradyarrhythmia
Right-ventricular failure from acute MI or acute/decompensated cor pulmonale
Post-cardiac-arrest myocardial stunning; postcardiotomy shock
# Infection mimics
Septic shock with myocardial depression (sepsis-induced cardiomyopathy), which can also coexist as a mixed shock state
# Non infectious mimics
Hypovolaemic shock (low CVP and PCWP, elevated SVR) — distinguished by low filling pressures
Obstructive shock from cardiac tamponade, tension pneumothorax, massive pulmonary embolism or aortic dissection
Distributive/anaphylactic or neurogenic shock with low SVR
Negative-inotrope or vasodilator drug overdose (beta-blocker, calcium-channel antagonist) and toxic/metabolic causes (pheochromocytoma, scorpion venom)
# Additional investigations
Arterial blood gas (typically hypoxaemia with anion-gap metabolic acidosis) and serial arterial lactate
Chest radiograph (pulmonary vascular congestion/oedema; normal in up to a third; enlarged heart suggests prior MI)
ECG looking for multilead Q waves/ST elevation, LBBB; ST elevation in aVR with diffuse ST depression suggests left main / global ischaemia
Echocardiography to define mechanism (LV/RV/biventricular, VSR, acute MR, tamponade, LVOT obstruction, valve lesions, aortic dissection)
Right-sided ECG leads (e.g. V4R) for suspected RV infarction
Pulmonary artery catheterisation in diagnostic/management uncertainty or severe unresponsive shock; equalised diastolic pressures suggest tamponade
Coronary angiography immediately in all cardiogenic shock complicating MI to guide reperfusion
Renal function (creatinine), hepatic transaminases (elevated in ~20%, marker of high mortality), admission glucose, WBC and CRP
# Red flags
Lactate >8 mmol/L (places patient in SCAI stage E)
Cardiac arrest with ongoing CPR and/or ECMO (extremis stage)
Rising hepatic transaminases from liver hypoperfusion (~20%; high-mortality marker)
New systolic murmur, thrill or sudden deterioration suggesting mechanical complication (VSR, papillary rupture, free-wall rupture)
Progressive oliguria/anuria and altered mental status indicating worsening hypoperfusion
Failure to respond to initial interventions (SCAI stage D, 'deteriorating')
# Pitfalls
Assuming normotension excludes shock — BP may be preserved by markedly elevated SVR.
Missing a mechanical complication: a murmur is absent in up to 50% of acute papillary-muscle-rupture MR.
Overlooking RV infarction in inferior MI, where fluid management differs (volume to maintain RV preload).
Treating presumed cardiogenic shock as hypovolaemia — filling pressures (PCWP/CVP) are elevated, not low.
Failing to recognise mixed shock (e.g. sepsis-induced cardiomyopathy superimposed on distributive shock).
# Confirm
Hypotension with hypoperfusion despite adequate filling, due to pump failure (often after large MI). Raised lactate, low cardiac output, congestion.
ICU / cardiology.
Vasopressor + inotrope, cautious diuresis; treat the cause
# Steps
ICU; treat the cause — urgent revascularisation for ischaemic cause.
Inotrope/vasopressor (e.g. noradrenaline ± dobutamine) titrated to perfusion.
Cautious diuresis/fluid guided by filling status; treat arrhythmia.
Consider mechanical support (IABP/Impella/ECMO) in selected cases.
Invasive monitoring; serial lactate, urine output.
ICU — vasopressor/inotrope + treat cause
