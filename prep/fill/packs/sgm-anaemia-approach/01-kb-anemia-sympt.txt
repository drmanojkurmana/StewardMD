# Symptomatic anaemia
Symptomatic anaemia, Hematology.
Red flags: Active bleeding → resuscitate.
# Investigations
CBC, peripheral smear
Iron studies, B12/folate
Identify blood loss
# Pathophysiology
Anemia results from a defect in any of the three requirements for normal red-cell production: renal erythropoietin (EPO) output, a functioning erythroid marrow, and adequate substrate (especially iron). EPO rises logarithmically once haemoglobin falls below ~100-120 g/L, but in chronic kidney disease and chronic inflammation EPO is inappropriately low for the degree of anemia. With adequate EPO and substrate, marrow red-cell output can rise four- to fivefold over 1-2 weeks. Mechanistically anemia is either hyperproductive/regenerative (blood loss or haemolysis, with reticulocytosis) or hypoproliferative (nutritional deficiency, marrow replacement, absent precursors, stem-cell defects, or EPO deficiency). Inflammatory cytokines such as TNF lower EPO and raise hepcidin, which blocks iron absorption and release from stores, producing the anemia of inflammation.
# Prognosis
Anemia in those over 65 is associated with increased risk of death, hospitalization, and frailty. In acute posthemorrhagic anemia, if bleeding stops the marrow response (reticulocytosis, rising EPO) gradually corrects the anemia. Myelodysplasia has a variable course, from years of stability to rapid evolution into acute leukaemia.
# Severity classification
Two complementary classifications. (1) By red-cell size (Wintrobe): microcytic (iron deficiency, thalassaemia, sideroblastic, anemia of inflammation), normocytic (renal disease, marrow invasion, myeloma, aplastic anemia, pure red cell aplasia, endocrinopathies), and macrocytic - subdivided into oval macrocytes (B12/folate deficiency, myelodysplasia, drugs) and round macrocytes (alcohol, liver disease, hypothyroidism, hypoxia, reticulocytosis, smoking, dysproteinemia). (2) By mechanism: hyperproductive (blood loss, haemolysis - reticulocytes high) versus underproductive/hypoproliferative (nutritional deficiency, absent precursors, lack of EPO, marrow replacement, stem-cell defects - reticulocytes low/inappropriate). The absolute reticulocyte count plus MCV drive the diagnostic algorithm. For acute blood loss: vascular instability appears with sudden loss of ~25% of total blood volume, and >30% loss exceeds compensatory mechanisms.
# Clinical pearls
Symptoms and signs of anemia correlate poorly with the actual haemoglobin/haematocrit; clinical severity reflects the rate of onset and the patient's cardiovascular reserve rather than the absolute level.
Slowly developing anemia is remarkably well tolerated because three compensatory mechanisms develop over time: rapid rise in cardiac output (minutes), a rise in 2,3-DPG that lowers haemoglobin oxygen affinity and improves tissue oxygen offloading (hours to days), and an expansion of plasma volume (weeks).
Plasma-volume expansion can occasionally overwhelm the heart and precipitate features of heart failure/oedema in chronic anemia.
Patients with limited cardiac reserve or atherosclerosis become symptomatic (angina, TIA/stroke) at a higher haematocrit than those with normal cardiovascular function.
The absolute reticulocyte count is the key first branch point for mechanism: an elevated count points to blood loss or haemolysis, while a low or inappropriately low count points to impaired production.
Manual percentage reticulocyte counts must be corrected for the haematocrit (a 1% count becomes an apparent 2% at a haematocrit of ~23%); the machine-derived absolute reticulocyte count needs no such correction.
Around 75% of all anemia is hypoproliferative, most often from mild-to-moderate iron deficiency or inflammation.
Anemia is frequently multifactorial (e.g., chronic inflammation plus occult GI blood loss in a patient on anti-inflammatory drugs), so iron status should be evaluated fully before and during treatment of any anemia.
Serum ferritin is an acute-phase reactant: it can be raised several-fold by inflammation and mask coexisting iron deficiency, whereas a ferritin <30 ug/L reliably indicates depleted body iron stores.
A high RDW favours iron deficiency (anisocytosis), whereas a low/normal RDW with microcytosis favours thalassaemia, where cells are more uniform in size.
Iron-deficiency pica and the neuropathy of B12 deficiency are deficiency-specific clues to seek on history and examination.
In acute blood loss the haemoglobin/haematocrit initially does NOT reflect the volume lost; the early picture is hypovolaemia, and the haemoglobin only falls over hours as fluid shifts and resuscitation dilute the blood.
# Additional differentials
Anemia of inflammation/chronic disease (diagnosis of exclusion; MCV often normal; hepcidin-mediated block of iron reutilization)
Thalassaemia (microcytic, low/normal RDW, defective globin synthesis)
Sideroblastic anemia (defective heme synthesis; microcytic)
Megaloblastic anemia from B12 or folate deficiency (oval macrocytes, hypersegmented neutrophils)
Myelodysplastic syndrome (macrocytic anemia or pancytopenia; stem-cell defect)
Anemia of chronic kidney disease (EPO lack; usually present once GFR <30)
Anemia of aging/older adults (low EPO for degree of anemia; 11% community-dwelling, up to 40% nursing-home over 65)
Aplastic anemia (hypocellular marrow, pancytopenia)
Pure red cell aplasia (isolated very low reticulocytes; autoimmune or parvovirus B19)
Acute/chronic leukaemia and other primary marrow neoplasms
Copper deficiency (with neutropenia and neurologic disease) and vitamin C deficiency/scurvy
Drug-induced macrocytosis (chemotherapy, some antiseizure drugs)
Alcohol use, liver disease, hypothyroidism (round macrocytosis)
# Infection mimics
Marrow-replacing infections such as histoplasmosis or tuberculosis causing a myelophthisic anemia
Parvovirus B19 causing pure red cell aplasia
# Non infectious mimics
Acute haemorrhage where the initial normal haemoglobin masks major blood loss; hypovolaemia dominates and anemia appears only after fluid shifts/resuscitation
Spurious/relative changes: serum iron and transferrin saturation vary diurnally, which can mislead iron-status interpretation
# Additional investigations
Corrected (or absolute) reticulocyte count as the first mechanistic discriminator
Red cell indices and RDW (MCV-based microcytic/normocytic/macrocytic classification; RDW to separate iron deficiency from thalassaemia)
Full iron studies: serum iron, TIBC, percent transferrin saturation (normal sat 25-50%), and ferritin for total-body stores
Haemolysis panel: LDH (raised), haptoglobin (low), free haemoglobin in blood/urine, and direct antiglobulin (antibody) test when reticulocytes are high and bleeding excluded
Blood-film morphology review (schistocytes, spherocytes, sickle cells, target cells, teardrop cells, macro-ovalocytes, hypersegmented neutrophils, Howell-Jolly bodies, basophilic stippling) to direct focused testing
Serum EPO level and BUN/creatinine for the normocytic, hypoproliferative workup
Serum protein electrophoresis / free light chains when myeloma is considered
Bone marrow aspirate and biopsy (biopsy before aspirate) when marrow infiltration or generalised dysfunction is suspected
# Red flags
Circulating blasts or other immature white cells, severe pancytopenia, or a very low reticulocyte count (<0.1%) mandating urgent marrow examination
Myelophthisic blood film (nucleated red cells, teardrop cells, immature white cells) suggesting marrow infiltration/replacement
Hypotension, angina, or other signs of cardiac compromise as clear indications that the anemia is not being tolerated
Acute haemorrhage with sudden loss of >25-30% of blood volume: vascular instability and decreased organ perfusion supervene and cannot be compensated by sympathetic mechanisms
Loss of consciousness or acute kidney injury early in major haemorrhage, reflecting hypovolaemic threat to brain and kidneys before any fall in haemoglobin
Clinical signs of concealed internal bleeding: Grey Turner sign (flank ecchymosis, retroperitoneal), Cullen sign (umbilical ecchymosis), or dullness to chest percussion (intrapleural)
# Pitfalls
Anchoring on a normal haemoglobin in early acute haemorrhage and underestimating blood loss; an abrupt fall in haemoglobin should prompt suspicion of acute posthemorrhagic anemia regardless of the given history.
Estimates of blood volume lost at a bloody scene are often overestimated, while concealed internal bleeding may be missed entirely.
Relying on ferritin alone during inflammation, which can falsely normalise or elevate it despite true iron deficiency.
Treating macrocytosis as automatically B12/folate deficiency; if megaloblastic markers (hypersegmentation, macro-ovalocytes) are present but nutritional tests are normal, suspect myelodysplasia and proceed to marrow examination.
Forgetting that the anemia of chronic disease/inflammation frequently has a normal MCV, so a normal MCV does not exclude it.
Performing marrow aspiration before biopsy, which distorts the biopsy with aspiration-induced bleeding.
Overlooking drug causes: ACE inhibitors can suppress EPO; chemotherapy and some antiseizure drugs cause macrocytosis.
# Symptomatic anaemia: treatment
Fatigue, dyspnoea, pallor, tachycardia with low haemoglobin; classify by MCV and look for blood loss/haemolysis.
By cause/severity; admit if unstable.
Transfuse if symptomatic/severe + treat the deficiency
# Steps
Assess haemodynamic tolerance; transfuse for symptomatic/severe anaemia (restrictive threshold ~70 g/L, ~80 if cardiac).
Identify and treat the cause — iron/B12/folate deficiency, bleeding, haemolysis, marrow, chronic disease.
Iron deficiency: oral iron (IV if intolerant/malabsorption) and find the source (GI evaluation in at-risk).
Replace B12/folate as indicated (give B12 before folate to avoid subacute combined degeneration).
Reticulocyte count and blood film direct further work-up.
IV iron when oral iron unsuitable
Transfuse if symptomatic; treat the underlying deficiency
# Investigations
FBC, MCV, blood film, reticulocytes
Iron studies, B12/folate
Haemolysis screen; source of bleeding
