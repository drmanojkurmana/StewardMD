# Iron Deficiency Anemia
Iron Deficiency Anemia, Haematology.
Aliases: IDA.
# Pathophysiology
Iron supply becomes inadequate for needs through excessive demand, insufficient intake, defective absorption, or blood loss; multiple causes frequently co-occur, especially in the elderly and in multimorbidity. In ISOLATED iron deficiency, stores are depleted while hemoglobin is still normal; when iron remains sufficient for erythropoiesis, red cells are normochromic/normocytic with decreased ferritin (<15-30 µg/L). As transferrin saturation falls <15-20%, iron supply to erythropoiesis is insufficient and cells become hypochromic and microcytic, the classic feature of iron-deficiency anemia. Iron deficiency is ABSOLUTE when stores are exhausted and FUNCTIONAL when erythropoiesis is iron-restricted but stores are adequate or even increased. Low hepcidin in iron deficiency maximizes dietary absorption; TMPRSS6 mutations cause hereditary iron-refractory IDA (IRIDA) with reduced absorption and oral-iron refractoriness. In inflammation, high hepcidin sequesters iron into stores, producing functional iron deficiency with low serum iron and transferrin saturation despite elevated ferritin; absolute and functional deficiency often coexist (IBD, CKD, aging). Beyond anemia, heart, muscle, and brain are affected; immune response may be blunted as lymphocytes require iron during the metabolic burst.
# Prognosis
Iron deficiency and IDA are highly treatable with iron replacement. Oral iron usually requires at least ~3 months of therapy to restore hemoglobin and replenish stores and prevent early recurrence; inadequate response (no reticulocytosis at 1 week or <1 g/dL hemoglobin rise at 2 weeks) should prompt a search for noncompliance, ongoing blood loss, or malabsorption. Prognosis ultimately depends on the underlying cause (e.g., occult GI malignancy) rather than the anemia itself.
# Severity classification
Severe anemia is generally taken as hemoglobin <=8 g/dL (the threshold at which anemia commonly becomes detectable and at which rapid IV correction is considered).
# Clinical pearls
Classic blood picture is a microcytic, hypochromic anemia (MCV <80 fL in established disease), but microcytosis can be ABSENT in early iron deficiency or masked by liver disease or concomitant folate/B12 deficiency.
Low serum ferritin is the mainstay of absolute iron deficiency, reflecting depleted stores; ferritin <30 µg/L has high sensitivity and specificity (92% and 98%) and can be used as a single diagnostic test.
Transferrin saturation (serum iron/TIBC) measures iron available for erythropoiesis; erythropoiesis becomes iron-restricted at values <15-20%.
Anemia develops only in the LATE stage because the erythron is relatively spared compared with heart and skeletal muscle, which become iron-depleted earlier; slow progression plus compensation means anemia is often undetected, especially in the young, until hemoglobin falls below ~8 g/dL.
Clinical clues include pica (compulsive ingestion of nonnutritive substances such as ice, soil, or clay), nail fragility and koilonychia, hair loss, cheilosis, atrophic glossitis, and dysphagia from esophageal webs (Plummer-Vinson syndrome).
Even symptomatic isolated iron deficiency WITHOUT anemia matters: suboptimal myocyte, cardiomyocyte, and neuron function can cause fatigue, irritability, poor concentration, restless legs syndrome, and reduced exercise capacity.
Severe iron-deficiency anemia may be accompanied by thrombocytosis, due to prevalent megakaryocyte commitment of the common erythroid-platelet progenitor when iron is insufficient.
Serum hepcidin is low/undetectable in absolute iron deficiency (maximizing dietary iron absorption) but elevated in inflammation and in IRIDA (TMPRSS6 mutations).
Inadequate therapeutic response (no reticulocytosis after 1 week, or <1 g/dL hemoglobin rise after 2 weeks of iron therapy) should prompt a search for the cause: noncompliance, ongoing blood loss, or malabsorption.
# Additional differentials
Anemia of inflammation (anemia of chronic disease) - the second most common anemia worldwide, due to functional iron deficiency; a diagnosis of exclusion usually occurring with an inflammatory disorder such as cancer or infection.
Thalassemia trait - microcytosis with normal-to-high ferritin and serum iron; red cells are more homogeneous in volume with a low/normal RDW.
Mixed forms (combined absolute and functional iron deficiency).
Other nutritional anemias - vitamin B12 and folate deficiency (megaloblastic, macrocytic); may coexist with and mask iron deficiency in malabsorption.
Sideroblastic anemia (rare) as a cause of microcytic hypochromic red cells.
# Non infectious mimics
Anemia of inflammation / chronic disease (functional iron deficiency from high hepcidin: low serum iron and transferrin saturation with NORMAL-to-HIGH ferritin).
Thalassemia trait (microcytic but with normal-to-high ferritin and iron, and a low/normal RDW).
# Additional investigations
Serum ferritin (mainstay; <30 µg/L diagnostic, sensitivity 92% / specificity 98%); note ferritin is an acute-phase reactant and may rise severalfold in inflammation.
Transferrin saturation (serum iron/TIBC); iron-restricted erythropoiesis at <15-20%.
Serum iron (reduced, but diurnal fluctuation prevents use as a single test).
MCV and RDW from the CBC (MCV <80 fL when established; RDW often elevated reflecting anisocytosis).
Soluble transferrin receptor (sTfR) - increased in iron deficiency; sTfR/log ferritin ratio >2 favors iron deficiency.
