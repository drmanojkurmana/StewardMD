# Acute and Chronic Myocarditis
Acute and Chronic Myocarditis, Cardiovascular.
Aliases: Myocarditis; Inflammatory cardiomyopathy; Viral myocarditis; Giant cell myocarditis; Eosinophilic myocarditis; Cardiac sarcoidosis; Lyme carditis; Chagas cardiomyopathy; Checkpoint inhibitor myocarditis.
# Pathophysiology
Infectious agents injure the myocardium both by direct cellular invasion (disrupting normal cellular processes) and by activating immune responses, with or without persistent infection. Viral myocarditis has classically been described in three phases from murine enterovirus (coxsackie B) models: (1) direct myocardial invasion, with viral entry sometimes enhanced by coxsackie/adenovirus receptor variants and enteroviral protease degrading dystrophin to trigger apoptosis and impair autophagy; (2) host immune response to conserved antigenic patterns, activating macrophages and expanding T- and B-cell populations, which can produce 'virally triggered' myocarditis even without direct viral injury, possibly via molecular mimicry between viral and cardiac antigens; and (3) progression to chronic cardiomyopathy, demonstrated in animal models. Direct genome entry occurs with enteroviruses, HIV, and dengue, but most common viruses do not appear to damage the heart directly, and early injury is often driven by cytokine storm and nonspecific immune activation. Notably, this initial immune response appears crucial to recovery, since early immunosuppression worsened viral replication and cardiac injury in animals. Viral 'hijacking' of cellular machinery to produce extracellular vesicles carrying viral RNA may allow protected spread to other cells. In Chagas disease, acute infection produces dense parasitic pseudocysts that rupture and cause diffuse myocyte necrosis, while chronic disease reflects persistent parasitemia plus depopulation of cardiac and gastrointestinal parasympathetic neurons. Noninfective inflammatory myocarditis (sarcoid granulomas, giant cell, eosinophilic, autoimmune, checkpoint inhibitor) reflects antigen-driven, dysregulated immune attack on myocardium; the paradigm is cardiac transplant rejection, which showed that antibodies and cytokines, not only lymphocytes, mediate reversible myocardial depression and that antigens are exposed by prior injury or infection.
# Prognosis
Prognosis is good for 'uncomplicated' acute myocarditis (LVEF >=0.50 without ventricular arrhythmias or shock), which represents about three-fourths of patients; spontaneous recovery is common, almost all retain LVEF >=0.50 on follow-up, and they survive without transplantation at 5 years. Among the one-fourth with complicated presentations, roughly 18% die or undergo transplantation over the next 5 years. In fulminant myocarditis, more than half of affected patients recover to near-normal LVEF despite presenting with refractory cardiogenic shock. Reduced LVEF at presentation predicts chronic cardiomyopathy. Chagas cardiomyopathy has lower survival than other cardiomyopathies once overt heart failure develops, and LVEF improvement is uncommon. Most patients with giant cell myocarditis in cardiogenic shock require urgent mechanical support or transplant. Checkpoint inhibitor myocarditis fatality was initially very high but is improving with earlier recognition and treatment.
# Severity classification
Fulminant myocarditis (~3-9%) denotes circulatory shock requiring high-dose inotropes or mechanical circulatory support. Diagnostically, 'probable myocarditis' requires at least one symptom plus one supporting finding (elevated troponin, consistent ECG, or reduced LVEF/wall-motion abnormality on imaging), while 'definite myocarditis' historically required a positive endomyocardial biopsy with lymphocytic infiltrate.
# Clinical pearls
Myocarditis is inflammation of heart muscle that can present acutely, subacutely, or insidiously, with outcomes ranging from full resolution to a relapsing course or progression to chronic cardiomyopathy; it is termed acute when symptoms have lasted under a month, often only days.
Chest pain is the most common symptom of acute myocarditis, occurring in more than 80% of patients, who frequently also have dyspnea or arrhythmias; fever is present in over half of cases and a viral prodrome may precede onset by days to weeks.
Acute myocarditis in adults typically occurs between roughly 30 and 45 years and is more common in men, whereas women tend to present after age 45.
At presentation about one-fourth of acute myocarditis cases have an LVEF below 0.50, ventricular arrhythmias, or shock; fulminant myocarditis (circulatory shock) is reported in about 3-9% of cases.
Diagnosis can now be made noninvasively when a typical symptomatic presentation is accompanied by elevated troponin plus cardiac MRI showing both myocardial edema and nonischemic injury without another cause; biopsy is no longer strictly required for a 'definite' diagnosis.
Because chest pain, ECG changes, and elevated troponin overlap between acute myocarditis and myocardial infarction, coronary imaging is often the first differential step; myocarditis is later diagnosed in up to one-third of patients with MINOCA (myocardial infarction with nonobstructed coronary arteries).
Eosinophil counts should be checked routinely because hypereosinophilia is present in most cases of eosinophilic myocarditis; right bundle branch block should raise suspicion for sarcoidosis or Chagas rather than typical cardiomyopathies, in which left bundle branch block predominates.
Around one in five patients with inflammatory myocarditis carry genetic variants considered pathogenic for cardiomyopathy, and 20-40% of dilated cardiomyopathy patients have variants sufficient to explain their disease; CMR fibrosis patterns once attributed to prior viral myocarditis are now recognized as common in genetic cardiomyopathy too.
Giant cell myocarditis accounts for 10-20% of biopsy-proven myocarditis, occurs equally in men and women (typically older than viral cases), is more often linked to systemic autoimmune disease, and usually presents acutely with rapidly progressive heart failure and tachyarrhythmias.
Chagas disease (Trypanosoma cruzi) is the most common infective cause of cardiomyopathy worldwide; after a usually silent initial infection patients enter an 'indeterminate phase' lasting decades, during which persistent low-level parasitemia is now recognized as the major driver of progression to chronic heart failure.
Diphtheria toxin (Corynebacterium diphtheriae) interferes with cardiac protein synthesis and especially affects the conduction system; cardiac involvement is the most common cause of death in this infection, and clinical suspicion alone justifies giving antitoxin with higher priority than antibiotics.
Combined immune checkpoint inhibitor myocarditis is the most dramatic noninfectious form: troponin is often elevated, biopsy shows extensive lymphocytic infiltration, and it should be suspected immediately with any acute cardiac presentation in a treated patient; CCU admission is recommended for elevated troponin with ECG changes.
# Additional differentials
Acute coronary syndrome / myocardial infarction (shared chest pain, ECG, troponin) and MINOCA
Genetic dilated or arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy (overlapping CMR fibrosis patterns)
Stress (takotsubo) cardiomyopathy and other dilated cardiomyopathies
Pericarditis and other COVID-associated cardiac involvement
Sepsis-related reversible myocardial depression from systemic inflammatory response
# Infection mimics
Sepsis with reversible systemic-inflammation-mediated myocardial depression (mimics myocarditis but is generally reversible if the patient survives)
Chagas cardiomyopathy presenting as dilated cardiomyopathy with conduction disease
Lyme carditis (Borrelia burgdorferi) presenting with conduction block
Granulomatous infections such as tuberculosis or histoplasmosis that must be excluded before immunosuppression for presumed sarcoidosis
# Non infectious mimics
Cardiac sarcoidosis (noncaseating granulomas; may mimic arrhythmogenic RV cardiomyopathy)
Giant cell myocarditis
Eosinophilic myocarditis / hypereosinophilic syndrome with Loeffler endocarditis
Systemic autoimmune disease: polymyositis/dermatomyositis, systemic lupus erythematosus (which also causes accelerated CAD, sterile endocarditis, pericarditis, vasculitis)
Immune checkpoint inhibitor myocarditis and vaccine-associated myocarditis
# Additional investigations
Cardiac troponin (often elevated) and B-type natriuretic peptide
Cardiac MRI showing myocardial edema plus nonischemic late gadolinium enhancement (typical mid-wall location)
ECG (ST elevation mimicking infarction, conduction block, tachyarrhythmias, nonspecific ST-T changes)
Coronary artery imaging to exclude infarction
Eosinophil count (hypereosinophilia in most eosinophilic myocarditis); creatine phosphokinase and C-reactive protein
Respiratory viral panel; selected serologies for HIV, Chagas, CMV, dengue, Lyme, and systemic autoimmune disease (viral titers rarely guide initial therapy)
Endomyocardial biopsy (with immunohistochemistry) when ventricular arrhythmias, conduction block, eosinophilia, or autoimmune disease suggest a specific etiology
Chest CT (mediastinal lymphadenopathy) and whole-chest FDG-PET for active sarcoid lesions; PCR for T. cruzi in acute Chagas
# Red flags
Fulminant presentation with cardiogenic shock or need for high-dose inotropes/mechanical circulatory support (3-9% of cases)
Ventricular arrhythmias or syncope, which may precede sudden cardiac death (inflammatory myocarditis found in 3-10% of sudden deaths in previously healthy young adults)
New conduction block, especially right bundle branch block (suggests sarcoidosis or Chagas)
LVEF <0.50 at presentation (marks higher-risk 'complicated' course and chronic cardiomyopathy risk)
Hypereosinophilia (suggests eosinophilic myocarditis, a cause of fulminant disease)
Acute cardiac symptoms in a patient on immune checkpoint inhibitors, often with concurrent involvement of other organs
# Pitfalls
Assuming any chest pain/ECG change/troponin elevation is infarction can miss myocarditis, which underlies up to one-third of MINOCA cases.
Sarcoid granulomas are scattered and commonly missed on endomyocardial biopsy, so a negative biopsy does not exclude cardiac sarcoidosis.
Eosinophilic myocarditis may be overlooked in milder presentations if hypereosinophilia is not assessed.
Viral titers and detection of viral genome fragments or anticardiac antibodies in chronic cardiomyopathy are of uncertain significance, as the same viruses (e.g., parvovirus B19, HHV6) are also found in normal control hearts and these assays are not in routine clinical use.
There are no data supporting routine immunosuppression for presumed viral acute myocarditis, and immunosuppression for nonspecific lymphocytic chronic myocarditis has not shown convincing benefit.
Initial care of suspected checkpoint inhibitor myocarditis should generally not be delayed to obtain endomyocardial biopsy.
# Management
Guideline-directed heart failure therapy for left ventricular dysfunction, using ACE inhibitors or ARBs (or an ARNI), beta-blockers, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists and diuretics
Rhythm monitoring and arrhythmia management, with restriction from strenuous exercise during the acute inflammatory phase
Mechanical circulatory support with inotropes, intra-aortic balloon pump or ECMO and intensive care for fulminant myocarditis with cardiogenic shock
Immunosuppression with corticosteroids and steroid-sparing agents for biopsy-proven autoimmune, giant-cell, eosinophilic or sarcoid myocarditis
Treatment of the specific identified cause, such as withdrawing an offending drug or treating an amenable infection
Endomyocardial biopsy in selected or deteriorating cases to guide immunosuppression
Serial troponin and cardiac MRI follow-up to track recovery, with device therapy or transplantation for refractory disease
