A sudden oesophageal tear announces itself after forceful vomiting against a closed glottis. This Boerhaave rupture classically splits the left posterolateral wall of the distal oesophagus, spilling saliva and gastric contents into the mediastinum and pleura. Pain, fever and surgical emphysema follow within hours, and mediastinitis turns rapidly fatal without drainage and repair.

Severity decides the path. The Pittsburgh severity score tallies pleural effusion, fever, leukocytosis, hypotension and related markers of established sepsis; a total above 5 marks a sick patient who usually needs an operation, while a low score with a contained leak suits a conservative course. Contrast swallow and computed tomography map the defect and any undrained collection before the decision is made.

Timing matters as much as anatomy. A perforation recognised within 24 hours, with the leak contained and the patient free of sepsis, can be managed without thoracotomy: nil by mouth, broad-spectrum antibiotics, proton pump inhibition and nutritional support, often with endoscopic stenting to seal the defect while tissues heal. Once mediastinal sepsis is established or the diagnosis is delayed, the same patient needs thoracotomy, debridement and direct repair.

Primary repair closes mucosa and muscle in layers, but suture lines in inflamed tissue need reinforcement. A buttress flap of pleura, intercostal muscle or pericardial fat is laid over the closure to protect it. Defects that destroy over 50 percent of the circumference cannot be closed primarily; these need resection of the damaged segment with immediate or staged reconstruction.

Caustic ingestion injuries follow different chemistry. Strong alkali produces liquefactive necrosis that penetrates deeply through the wall, while strong acid produces a coagulative eschar that somewhat limits depth but ravages the stomach. Endoscopy within 24 to 48 hours grades the burn by the Zargar scale from 0 (normal) through IIIB (extensive necrosis), deliberately avoiding the 5 to 15 day window when sloughing makes the wall friable and the scope itself may perforate.

The worst grade demands the boldest surgery. Grade IIIB injury with full-thickness death of the wall, mediastinitis or peritonitis cannot be salvaged by repair; it needs emergency oesophagectomy with a cervical oesophagostomy spout and a feeding jejunostomy, leaving reconstruction for about 6 months until inflammation resolves and nutrition is rebuilt.

Survivors often stricture. Fibrosis declares itself 3 to 8 weeks after the burn with progressive dysphagia, and serial endoscopic dilation is the first line, repeated until the lumen holds. Long, tortuous or refractory strictures defeat the dilator; these need resection with colonic interposition, usually left colon carried on the middle colic pedicle, routed behind the sternum or through the old bed.

When the gullet must be replaced, the stomach is the first-choice conduit, tubed and pulled up on the right gastroepiploic arcade with its rich submucosal network. The anastomosis sits in the neck after a McKeown three-stage resection or in the chest after an Ivor Lewis two-stage resection. Colon suits long defects and prior gastric surgery, and a jejunal segment bridges short cervical gaps where stomach cannot reach.
