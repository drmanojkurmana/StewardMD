Bladder exstrophy begins with a membrane that breaks too early. The cloacal membrane fails to gain reinforcement from ingrowing lateral mesoderm, and the unstable membrane ruptures before the 6th gestational week. The open bladder everts onto the abdominal wall, and the whole lower midline fails to close behind it.

The defect has a fixed pattern. Findings comprise an open exposed bladder template, a splayed pubic symphysis with widened pubic diastasis, divergent rectus abdominis muscles, epispadias of the penis or clitoris, and a low-set umbilicus. The bony pelvis gapes open, the pelvic floor has no anchor, and continence cannot develop without reconstruction.

Classic repair proceeds in three timed stages. Primary bladder and abdominal wall closure with bilateral pelvic osteotomy happens in the first 72 hours of life; epispadias repair follows at 1 to 2 years; and bladder neck reconstruction by the Young-Dees-Leadbetter technique completes the sequence at 4 to 5 years. Each stage builds on the last, and delay at any step compromises the next.

Osteotomy is what makes closure tension-free. Bilateral pelvic osteotomy, through posterior iliac, anterior innominate, or horizontal periacetabular cuts, is mandatory whenever closure happens beyond 72 hours, swinging the pubic bones together without tension. Without bone mobilization the soft tissues tear apart under strain, and the whole closure fails.

One operation can do everything at once. Complete Primary Repair of Exstrophy, the Mitchell CPRE technique, accomplishes bladder closure, epispadias repair, and bladder neck reconstruction together in the neonatal period. It trades one major neonatal operation for the three-stage sequence, in centres with the experience to do it safely.

The Kelly operation rebuilds the outlet differently. The Kelly procedure dissects the bladder neck, pelvic floor, and external sphincter complex free and repositions them anatomically around the urethra, restoring continence without needing to tighten the bladder neck itself. Continence comes from restored anatomy rather than deliberate obstruction.

Bladder size gates the final step. A capacity of at least 85 mL to 100 mL measured under anaesthesia is an absolute prerequisite before formal bladder neck reconstruction, because a small stiff bladder cannot store at low pressure no matter how good the outlet. Below this threshold the surgeon waits and cycles the bladder, remeasuring until growth suffices.

Exstrophy bladders turn malignant far more often. Unclosed or reconstructed patients carry a lifelong 400-fold increased risk of bladder adenocarcinoma arising from glandular metaplasia, cystitis glandularis, of the exposed mucosa. Lifelong surveillance with cystoscopy and biopsy of suspicious patches is therefore part of the repair, not an afterthought.
