# Glomerular Diseases (Spectrum)
Glomerular Diseases (Spectrum), Nephrology.
Aliases: IgA nephropathy; FSGS; membranous nephropathy; minimal change disease.
# Pathophysiology
Each kidney holds ~0.9 million glomerular tufts whose fenestrated endothelium, GBM, and podocyte foot processes with slit-pore membranes form a size- and charge-selective filtration barrier; glomerular injury allows gram quantities of protein (mainly albumin) to escape. Disease arises through genetic mutations (e.g., NPHS1/nephrin, NPHS2/podocin, TRPC6, APOL1 risk variants for FSGS; type IV collagen alpha3/4/5 in Alport's; complement-pathway/CFHR mutations in MPGN and C3 glomerulopathy/aHUS), infection, toxins, autoimmunity, hypertension/atherosclerosis, thrombosis/emboli, and diabetes; many cases remain idiopathic. Inflammation of glomerular capillaries (glomerulonephritis) is typically immune-mediated: preformed circulating immune complexes deposit on the subendothelial side or mesangium, while in-situ formation produces subepithelial deposits; anti-GBM antibodies bind linearly to the GBM. Deposits and Toll-like receptor activation recruit neutrophils, macrophages, and T cells, activate complement to C5-9 membrane-attack complexes, and release proteases and oxidants that damage the mesangium, capillaries, and GBM and efface podocytes; early CD4/CD8 T-cell activation is important. Depending on antigen location and host genetics, injury produces endocapillary or extracapillary (crescentic) proliferation. Progression to chronic kidney failure tracks with downstream tubulointerstitial nephritis and fibrosis, driven by proteinuria-laden filtrate, inflammatory mediators carried from inflamed glomeruli, and fibroblast/myofibroblast expansion (TGF-beta, FGF-2, HIF-1alpha, PDGF) laying down collagen.
# Prognosis
Prognosis is lesion-specific and tracks with the degree of tubulointerstitial fibrosis. Poststreptococcal GN resolves in most children within 3-6 weeks with rare progression to ESKD, but is worse in the elderly (up to 60% azotemia). Minimal change disease responds to steroids in 90-95% of children and 80-90% of adults though relapse is common (70-75% of children). Primary FSGS with nephrotic-range proteinuria reaches ESKD in ~50% by 10 years and rarely remits spontaneously, but treatment-induced remission improves outcome. In membranous nephropathy roughly one-third remit spontaneously, one-third have relapsing nephrotic syndrome with preserved function, and one-third progress to kidney failure or die of complications. IgA nephropathy progresses to ESKD in only 25-30% over 20-25 years; persistent proteinuria >=6 months is the strongest adverse predictor. Dense deposit disease progresses to ESKD in ~50%. Untreated RPGN/crescentic disease and ANCA vasculitis carry high mortality, but ANCA vasculitis achieves remission in 75-90% with treatment (relapse 25-50%).
# Severity classification
Glomerular disease is organized into six clinical syndromes (acute nephritic, pulmonary-renal, nephrotic, basement membrane, glomerular-vascular, infectious disease-associated) graded semiquantitatively by proteinuria, hematuria, and vascular injury. Biopsy descriptors: focal (<50% of glomeruli) vs diffuse (>50%); segmental (part of a tuft) vs global; endocapillary vs extracapillary (crescentic) proliferation. Lupus nephritis uses the ISN/RPS class I-VI scheme (I minimal mesangial, II mesangial proliferative, III focal, IV diffuse, V membranous, VI sclerotic). IgA nephropathy uses the Oxford MEST-C score (mesangial/endocapillary hypercellularity, segmental sclerosis, tubular fibrosis, crescents). Albuminuria is staged normal (8-10 mg/24 h), microalbuminuria (30-300 mg/24 h), and frank proteinuria (>300 mg/24 h).
# Clinical pearls
Glomerular disease is grouped into six clinical syndromes that overlap and are not mutually exclusive: acute nephritic, pulmonary-renal, nephrotic, basement membrane, glomerular-vascular, and infectious disease-associated.
Nephritic syndrome = inflammatory pattern: hematuria with red blood cell casts, dysmorphic red cells, pyuria, hypertension, fluid retention, only mild-to-moderate (1-2 g/24 h) proteinuria, and a falling GFR.
Nephrotic syndrome = heavy proteinuria (>3.0-3.5 g/24 h) with hypoalbuminemia, hypercholesterolemia, edema/anasarca, hypertension, and only minimal/microscopic hematuria; isolated heavy proteinuria without these features is termed nephrotic-range proteinuria.
Red blood cell casts or dysmorphic red cells in the sediment make glomerulonephritis likely; as few as 3-5 red cells in spun first-morning urine is suspicious.
Gross hematuria is rare in glomerular disease except in IgA nephropathy and sickle cell disease; most glomerular hematuria is asymptomatic and microscopic.
Kidney biopsy is the key diagnostic step in most glomerulonephritis; it identifies the lesion and often directs treatment, but is reserved for clinically stable patients with adequate clotting who can receive therapy.
Minimal change disease (MCD) causes 70-90% of childhood nephrotic syndrome but only 10-15% in adults; light microscopy is normal, immunofluorescence negative (or scant mesangial IgM), and electron microscopy shows foot-process effacement; childhood proteinuria is selective (mostly albumin).
FSGS is the most common cause of primary glomerular disease leading to ESKD in the US; lesions are focal and segmental and concentrated at the corticomedullary junction, so a superficial biopsy can miss them and be misread as MCD.
Membranous glomerulonephritis (MGN) accounts for ~25% of adult nephrotic syndrome; 70-80% of primary cases have circulating IgG4 anti-PLA2R antibodies, and biopsy may no longer be required when anti-PLA2R is positive with normal creatinine and no secondary cause.
IgA nephropathy is defined by dominant/codominant mesangial IgA deposits and is one of the most common glomerulonephritides worldwide; the two classic presentations are persistent asymptomatic microscopic hematuria and recurrent gross hematuria during/just after an upper respiratory infection (synpharyngitic).
Membranoproliferative GN (MPGN) is now viewed as a pattern of injury (mesangial/subendothelial immune complexes, complement, or monoclonal deposits) producing GBM double contours ('tram-tracking'), rather than a single disease; classify by underlying immune-complex, complement, or monoclonal cause.
C3 glomerulopathy (dense deposit disease and C3 glomerulonephritis) shows sole/dominant glomerular C3 with little or no immunoglobulin and is driven by alternative-complement-pathway dysregulation (e.g., CFHR mutations, C3 nephritic factor).
RPGN is the clinical picture of acute nephritis with serum creatinine rising over days; crescentic glomerulonephritis is its pathologic equivalent and warrants urgent biopsy and treatment.
When RPGN presents with lung hemorrhage it is a pulmonary-renal syndrome; consider anti-GBM/Goodpasture disease, ANCA small-vessel vasculitis, lupus, and cryoglobulinemia.
ANCA small-vessel vasculitis (GPA, MPA, EGPA, renal-limited) causes a pauci-immune (little/no immune-complex deposition) segmental necrotizing/crescentic glomerulonephritis; anti-PR3 is more common in GPA and anti-MPO in MPA/EGPA.
Anti-GBM disease targets the alpha3 NC1 domain of type IV collagen; biopsy shows linear IgG staining along the GBM (versus the granular 'lumpy-bumpy' deposits of immune-complex disease); 10-15% also have anti-MPO ANCA.
Poststreptococcal GN classically shows subepithelial 'humps' on electron microscopy, low C3/CH50 with normal C4 in the first week, and a positive streptozyme test; it rarely requires biopsy and has a good prognosis in children.
Lupus nephritis is classified ISN/RPS class I-VI; proliferative class III/IV lesions (and class IV with crescents) carry the worst prognosis, while mesangial class I/II need little therapy and class V is a membranous pattern.
Kidney failure in glomerulonephritis correlates histologically with the degree of tubulointerstitial nephritis and interstitial fibrosis rather than with the specific glomerular lesion; interstitial fibrosis is an ominous sign of irreversibility.
On biopsy, <50% glomerular involvement is focal and >50% diffuse; injury within a tuft is segmental (part) or global (most); endocapillary proliferation is within the tuft and extracapillary proliferation (crescents) extends into Bowman's space.
# Additional differentials
Acute nephritic syndrome: poststreptococcal GN, subacute bacterial endocarditis-associated GN, lupus nephritis, anti-GBM disease, IgA nephropathy, ANCA small-vessel vasculitis (GPA, MPA, EGPA), IgA vasculitis (Henoch-Schonlein purpura), cryoglobulinemia, MPGN, C3 glomerulopathy, mesangioproliferative GN.
Nephrotic syndrome: minimal change disease, FSGS, membranous glomerulonephritis, diabetic nephropathy, AL/AA amyloidosis, light-chain deposition disease, fibrillary-immunotactoid disease, Fabry's disease.
Pulmonary-renal syndromes: Goodpasture's/anti-GBM disease, ANCA vasculitis, IgA vasculitis, cryoglobulinemia.
Basement membrane syndromes: anti-GBM disease, Alport's syndrome, thin basement membrane disease, nail-patella syndrome.
Glomerular vascular syndromes: atherosclerotic and hypertensive nephropathy, cholesterol emboli, sickle cell disease, thrombotic microangiopathies, antiphospholipid syndrome.
Microscopic hematuria with minimal proteinuria can also reflect non-glomerular causes: urinary tract malignancy (especially older men), BPH, interstitial nephritis, papillary necrosis, hypercalciuria, kidney stones, cystic kidney disease, or renal vascular injury.
# Infection mimics
Poststreptococcal glomerulonephritis after nephritogenic streptococcal pharyngitis (1-3 weeks) or skin infection (2-6 weeks).
Subacute bacterial endocarditis-associated and other persistent-infection (shunt, abscess, infected prosthesis; often Staphylococcus) glomerulonephritis, which can mimic or coexist with ANCA vasculitis.
Hepatitis B and C (membranous, MPGN, cryoglobulinemic patterns), HIV-associated nephropathy (collapsing FSGS), syphilis, leprosy, malaria, and schistosomiasis - globally, malaria and schistosomiasis may be the most common causes of glomerulonephritis.
IgA-dominant Staphylococcus-associated infectious glomerulonephritis can mimic primary IgA nephropathy.
# Non infectious mimics
Diabetic nephropathy and amyloidosis both thicken the GBM and can mimic membranous nephropathy on light microscopy.
Secondary FSGS (adaptive hyperfiltration/obesity, reflux, hypertension, drugs such as heroin/interferon/bisphosphonates, viruses) and genetic/familial podocytopathies must be excluded before diagnosing primary FSGS, as immunosuppression is not indicated in secondary/genetic forms.
Secondary membranous nephropathy from malignancy (breast, lung, colon), infection, lupus or other autoimmune disease, IgG4 disease, or drugs (gold, penicillamine, NSAIDs).
Membranous lupus nephritis (subendothelial deposits/tubuloreticular inclusions) may precede extrarenal lupus and mimic primary MGN.
C3 glomerulopathy can be morphologically indistinguishable from resolving postinfectious glomerulonephritis.
Mesangioproliferative pattern is shared by IgA nephropathy, P. falciparum malaria, resolving postinfectious GN, and lupus class II.
# Additional investigations
Urinalysis with microscopy for dysmorphic red cells, red blood cell casts, pyuria; quantify proteinuria by spot urine albumin/creatinine ratio (UACR >30 mg/g abnormal) or 24-h collection; distinguish selective (albumin-predominant, childhood MCD) from nonselective proteinuria.
Serologies: ASO/anti-DNase/antihyaluronidase and streptozyme; ANA and anti-dsDNA; complement C3 and C4 (low C3 with normal C4 in poststreptococcal GN and C3 glomerulopathy; hypocomplementemia in active lupus nephritis); ANCA (anti-PR3, anti-MPO); anti-GBM antibody to the alpha3 NC1 domain specifically; cryoglobulins and rheumatoid factor; antiphospholipid antibodies; HIV and hepatitis B/C serologies.
Target-antigen antibodies in nephrotic disease: anti-PLA2R and anti-THSD7A (membranous nephropathy); DNAJB9 (fibrillary GN).
Kidney ultrasound to assess kidney size (small kidneys suggest chronicity) and exclude obstruction.
Kidney biopsy with light microscopy (H&E, PAS, Jones silver, Congo red, Masson trichrome), immunofluorescence (IgG/IgM/IgA, C3/C4, PLA2R/THSD7A/DNAJB9), and electron microscopy to localize deposits and assess the GBM.
C3 nephritic factor, factor H, soluble C5b-9, paraprotein screening, and CFHR genetic testing (with family screening) when C3 glomerulopathy is suspected.
# Red flags
RPGN / crescentic glomerulonephritis - serum creatinine rising over days is a nephrologic emergency requiring urgent biopsy and immunosuppression.
Pulmonary-renal syndrome (glomerulonephritis with lung hemorrhage/hemoptysis and falling hemoglobin) - consider anti-GBM/Goodpasture, ANCA vasculitis, lupus, cryoglobulinemia; lung hemorrhage responds to plasmapheresis.
Anti-GBM disease with oliguria, serum creatinine >5-6 mg/dL, need for acute dialysis, or >50% crescents on biopsy - markers of a poor prognosis requiring prompt treatment.
ANCA small-vessel vasculitis - high mortality without treatment; virtually all patients need urgent induction therapy.
Malignant hypertension can rapidly complicate glomerulosclerosis with fibrinoid arteriolar necrosis, thrombotic microangiopathy, and acute kidney failure.
Nephrotic syndrome is a hypercoagulable state - membranous nephropathy carries the highest risk of renal vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, and deep-vein thrombosis.
# Pitfalls
A superficial kidney biopsy that fails to sample deeper corticomedullary glomeruli can miss the focal/segmental lesions of FSGS and be misdiagnosed as minimal change disease - suspect FSGS in steroid-resistant 'MCD'.
Test serum specifically against the alpha3 NC1 domain of collagen IV: non-nephritic anti-alpha1 NC1 antibodies in paraneoplastic syndromes cannot be distinguished by assays using whole basement-membrane fragments.
ANCA-associated vasculitis can accompany or be confused with subacute bacterial endocarditis-associated GN; it must be ruled out because the treatments differ.
There is no role for immunosuppression in poststreptococcal GN even when crescents are present, nor in secondary or genetic FSGS.
Glomerular hematuria is usually microscopic and asymptomatic, so the diagnosis is often delayed; isolated microscopic hematuria in older men still requires exclusion of urinary tract malignancy.
Nephrotic-range proteinuria without edema or hypoalbuminemia is not necessarily full nephrotic syndrome, and transient/orthostatic proteinuria (fever, exercise, obesity, CHF, upright posture) must be distinguished from sustained pathologic proteinuria.
# Management
Direct disease-specific immunosuppression by biopsy-proven lesion, using corticosteroids with agents such as cyclophosphamide, rituximab, calcineurin inhibitors or mycophenolate for immune-mediated glomerulonephritis
Renin-angiotensin blockade with an ACE inhibitor or angiotensin receptor blocker to reduce proteinuria and control blood pressure, the cornerstone of non-immunological management
Manage oedema and volume overload with dietary salt restriction and diuretics, and treat nephrotic hyperlipidaemia with a statin
Reduce thrombotic risk with anticoagulation in severe nephrotic states, and treat the underlying trigger such as infection, lupus or vasculitis
Urgent immunosuppression with plasma exchange for rapidly progressive crescentic disease such as anti-GBM or ANCA-associated glomerulonephritis
Monitor renal function, proteinuria and blood pressure, avoid nephrotoxins, and provide immunisation appropriate to immunosuppression
Refer to nephrology for kidney biopsy to guide therapy, and plan renal replacement therapy or transplantation for progression to established kidney failure
