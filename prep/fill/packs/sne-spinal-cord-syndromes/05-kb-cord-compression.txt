# Spinal cord compression
Spinal cord compression, Neurology / Emergency.
# Red flags
Time-critical for neurological recovery; high-dose steroids if malignant
# Investigations
Urgent whole-spine MRI
Neurosurgical / oncology review
# Pathophysiology
Because the cord packs nearly the entire motor output and sensory input of the trunk and limbs into a small cross-section, even a small compressive mass causes disproportionately severe, often reversible-if-treated-early, deficits. In neoplastic compression, metastases lodge in the vertebral body or pedicle (the axial skeleton is rich in marrow); the enlarging deposit compresses the underlying dura, or a paravertebral mass (lymphoma, myeloma, paediatric tumours) extends through the intervertebral foramen into the canal. Expanding extradural tumour injures axons and myelin mechanically and compromises blood flow, producing ischaemia and infarction. In spinal epidural abscess, as the collection expands it causes additional cord damage through venous congestion and thrombosis, so that once weakness appears deterioration can be rapid and irreversible. The thoracic spine is the commonest site of metastatic compression (~70%), followed by lumbosacral (~20%) and cervical (~10%).
# Prognosis
Outcome is strongly time- and function-dependent: treatment is most successful in patients who are still ambulatory and have intact sphincter control when therapy begins, and a good response is expected in those ambulatory at presentation - treatment usually prevents new weakness and up to a third regain some motor function. Motor deficits fixed for more than 12 h seldom improve, and beyond 48 h substantial recovery is unlikely. For spinal epidural abscess, prompt diagnosis and treatment allow significant recovery in up to two-thirds, but surgery is unlikely to reverse deficits fixed for more than a few days. Recovery from paraparesis is better after surgery than radiotherapy alone, though survival may be short with widespread metastatic disease.
# Severity classification
Clinically, prognosis is best stratified by ambulatory and sphincter status at presentation and by duration of any motor deficit.
# Clinical pearls
A horizontally demarcated sensory level (identified by moving a pinprick or cold stimulus up the trunk) below which sensory, motor and autonomic function is impaired is the hallmark of a cord lesion; for the spinothalamic tract the true level may sit one to two segments above the clinically detected level.
The upper border of sensory loss often lies one or two vertebrae BELOW the actual site of compression, so the radiographic target may be higher than the clinical level suggests.
Pain is usually the first symptom of metastatic compression and can precede neurological signs by days to months; it characteristically worsens when supine/recumbent and at night, helping distinguish it from degenerative disk pain which is often relieved by lying down.
Lhermitte's sign (an electric tingling shooting down the back and limbs on neck flexion/extension) can be an EARLY clue to cervical cord compression.
Loss of bowel/bladder control is usually a LATE feature; motor and sensory loss typically precede sphincter disturbance.
Useful trunk sensory landmarks for localising a thoracic level are the nipples (T4) and umbilicus (T10).
Spinal column infection (osteomyelitis/abscess) characteristically crosses the disk space to involve the adjacent vertebral body, whereas tumour tends to respect the disk space - a distinguishing imaging feature.
Vertebral collapse is not a reliable marker of malignancy - about 20% of collapses, especially in older and postmenopausal patients, are due to osteoporosis rather than tumour.
Pedicle erosion (the 'winking owl' sign) is the earliest plain-film sign of vertebral tumour, but plain films are insensitive and a normal film does not exclude malignancy.
# Additional differentials
Intradural extramedullary tumours (meningioma, neurofibroma/schwannoma) - usually slow-growing and benign; long symptom duration favours an intradural origin
Intramedullary lesions (ependymoma, haemangioblastoma, low-grade astrocytoma, rarely metastasis) - present with poorly localised burning pain and SACRAL SPARING rather than radicular pain
Cervical spondylotic / degenerative cervical myelopathy - osteophytes, ossified posterior longitudinal ligament/ligamentum flavum, disk degeneration; insidious, stepwise; early signs are subtle (paraesthesia, loss of hand dexterity, 'clumsiness')
Acute spondylytic myelopathy from hyperextension injury on a pre-existing stenotic canal, sometimes producing a central cord syndrome
Conus medullaris syndrome - saddle anaesthesia (S3-S5), early prominent bladder/bowel dysfunction with lax anal tone, preserved leg strength
# Infection mimics
Spinal epidural abscess - midline spinal/neck pain, fever and progressive limb weakness; mostly Staphylococcus aureus (consider MRSA), with gram-negative bacilli, streptococci, anaerobes and fungi less commonly; risk factors include diabetes, IV drug use, alcoholism, renal failure, malignancy, HIV and recent spinal surgery/anaesthesia/trauma
Vertebral osteomyelitis / diskitis - neurological deficit in about a third of cases, mostly mediated by epidural abscess; fever may be absent, especially in cervical disease
Tuberculous spondylitis (Pott's disease) with epidural extension - an important cause in the developing world
Infectious noncompressive myelitis to consider once compression excluded (VZV, HSV, CMV, HIV, HTLV-1, Borrelia, Listeria, syphilis, Mycoplasma)
# Non infectious mimics
Osteoporotic vertebral collapse
Spinal epidural haematoma - acute focal/radicular pain then cord or conus signs; predisposed by anticoagulation, trauma, tumour or blood dyscrasias, occasionally post-LP or epidural anaesthesia
Hematomyelia - acute painful transverse myelopathy from intramedullary haemorrhage (trauma, vascular malformation, vasculitis, coagulopathy)
Spinal cord infarction / anterior spinal artery syndrome - sudden myelopathy without antecedent pain, with preserved vibration/position sense
Radiation myelopathy, neoplastic leptomeningitis and spinal lipomatosis
Subacute combined degeneration (vitamin B12 deficiency), copper deficiency
Inflammatory/demyelinating myelopathy - multiple sclerosis, neuromyelitis optica, sarcoidosis
# Additional investigations
MRI with gadolinium centred on the suspected level is the initial procedure; imaging the entire spine is important because up to ~40% with compression at one level have asymptomatic epidural metastases elsewhere (and multiple epidural deposits are present in ~25%)
CT myelography reserved for patients who cannot undergo MRI promptly or have non-diagnostic MRI
ESR and CRP are valuable for excluding spinal infection (raised in ~98-100% of vertebral osteomyelitis)
Blood cultures (positive in over half of epidural abscess cases); image-guided or open biopsy/aspiration when cultures are negative
If cord compression suspected but only radicular symptoms without myelopathy, imaging may safely be deferred 24-48 h - but suspected myelopathic compression is imaged immediately
In compression from an unknown primary, a simple workup (chest imaging, mammography, PSA, abdominal CT) often reveals the malignancy
# Red flags
Established motor deficit (paraplegia/quadriplegia) present >12 h rarely improves, and beyond 48 h the prognosis for meaningful motor recovery is poor - urgency is measured in hours
Autonomic dysfunction (decreased anal tone, reduced perineal sensation, distended bladder, absent anal wink or bulbocavernosus reflex) signals cord involvement and is an unfavourable prognostic sign
Once weakness appears in spinal epidural abscess, progression can be rapid and irreversible
Recent-onset persistent thoracic back pain in a cancer patient is suspicious for vertebral metastasis (the thoracic spine is rarely involved by spondylosis)
Pain crescendo pattern, pain aggravated by cough/Valsalva/recumbency, and Lhermitte's sign warrant urgent imaging
# Pitfalls
Mistaking a myelopathy for polyneuropathy or Guillain-Barre syndrome: ascending paraesthesias suggest neuropathy, but a sharply demarcated sensory level plus bladder disturbance points to a cord origin.
Acute severe transverse lesions cause initial FLACCID weakness and areflexia ('spinal shock') that can be misread as anterior horn cell disease or acute polyneuropathy before hyperreflexia supervenes over days to weeks.
Cauda equina syndrome (low back/radicular pain, saddle/perineal sensory loss, asymmetric leg weakness, sphincter and sexual dysfunction, lost ankle/bulbocavernosus/anal reflexes) reflects nerve-root rather than cord compression and must be distinguished from conus and cord syndromes - mass lesions low in the canal often produce a mixed picture.
Gadolinium enhancement can deceptively 'normalise' vertebral metastases by raising their signal to that of normal marrow on MRI.
Cervical osteomyelitis/epidural abscess may present without fever, delaying recognition - keep it in mind for neck pain with IV drug use, immunosuppression or diabetes.
# Spinal cord compression: treatment
Bilateral leg weakness, a sensory level, and bladder/bowel dysfunction ± back pain. Urgent whole-spine MRI.
Emergency spinal surgery / oncology.
Urgent MRI + high-dose dexamethasone
