Small renal cancers are removed with the kidney left in place. Partial nephrectomy, or nephron-sparing surgery, is the preferred standard for T1a masses of 4 cm or less and for feasible T1b tumours, because keeping nephrons preserves long-term renal function and lowers cardiovascular risk. The operation excises the tumour with a thin rim of normal parenchyma and reconstructs the defect, trading a longer operation for a lifetime of better kidney function.

Clamp time decides how much kidney survives the operation. Warm ischemia, with the renal artery clamped at body temperature, should stay under 20 to 25 minutes, while cold ischemia with ice slush poured around the kidney extends the safe window to 35 minutes. Beyond these limits acute tubular necrosis sets in and the preserved kidney never recovers full function, so surgeons plan the resection and renorrhaphy against the clock.

A minority of tumours grow into the venous system. Renal cell carcinoma extends into the renal vein and inferior vena cava in 4 to 10 percent of cases, and the Neves-Zincke classification stages the thrombus from Level I in the renal vein up through Level IV above the diaphragm into the atrium. The level dictates the entire operative plan, from a simple caval milking manoeuvre to opening the chest, and it must be mapped precisely on preoperative imaging.

Level IV thrombus above the diaphragm is a cardiac operation as much as a urological one. These patients need cardiopulmonary bypass with deep hypothermic circulatory arrest to open the vena cava and atrium in a bloodless field, plus transesophageal echocardiography to watch the thrombus in real time. The approach is multidisciplinary by necessity, with cardiac surgery, anaesthesia, and urology operating as one team.

Metastatic disease is risk-stratified before drugs are chosen. The IMDC criteria use 6 clinical factors: Karnofsky performance below 80 percent, under 1 year from diagnosis to systemic treatment, anemia, hypercalcemia, neutrophilia, and thrombocytosis. Favourable-risk patients carry none of these, while intermediate and poor-risk patients carry one or more, and the group assignment steers first-line therapy.

Intermediate and poor-risk metastatic clear cell disease gets combination immunotherapy. First-line options pair dual checkpoint blockade with nivolumab plus ipilimumab, or a VEGFR-TKI with an anti-PD-1 or anti-PD-L1 agent such as pembrolizumab. Combinations beat single-agent sunitinib on survival in this population, at the cost of immune-related toxicities that need proactive monitoring.

High-risk disease after complete resection earns adjuvant treatment. Pembrolizumab given for 1 year prolongs disease-free survival in resected high-risk clear cell carcinoma, as shown in the KEYNOTE-564 trial. It is the only adjuvant option with this level of evidence, so completely resected patients with aggressive pathology are counselled for a year of therapy despite the risk of immune side effects.

Not every small mass needs an operation. Active surveillance with serial imaging, or thermal ablation by cryoablation or radiofrequency ablation, is acceptable for masses under 3 cm in elderly or comorbid patients whose competing mortality outweighs cancer risk. Biopsy first confirms the histology, and growth beyond thresholds or patient preference converts surveillance into intervention.
