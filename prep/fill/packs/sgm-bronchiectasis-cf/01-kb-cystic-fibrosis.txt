# Cystic Fibrosis
Cystic Fibrosis, Respiratory.
Aliases: CF; CFTR disease.
# Pathophysiology
Mutation of CFTR, an apical epithelial anion channel that conducts chloride and bicarbonate (~1480 amino acids, gated by ATP hydrolysis without active transport against gradient), impairs hydration and clearance of exocrine secretions. In the airway, loss of CFTR-dependent anion secretion depletes the periciliary fluid layer, causing ciliary collapse and failure to clear overlying mucus; mucoobstruction with chronic infection drives an aggressive, unrelenting neutrophilic inflammatory response with proteases and oxidants, leading to airway remodeling and bronchiectasis. Low airway-surface-fluid pH may impair bacterial killing. Similar mechanisms affect pancreatic acini/ducts (fibrotic destruction, cyst formation, exocrine and eventually endocrine insufficiency), hepatobiliary tissue (biliary obstruction, focal/multilobular cirrhosis, portal hypertension), and intestine (meconium ileus, distal intestinal obstructive syndrome). Over 2000 CFTR allelic variants are described and grouped into classes I-VI by molecular mechanism (e.g., F508del is a class II protein-maturation/folding defect degraded in the endoplasmic reticulum; G551D is a class III gating defect).
# Prognosis
Modulator and other therapies have transformed CF from a disease that historically led to death in early childhood to one with frequent survival well into the fifth decade and beyond; US median survival now exceeds 60 years. Genotype is of limited value for predicting respiratory rate of decline or longevity. End-stage pulmonary failure may be treated with lung transplantation (median survival >9 years among adults). Marked geographic disparity exists: median survival is <30 years in much of Latin America versus >60 years in the United States.
# Severity classification
CFTR mutations are categorized by molecular mechanism into classes I-VI: class I, absent synthesis (e.g., nonsense mutations G542X, R553X, W1282X); class II, defective protein maturation and premature degradation (e.g., F508del, ~85% of US alleles, the most common); class III, disordered gating/regulation (e.g., G551D, ~4% of patients); class IV, defective conductance through the channel pore; class V, reduced transcript number from promoter/splicing abnormality; class VI, accelerated turnover from the cell surface. Severity is not formally graded by stage; FEV1 <30% predicted is used as a lung-transplant referral threshold.
# Clinical pearls
Cystic fibrosis is an autosomal recessive exocrinopathy affecting multiple epithelial tissues; the gene product CFTR is an apical anion (chloride and bicarbonate) channel that regulates the volume and composition of exocrine secretion.
Major morbidity and mortality arise from pulmonary disease: copious hyperviscous adherent secretions obstruct small and medium airways, and a complex flora including Staphylococcus aureus, Haemophilus influenzae, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa is routinely cultured from CF sputum.
P. aeruginosa shows stereotypic pathogenesis: an early sentinel colonization often becomes lifelong infection by the same strain, and over years evolves a mucoid (alginate-producing) phenotype that confers selective advantage and poor host prognosis.
Radiographic sinusitis occurs in most patients with CF; the sinuses may act as a reservoir for bacterial seeding of the lower airways.
Exocrine pancreatic insufficiency causes chronic malabsorption, poor growth, and fat-soluble vitamin deficiency; high blood immunoreactive trypsinogen (used in newborn screening) and low fecal elastase-1 are characteristic.
CF-related diabetes mellitus occurs in >30% of adults and is multifactorial (endocrine pancreatic destruction plus, in some, insulin resistance); annual diabetes testing is recommended for adults with CF.
Men typically show complete involution of the vas deferens despite functioning spermatogenesis, and ~99% of males with CF are infertile (unable to conceive without in vitro fertilization).
Diagnosis is confirmed by disease-causing variants on both CFTR alleles and/or sweat chloride >=60 mEq/L together with characteristic exocrine manifestations; sweat electrolyte testing follows pilocarpine iontophoresis.
Several severe defects (F508del, G551D, nonsense alleles) predict pancreatic insufficiency, present in ~80% of patients; F508del alone accounts for ~85% of defective CFTR alleles in the United States.
CFTR modulators (ivacaftor potentiator; lumacaftor/tezacaftor/elexacaftor correctors) are highly effective modulator therapy (HEMT); triple combination therapy is projected to benefit >90% of patients and improves FEV1, reduces exacerbations, and lowers sweat chloride.
Pulmonary exacerbation poor-prognosis pathogens that are rigorously monitored include Burkholderia cepacia complex, Stenotrophomonas maltophilia, Achromobacter, mucoid P. aeruginosa, and atypical mycobacteria; MRSA may also signal worse outcomes.
Infection commonly persists despite modulator therapy, so chronic respiratory infection and inflammation continue even after substantial clinical improvement on HEMT.
# Additional differentials
Non-CF bronchiectasis (CF carrier status predisposes; heterozygous CFTR mutations implicated)
Chronic rhinosinusitis (associated with relative CFTR deficiency)
Congenital bilateral absence of the vas deferens as an isolated forme fruste of CFTR mutation
Idiopathic/recurrent pancreatitis associated with CFTR mutation in at least one allele
# Additional investigations
Sweat chloride after pilocarpine iontophoresis (chloride >=60 mEq/L supports diagnosis; highly specific)
CFTR mutation analysis / panels of several hundred variants, or complete CFTR sequencing with splice-junction analysis
Immunoreactive trypsinogen (newborn screening)
Fecal elastase-1 (low in exocrine pancreatic insufficiency)
Sputum culture surveillance for resistant pathogens (Burkholderia cepacia complex, Stenotrophomonas, Achromobacter, mucoid P. aeruginosa, atypical mycobacteria, MRSA)
FEV1 (% predicted) for monitoring and transplant referral thresholds
Annual diabetes mellitus testing in adults; monitor liver function and CYP3A drug interactions on modulator therapy
# Red flags
Hemoptysis requiring hospitalization
Pneumothorax requiring hospitalization
Sputum culture growing Burkholderia cepacia complex, Stenotrophomonas maltophilia, Achromobacter, mucoid P. aeruginosa, or atypical mycobacteria (poor prognostic indicators)
Allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis (~5% of patients) suggested by failure to respond to aggressive inpatient antibiotics
FEV1 <30% predicted (threshold for lung transplant referral)
# Pitfalls
Hyperviscosity of eccrine sweat is NOT a feature of CF; the sweat test reflects defective ductal chloride reabsorption, not thick sweat.
Anion flux through CFTR does NOT involve active transport against a concentration gradient.
Genotype does NOT reliably predict respiratory prognosis or longevity, even though some severe alleles predict pancreatic insufficiency.
A subset of patients with F508del respond poorly to triple combination therapy, so modulator benefit is not universal.
HEMT may reduce sputum bacterial density but does NOT eradicate infection; chronic respiratory infection and inflammation persist.
CF has been underdiagnosed outside historically white populations (Eastern Europe, Latin America, Asia, India, and US minorities), often due to rare/poorly characterized variants and limited access to DNA/sweat testing.
# Management
Start CFTR modulator therapy such as elexacaftor-tezacaftor-ivacaftor in eligible genotypes to correct the underlying channel defect
Promote airway clearance with chest physiotherapy and mucoactive agents including inhaled dornase alfa and hypertonic saline to mobilise viscous secretions
Manage chronic airway infection with inhaled antibiotics such as tobramycin or colistin for Pseudomonas, structured eradication regimens, and intravenous antibiotics for pulmonary exacerbations
Treat exocrine pancreatic insufficiency with pancreatic enzyme replacement, fat-soluble vitamin supplementation and a high-energy diet to support growth and nutrition
Screen for and treat cystic-fibrosis-related diabetes with insulin, and manage sinus disease as a reservoir for lower-airway seeding
Undertake regular surveillance with sputum cultures for resistant pathogens, serial lung function and annual diabetes screening
Deliver care through a specialist multidisciplinary cystic fibrosis centre and refer for lung transplantation as lung function declines
Provide genetic counselling and offer carrier testing to relatives for this autosomal recessive disorder
