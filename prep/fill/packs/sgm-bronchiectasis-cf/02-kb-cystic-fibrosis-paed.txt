# Cystic Fibrosis (Paediatric)
Cystic Fibrosis (Paediatric), Respiratory, Paediatrics.
Aliases: CF; cystic fibrosis; paediatric cystic fibrosis.
# Pathophysiology
Mutations in the CFTR gene impair the CFTR chloride and bicarbonate channel at epithelial surfaces. Defective ion transport produces thick, dehydrated secretions across multiple organs. In the airways, viscous mucus impairs mucociliary clearance, promoting chronic infection, neutrophilic inflammation, and progressive bronchiectasis. In the pancreas, ductal obstruction leads to exocrine insufficiency and malabsorption; similar mechanisms affect the intestine, biliary tree, sweat glands (hence high sweat chloride), and reproductive tract.
# Prognosis
Survival has improved dramatically with multidisciplinary care, aggressive infection and nutrition management, and CFTR modulator therapy, with many people now living well into adulthood; prognosis is largely determined by the rate of progressive lung disease and nutritional status.
# Clinical pearls
An autosomal recessive multisystem disorder caused by mutations in the CFTR gene, the most common of which is F508del.
It is a genetic disease, not an infection, though recurrent respiratory infections dominate its course and morbidity.
Suspect it in a child with chronic productive cough, recurrent chest infections, failure to thrive, and greasy, bulky, foul-smelling stools from fat malabsorption.
Meconium ileus in a newborn is highly suggestive and is often the earliest presentation.
Many countries diagnose it through newborn screening, but a positive screen must be confirmed, classically with an elevated sweat chloride test.
Pancreatic exocrine insufficiency is common and requires enzyme replacement and fat-soluble vitamin supplementation, with attention to overall nutrition.
Chronic airway infection, notably with Pseudomonas aeruginosa, drives progressive bronchiectasis and lung function decline.
Care is delivered by a specialist multidisciplinary team, and CFTR modulator therapies have transformed outcomes for eligible genotypes.
# Additional differentials
Primary ciliary dyskinesia
Immunodeficiency with recurrent infections
Asthma with recurrent chest infections
Protracted bacterial bronchitis
Coeliac disease or other causes of malabsorption and failure to thrive
Shwachman-Diamond syndrome
# Additional investigations
Newborn screening (immunoreactive trypsinogen) with confirmatory testing
Sweat chloride testing
CFTR genetic analysis
Assessment of pancreatic function (e.g. faecal elastase)
Respiratory cultures to identify airway pathogens
Chest imaging and, in older children, lung function testing
Nutritional assessment and fat-soluble vitamin levels
# Red flags
Failure to thrive or faltering growth reflecting malabsorption and increased energy demands
New or persistent Pseudomonas aeruginosa infection requiring eradication
Acute pulmonary exacerbation with increased cough, sputum, breathlessness, or falling lung function
Haemoptysis or pneumothorax
Distal intestinal obstruction syndrome or meconium ileus in the newborn
Signs of CF-related diabetes or liver disease emerging over time
# Pitfalls
Attributing recurrent chest infections and loose stools to common childhood illness and missing the diagnosis
Assuming a negative newborn screen fully excludes CF, since screening can miss some cases
Underestimating nutritional needs and failing to optimise pancreatic enzyme and vitamin replacement
Delaying treatment of new Pseudomonas infection when early eradication is most effective
Overlooking emerging complications such as CF-related diabetes and liver disease
# Management
Start CFTR modulator therapy in children of eligible genotype to correct the underlying anion-channel defect
Institute daily airway clearance physiotherapy with mucoactive agents such as dornase alfa and hypertonic saline to clear viscous secretions
Undertake airway culture surveillance with eradication and treatment of Staphylococcus aureus and Pseudomonas aeruginosa, escalating to intravenous antibiotics for exacerbations
Treat exocrine pancreatic insufficiency with enzyme replacement and fat-soluble vitamin supplementation, and provide a high-energy diet to promote growth and prevent failure to thrive
Manage gastrointestinal complications such as meconium ileus and distal intestinal obstruction syndrome, and keep immunisations up to date
Monitor growth, lung function in older children and screen for cystic-fibrosis-related diabetes
Deliver care through a specialist paediatric cystic fibrosis multidisciplinary team, and offer genetic counselling with carrier testing for the family
