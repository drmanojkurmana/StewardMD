# Bronchiectasis
Bronchiectasis, Respiratory, Pulmonology.
Aliases: non-CF bronchiectasis; chronic suppurative lung disease.
# Pathophysiology
A vicious cycle of airway injury drives bronchiectasis: an initial insult (infection, impaired mucociliary clearance, immune deficiency, or obstruction) impairs clearance of secretions, allowing bacterial colonisation. Persistent bacterial presence provokes chronic neutrophilic inflammation with release of proteases and reactive oxygen species that progressively damage the bronchial wall, weakening and dilating the airways. The damaged, dilated airways clear secretions still less effectively, perpetuating infection and inflammation and leading to further structural destruction.
# Prognosis
Prognosis varies widely with the underlying cause and disease extent; many patients remain stable for years with good airway clearance and infection control, but frequent exacerbations, chronic Pseudomonas colonisation, and extensive disease predict faster decline in lung function and higher mortality.
# Clinical pearls
Defined by permanent, abnormal dilatation of the bronchi, presenting with chronic productive cough, copious purulent sputum, and recurrent chest infections
Haemoptysis, breathlessness, and coarse crackles with occasional wheeze are common, and clubbing may be present in advanced disease
High-resolution CT is the diagnostic standard, showing airway dilatation greater than the accompanying vessel (signet-ring sign), lack of tapering, and airway wall thickening
Causes include prior severe infection, cystic fibrosis, primary ciliary dyskinesia, immunodeficiency, ABPA, and obstruction; a cause should be actively sought
Sputum microbiology guides therapy; chronic Pseudomonas aeruginosa colonisation is associated with more frequent exacerbations and worse outcomes
Airway clearance techniques and pulmonary rehabilitation are cornerstones of long-term management
Exacerbations are treated with appropriately targeted antimicrobials guided by prior culture results, and long-term suppressive strategies are considered in frequent exacerbators
Assessment for treatable contributors such as immunoglobulin deficiency or ABPA can meaningfully change management
# Additional differentials
Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease with chronic bronchitis
Cystic fibrosis
Chronic asthma with mucus hypersecretion
Pulmonary tuberculosis and non-tuberculous mycobacterial disease
Allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis
Chronic aspiration
# Additional investigations
High-resolution CT of the chest to confirm and map the extent of airway dilatation
Sputum culture including for mycobacteria and fungi to guide targeted therapy
Immunoglobulin levels and other immune workup to detect underlying immunodeficiency
Testing for cystic fibrosis and, where suspected, ciliary function studies
Aspergillus-specific serology and total IgE to evaluate for ABPA
# Red flags
Massive or recurrent haemoptysis requiring urgent intervention
Rapid clinical decline or systemic sepsis during an exacerbation
