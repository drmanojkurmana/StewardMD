# Pathophysiology
Poisoning is a dose-related adverse effect from exposure to a chemical, drug or other xenobiotic. Responses to a given dose vary with genetic polymorphism, enzyme induction or inhibition by co-ingestants, and acquired tolerance. Toxicity may be local or systemic depending on route, the agent's physicochemical properties and mechanism, and its severity/reversibility depends on the functional reserve of the patient or target organ (influenced by age and pre-existing disease).
# Prognosis
Overall mortality is low (<1% of all poison exposures) but rises to 1-2% among hospitalised patients with intentional (suicidal) overdose or complications of drugs of abuse, who account for most serious poisonings. About 20-25% of exposures need bedside professional evaluation and ~5% need hospitalisation. Acetaminophen is the pharmaceutical most often implicated in fatal poisoning, while carbon monoxide is the leading overall cause of poisoning death (often fatal before discovery). Most patients asymptomatic at 6 h are unlikely to deteriorate.
# Severity classification
Depression - grade 1 lethargic but arousable to voice/touch; grade 2 responds to pain not voice, brainstem reflexes intact; grade 3 unresponsive to pain, depressed brainstem reflexes, reduced tone/respiration/temperature; grade 4 flaccid, absent brainstem reflexes and respirations, depressed cardiovascular signs.
# Clinical pearls
Diagnosis of an unknown poisoning rests on pattern recognition: first classify the overall physiologic state as stimulated, depressed, discordant, or normal, then look for a recognizable toxidrome.
Core temperature is the single most reliable predictor of poor outcome in stimulant (e.g. cocaine) poisoning or in drug/alcohol/GHB withdrawal, so it must be measured even in difficult or combative patients.
Mydriasis is common to all stimulant states but is most marked with anticholinergics (muscarinic loss of pupil reactivity); cocaine and other sympathomimetics enlarge pupils yet retain some light reactivity.
The anticholinergic toxidrome is distinguished from other stimulant states by hot, dry, flushed skin, decreased bowel sounds and urinary retention, whereas sympathomimetic states cause diaphoresis and increased bowel activity.
Miosis points to a depressed state from opioids or cholinergic agents; muscarinic/nicotinic features separate cholinergic poisoning from opioid poisoning.
In opioid and sedative-hypnotic poisoning, vital-sign derangements are secondary to CNS depression and do not appear until consciousness is markedly reduced (grade 3-4 depression); marked cardiovascular depression with preserved consciousness instead suggests a peripherally acting sympatholytic.
Rotatory nystagmus among stimulated patients is essentially specific to phencyclidine and ketamine.
A near-normal exam may reflect a nontoxic exposure, psychogenic illness, or a 'toxic time-bomb' (slowly absorbed, slowly distributed, requiring metabolic activation, or disrupting metabolism); once-daily reformulations have made these increasingly common.
Rapid response of altered mental status to IV dextrose, naloxone or flumazenil is virtually diagnostic of hypoglycemia, opioid poisoning and benzodiazepine intoxication respectively.
Most patients who are or become asymptomatic by 6 h after ingestion are unlikely to develop later toxicity and may be discharged, but longer observation is required after 'toxic time-bomb' ingestions.
# Non infectious mimics
Psychogenic illness / anxiety reaction (mass hysteria, fear of poisoning) producing symptoms inconsistent with the exposure history
Structural CNS lesion - focal neurologic findings are uncommon in poisoning and should prompt evaluation for a structural cause
# Additional investigations
Characterise overall physiologic state and toxidrome from vital signs and neuromuscular exam
Anion gap (raised AGMA in methanol, ethylene glycol, salicylate, or any poisoning causing organ failure, seizures or shock; low anion gap with bromide, calcium, iodine, lithium, magnesium)
Osmolal gap (>10 mmol/L suggests a low-molecular-weight solute such as an alcohol or glycol)
Serum lactate (low relative to anion gap in alcohol/glycol intoxication, high and near the anion gap in hypoperfusion)
Glucose, electrolytes, ketones (hypo-/hyperglycaemia and hypo-/hyperkalaemia patterns can implicate specific agents)
ECG (bradycardia/AV block, QRS and QT prolongation, ventricular tachyarrhythmias suggest specific agent classes)
Abdominal/chest radiograph (radiopaque densities suggest heavy metals, drug packets, iron, enteric-coated or salicylate tablets; CXR for pulmonary edema/aspiration)
Quantitative serum levels (acetaminophen, alcohols incl. ethylene glycol/methanol, anticonvulsants, barbiturates, digoxin, iron, lithium, salicylate, theophylline, carboxyhemoglobin, methemoglobin) guide management
Coagulation (PT/INR) for warfarin/rodenticide risk stratification, though unreliable for direct oral anticoagulant overdose
# Red flags
Temperature elevation (grade 4 stimulation) - most reliable predictor of poor outcome in stimulant poisoning and withdrawal
Coma, seizures or cardiovascular collapse (grade 4 physiologic stimulation or depression)
Focal neurologic findings - uncommon in poisoning and suggest a structural CNS lesion
Suspicion of 'body packing'/'body stuffing' in patients ill after foreign travel or arrest
High blood levels of agents with more toxic metabolites (acetaminophen, ethylene glycol, methanol) signalling need for antidote or dialysis
# Pitfalls
Rapid hospital urine drug-of-abuse screens are screening tests only, cannot confirm the exact substance, and are not diagnostic or forensic; false positives (e.g. fluoroquinolones triggering false-positive opiate screens) and false negatives are common.
A negative drug screen does not exclude poisoning - the agent may be undetectable by the assay (e.g. synthetic cannabinoids/spice, cathinones/bath salts, kratom) or present below the limit of detection at the time of sampling; repeat testing later may turn positive.
Qualitative drug screens are not clinically useful or cost-effective when the patient is asymptomatic or the picture fits the reported history; reserve them for severe or unexplained toxicity (coma, seizures, cardiovascular instability, acidosis, nonsinus rhythms).
Blood levels obtained during absorption/distribution may exceed tissue levels and may not correlate with toxicity.
In polydrug overdose a clear toxidrome may be obscured when different drug classes counteract each other's physiologic effects.
Physostigmine fully reverses anticholinergic poisoning but can also produce some arousal in CNS depression of any cause, so arousal alone is not specific.
Overdose effects begin sooner, peak later and last longer than therapeutic-dose effects, and an agent's published (therapeutic) pharmacokinetics differ from its toxicokinetics in overdose.
# Drug intoxication / poisoning: treatment
Altered consciousness/toxidrome after suspected ingestion; identify the agent and toxidrome; assess ABCs and risk, Admit/observe; ICU if severe.
Resuscitate + specific antidotes + decontamination
# Steps
Resuscitate (airway, breathing, circulation); treat hypoglycaemia and give thiamine where relevant.
Identify the toxidrome; contact a poisons service (e.g. TOXBASE/Poison Centre) for agent-specific advice.
Decontamination (activated charcoal) only if early, airway-safe and appropriate agent.
Specific antidotes where indicated (naloxone, NAC, flumazenil caution, etc.); supportive monitoring.
Enhanced elimination/dialysis for selected toxins; psychiatric assessment after medical stabilisation.
Resuscitate; antidote by toxidrome + poisons advice
