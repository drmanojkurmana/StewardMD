# cortisol producing adenoma
cortisol producing adenoma, Endocrine system, Endocrinology.
Aliases: cortisol-secreting adrenal adenoma; adrenal Cushing syndrome (adenoma); autonomous cortisol-secreting adenoma.
# Pathophysiology
A cortisol-producing adenoma is a monoclonal benign neoplasm of the adrenal zona fasciculata that synthesises glucocorticoid independently of pituitary ACTH. Constitutive activation of the cyclic AMP / protein kinase A signalling pathway, frequently through somatic activating variants in PRKACA (and in some tumours changes in GNAS, CTNNB1 or components of the PKA pathway), drives steroidogenesis regardless of physiological feedback. The resulting chronic cortisol excess suppresses hypothalamic corticotropin-releasing hormone and pituitary ACTH, so circulating ACTH falls and the non-tumorous adrenal tissue, including the contralateral gland, atrophies. Sustained glucocorticoid excess then produces the multisystem features of Cushing syndrome through catabolic effects on skin, muscle and bone, insulin resistance and gluconeogenesis, mineralocorticoid-like and vascular effects raising blood pressure, and immunosuppressive and neuropsychiatric consequences.
# Prognosis
Unilateral adrenalectomy is usually curative for a benign cortisol-producing adenoma, with resolution of hypercortisolism and gradual improvement in metabolic, skeletal and cardiovascular consequences, although recovery of the suppressed hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis may take many months and requires interim steroid replacement. Untreated or long-standing cortisol excess, including mild autonomous secretion, carries excess cardiovascular, metabolic, skeletal and mortality risk.
# Clinical pearls
A benign, ACTH-independent cortical adenoma that autonomously secretes cortisol, producing the commonest surgically curable cause of endogenous, non-pituitary (adrenal) Cushing syndrome
The clinical spectrum ranges from florid Cushing syndrome (central obesity, moon facies, dorsocervical fat pad, wide violaceous striae, proximal myopathy, thin skin and easy bruising) to mild autonomous cortisol secretion detected incidentally on imaging
Because cortisol secretion is autonomous, plasma ACTH is suppressed and the contralateral adrenal and the unaffected ipsilateral cortex undergo atrophy, which is why postoperative glucocorticoid replacement is mandatory
The overnight low-dose dexamethasone suppression test is the pivotal screen: failure of morning cortisol to suppress, together with low or undetectable ACTH, points to an autonomously functioning adrenal source
Loss of circadian rhythm with a raised late-night salivary or midnight serum cortisol, and increased urinary free cortisol, corroborate hypercortisolism; a suppressed DHEA-sulphate is a useful pointer to a benign cortisol-producing adenoma
