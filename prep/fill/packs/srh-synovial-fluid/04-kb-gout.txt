# Gout
Gout, Rheumatology, Rheumatology.
Aliases: gouty arthritis; monosodium urate crystal arthritis; urate arthropathy; acute gout.
# Pathophysiology
Sustained hyperuricaemia, arising more often from renal under-excretion than from urate overproduction, leads to supersaturation of extracellular fluid and precipitation of monosodium urate crystals within joints and soft tissues. The crystals are engulfed by resident phagocytes and activate the NLRP3 inflammasome, driving release of interleukin-1 beta and an intense neutrophil-rich inflammatory response that produces the acute attack. Repeated crystal deposition and low-grade chronic inflammation eventually generate tophi and cause structural joint damage.
# Prognosis
Gout is highly treatable: with effective urate-lowering therapy to target, crystals dissolve, attacks cease, and tophi regress over time; poor adherence or untreated hyperuricaemia, however, leads to recurrent attacks, chronic arthropathy, and progressive joint destruction.
# Clinical pearls
Acute gout classically presents as an abrupt, exquisitely painful monoarthritis, most often of the first metatarsophalangeal joint (podagra), with redness, swelling, and warmth peaking within hours
It is caused by deposition of monosodium urate crystals in and around joints on a background of chronic hyperuricaemia and urate supersaturation
Definitive diagnosis is by joint aspiration and polarised light microscopy showing needle-shaped, strongly negatively birefringent monosodium urate crystals, often seen within phagocytes during an attack
Serum urate can be normal or even low during an acute flare, so a normal value does not exclude gout; the level is best measured or repeated once the attack has settled
Common triggers and associations include alcohol, purine-rich food and fructose, diuretics, chronic kidney disease, obesity, and the metabolic syndrome
