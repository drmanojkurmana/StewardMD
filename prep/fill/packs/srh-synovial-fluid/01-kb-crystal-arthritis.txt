# Crystal arthritis (gout / pseudogout)
Crystal arthritis (gout / pseudogout), Rheumatology.
# Red flags
Septic arthritis must be excluded by aspiration
# Investigations
Joint aspiration + polarised microscopy
Serum urate (off-attack)
# Pathophysiology
Gout arises from an increased body urate pool due to chronic hyperuricemia, leading to supersaturation and crystallization of monosodium urate (MSU) within joints and connective tissue. Hyperuricemia is predominantly due to impaired renal urate excretion (~90%) and less often urate overproduction (~10%); a minority (~10%) of cases reflect inherited metabolic disorders (PRPP synthetase superactivity, HPRT deficiency). Crystal shedding from inert synovial deposits, triggered by metabolic factors, diuresis, rapid urate changes (including urate-lowering therapy initiation), local trauma, or medical illness, activates resident synovial phagocytes (monocytes/macrophages, mast cells) and the NLRP3 inflammasome, generating IL-1beta and other proinflammatory cytokines (IL-6, IL-8, TNF-alpha). Phagocytosis of MSU crystals by neutrophils with a respiratory burst amplifies the acute inflammatory response. Persistent MSU deposition produces tophi (granulomatous inflammation around crystals) and chronic erosive gouty arthritis. In CPPD, increased cartilage inorganic pyrophosphate (largely from extracellular ATP breakdown, regulated by the ANKH transporter) combines with calcium to form CPP crystals; their release similarly drives inflammasome-mediated inflammation, analogous to hyperuricemia in gout. Apatite and calcium oxalate crystals can likewise stimulate synovial cell proliferation and release of proteases, collagenases, and prostaglandin E2, contributing to joint destruction.
# Prognosis
Untreated gout flares typically resolve spontaneously within 1-2 weeks, but recurrent flares over years can progress to chronic gouty arthritis with persistent low-grade synovitis, subcutaneous tophi, joint deformity, and erosive bony destruction. Controlling the underlying chronic hyperuricemia (urate-lowering therapy to a subsaturation target) can prevent recurrent flares and dissolve tophi, generally requiring lifelong therapy. For chronic CPPD there is no means to remove established CPP crystal deposits from cartilage and synovium (unlike urate lowering in gout), and progressive destructive large-joint disease may require joint replacement. Apatite-associated destructive arthropathy (e.g., Milwaukee shoulder) progresses indolently and may also lead to joint replacement.
# Clinical pearls
Initial gout flares are usually monoarticular; the first metatarsophalangeal joint (podagra) is involved in 70-90% of cases, followed by tarsal joints, ankles, and knees. Oligo- and polyarticular flares emerge over time.
Gout flares often begin at night to early morning, are among the most painful conditions humans experience, and the affected joint becomes warm, red, tender, and very swollen, frequently mimicking cellulitis (pseudocellulitis).
Untreated flares usually subside spontaneously within 1-2 weeks, with symptom-free intervals (intercritical gout) until the next episode.
Serum urate can be normal or low during an acute flare because inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 are uricosuric and can lower urate by about 2 mg/dL; seek historical or post-flare urate values instead.
In CPP crystal arthritis (pseudogout) the knee is most commonly affected, followed by the wrist; the first MTP joint (podagra) is rarely involved, a useful distinguishing clue from gout.
Initial acute CPPD attacks tend to last longer than typical gout flares, sometimes weeks to months.
Acute CPPD can be precipitated by trauma, severe medical illness, or surgery (especially parathyroidectomy, which rapidly lowers serum calcium and magnesium and sheds crystals).
Women account for only about 5-20% of gout patients; most are postmenopausal, elderly, and often on diuretics with coexisting osteoarthritis, hypertension, or mild renal insufficiency. Premenopausal gout is rare.
Hyperuricemia in a prepubertal boy should prompt consideration of inherited enzyme defects such as HPRT deficiency (Lesch-Nyhan spectrum) or PRPP synthetase superactivity.
Apatite (basic calcium phosphate) arthropathy can cause an extremely destructive arthropathy of the elderly, classically the shoulder (Milwaukee shoulder), with low synovial fluid leukocyte counts despite dramatic symptoms.
Hyperuricemia and gout are part of the insulin-resistance syndrome and associate with cardiometabolic-kidney comorbidities: obesity, hypertension, type 2 diabetes, myocardial infarction, stroke, CKD, and urate nephrolithiasis.
# Additional differentials
Palindromic rheumatism
Psoriatic arthritis
Osteoarthritis - CPPD pseudo-osteoarthritis variant mimics progressive OA, often in atypical joints (MCP, wrist, elbow, shoulder, ankle)
Rheumatoid arthritis - chronic symmetric CPP synovitis can clinically resemble RA
Neuropathic (Charcot) arthropathy - severe destructive CPPD may radiographically mimic it
# Infection mimics
Cellulitis - acute gout flare with warm, red, swollen periarticular tissue may mimic cellulitis (pseudocellulitis)
Septic arthritis - crystal arthritis closely mimics it; aspiration with Gram stain and culture is essential, especially as bacterial infection can coexist with crystals in synovial fluid
# Non infectious mimics
Acute CPP crystal arthritis (pseudogout) mimics acute gout flares
Apatite (basic calcium phosphate) deposition disease
Calcium oxalate arthropathy - acute presentation may be indistinguishable from MSU, CPP, or apatite crystal disease
Osteoarthritis (pseudo-osteoarthritis pattern of CPPD)
# Additional investigations
Polarized light microscopy of synovial fluid - MSU: needle/rod-shaped, strongly negatively birefringent; CPP: rhomboid/rod-shaped, weakly positively or non-birefringent; apatite: non-birefringent (requires alizarin red S staining or electron microscopy); calcium oxalate: bipyramidal, variable/strong birefringence, alizarin red S positive
Synovial fluid Gram stain and culture to exclude coexisting septic arthritis
Synovial fluid leukocyte count - gout flare typically 5000-75,000/uL; acute CPPD ~24,000/uL (range several thousand to 100,000); apatite and calcium oxalate usually <2000/uL
Arthrocentesis of an unaffected first MTP joint or knee between flares can still demonstrate MSU crystals
Plain radiographs - advanced gout: well-defined erosions with sclerotic/overhanging margins, soft tissue tophi; CPPD: punctate/linear chondrocalcinosis in fibrocartilage or hyaline cartilage
Musculoskeletal ultrasound - double-contour sign over articular cartilage indicating MSU deposition; can also reveal chondrocalcinosis in CPPD
Dual-energy CT - identifies MSU crystals by chemical composition
Baseline labs to assess comorbidities and treatment safety: serum creatinine, liver function tests, hemoglobin, WBC count, HbA1c, lipids
In CPPD patients under 50, evaluate for metabolic/familial causes: serum calcium, phosphorus, alkaline phosphatase, magnesium, iron, transferrin
HLA-B*5801 screening before allopurinol in high-prevalence populations (Southeast Asian, Pacific Islander/Native Hawaiian, Black patients) to reduce risk of severe cutaneous adverse reactions (knowledge point)
# Red flags
Crystal-proven arthritis does not exclude concurrent septic arthritis - bacterial infection can coexist with MSU or CPP crystals in synovial fluid; always Gram stain and culture
Acute CPPD can produce systemic features (fever, chills, raised acute-phase reactants), with low-grade fever in up to 50% and temperatures occasionally as high as 40C - mandates microbial culture to exclude infection
CPPD onset before age 50 warrants workup for underlying metabolic disease (hyperparathyroidism, hemochromatosis, hypophosphatasia, hypomagnesemia) or hereditary CPPD
# Pitfalls
Relying on a normal serum urate during an acute flare to exclude gout - urate is often normal or low at flare onset.
Attributing a destructive 'osteoarthritis' in atypical joints (MCP, wrist, elbow, shoulder, ankle) to primary OA rather than recognizing pseudo-osteoarthritis from CPPD.
Assuming chondrocalcinosis on imaging is always CPPD - calcium oxalate deposition in chronic renal failure is an exception.
Missing apatite arthropathy because individual apatite crystals are not visible on routine polarized light microscopy and need alizarin red S staining or electron microscopy.
Overlooking inherited enzyme defects when hyperuricemia/gout presents in a young patient, particularly a prepubertal boy.
# Confirm
Acute monoarthritis (often 1st MTP/knee) — confirm with joint aspiration showing crystals; ALWAYS exclude septic arthritis.
Outpatient; admit if septic arthritis not excluded.
Acute attack — NSAID, colchicine or corticosteroid (choose by comorbidity)
# Steps
Aspirate the joint — crystals confirm gout/pseudogout; Gram stain/culture EXCLUDES septic arthritis.
Acute attack: NSAID, or colchicine, or corticosteroid (oral/intra-articular) — choose by comorbidity.
Rest, ice, analgesia; continue any established urate-lowering therapy through the attack.
Do NOT start/stop allopurinol during an acute flare for the first time; initiate urate-lowering later with prophylaxis.
Address risk factors (diuretics, alcohol, renal function).
Urate-lowering therapy (after the flare, with prophylaxis)
Confirm crystals; exclude septic arthritis; treat flare

Joint aspiration (crystals + culture)
Serum urate (after flare), renal function
