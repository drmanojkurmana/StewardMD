# Synovial fluid analysis and arthrocentesis (grounding reference: Kelley & Firestein, Harrison, ACR/EULAR criteria)

Rheumatology topic srh-synovial-fluid: overview of pathophysiology, diagnostic criteria, clinical features, and management guidelines.

Arthrocentesis synovial fluid leukocyte count reliably categorizes joint effusions: normal fluid has fewer than 200 WBC/microL, non-inflammatory (osteoarthritis) ranges from 200 to 2,000 WBC/microL, inflammatory (rheumatoid arthritis, crystal arthropathies) spans 2,000 to 50,000 WBC/microL with greater than 50% neutrophils, and septic fluid typically exceeds 50,000 WBC/microL with greater than 75% polymorphs.

Under compensated polarized light microscopy with a red compensator, monosodium urate crystals appear needle-shaped with strong negative birefringence, displaying a brilliant yellow color when aligned parallel to the slow axis of the compensator and blue when perpendicular.

Calcium pyrophosphate dihydrate (CPPD) crystals are rhomboid or rod-shaped with weak positive birefringence under compensated polarized light, turning blue when parallel to the slow axis and yellow when perpendicular.

Basic calcium phosphate (hydroxyapatite) crystals are ultramicroscopic and non-birefringent under light microscopy; identification requires transmission electron microscopy or alizarin red S staining which demonstrates globular clumping, characteristic of Milwaukee shoulder syndrome.

Hemarthrosis reveals grossly bloody or xanthochromic synovial fluid that does not clot if blood was intra-articular prior to aspiration; the presence of fat globules (lipid droplets floating on centrifuged supernatant) confirms an intra-articular fracture communicating with marrow.

Cholesterol crystals appear as large, flat, notched rectangular plates with moderate variable birefringence in chronically inflamed rheumatoid or tuberculous joint effusions, representing lipid breakdown products from chronic synovial tissue necrosis.

Synovial fluid Gram stain identifies organisms in 50% to 75% of non-gonococcal septic arthritis cases but in fewer than 25% of disseminated gonococcal infections, necessitating chocolate agar or Thayer-Martin culture and nucleic acid amplification testing.

Crystal-induced arthritis and true bacterial septic arthritis frequently coexist in the same joint effusion; discovering urate or CPPD crystals never excludes concomitant septic arthritis, and fluid must always undergo microbiological culture.
