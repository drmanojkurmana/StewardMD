# Obstructive sleep apnoea (adult)
Adults
Outpatient clinic, preoperative assessment, ward
Use STOP-Bang to triage but confirm OSA with polysomnography or a home sleep apnoea test; treat OSA with sleepiness, impaired quality of life or hypertension with CPAP or APAP plus education and early adherence support, advise against drowsy driving, and flag suspected OSA before surgery (AASM).
Aliases: OSA; OSAHS; sleep apnea; obstructive sleep apnea; CPAP; APAP; PAP therapy; AHI; home sleep apnoea test; HSAT.
# Recognise
Symptoms: loud habitual snoring, witnessed apnoeas, nocturnal gasping or choking, unrefreshing sleep, excessive daytime sleepiness, morning headache, nocturia and poor concentration.
Risk factors: obesity, large neck, male sex, older age, retrognathia, enlarged tonsils, hypothyroidism, acromegaly, and evening alcohol or sedatives.
Associated conditions: resistant hypertension, AF, heart failure, stroke, type 2 diabetes and pulmonary hypertension. Always ask about sleepiness while driving.
STOP-Bang (1 point each): Snoring, Tiredness, Observed apnoea, high blood Pressure, BMI above 35 kg/m2, Age above 50, Neck circumference above 40 cm, male Gender. Score 0 to 2 low, 3 to 4 intermediate, 5 to 8 high risk.
Epworth Sleepiness Scale above 10 suggests excessive daytime sleepiness.
Questionnaires are for triage only: AASM recommends against diagnosing OSA from clinical tools or questionnaires without a sleep study.
# Diagnostic testing (AASM 2017)
Uncomplicated adults at increased risk of moderate to severe OSA (excessive sleepiness plus two of: loud snoring, witnessed apnoea or gasping, diagnosed hypertension): in-laboratory polysomnography or a technically adequate home sleep apnoea test (HSAT).
If a single HSAT is negative, inconclusive or technically inadequate, perform polysomnography.
Use polysomnography, not HSAT, with significant cardiorespiratory disease, neuromuscular weakness, awake or suspected sleep-related hypoventilation, chronic opioid use, prior stroke or severe insomnia.
A split-night study (diagnosis then PAP titration on the same night) is suggested when clinically appropriate. If polysomnography is negative but suspicion remains, consider a second study.
Severity by apnoea-hypopnoea index (AHI, or respiratory event index on HSAT) per hour: mild 5 to below 15, moderate 15 to below 30, severe 30 or more.
Suspected obesity hypoventilation (BMI 30 or more with daytime sleepiness or breathlessness): check serum bicarbonate; 27 mmol/L or more needs an arterial blood gas for PaCO2.
# Positive airway pressure (AASM 2019)
Recommend PAP for OSA with excessive sleepiness (strong). Suggest PAP for OSA with impaired sleep-related quality of life or with comorbid hypertension (conditional).
Start PAP either with auto-titrating PAP (APAP) at home or with in-laboratory titration in adults without significant comorbidities; use CPAP or APAP for ongoing therapy rather than routine bilevel PAP.
Give education at initiation, then behavioural and troubleshooting support and telemonitoring in the first weeks (mask fit, leaks, humidification, nasal congestion, claustrophobia).
Adherence benchmark widely used: at least 4 hours a night on 70% of nights; review device data (usage, residual AHI, leak) within the first 3 months.
Obesity hypoventilation with persistent hypercapnia on CPAP, or intolerance of high pressures: specialist review for bilevel PAP or NIV.
# Other measures
Weight loss for all overweight or obese patients. Tirzepatide is approved in the US (2024) for moderate to severe OSA with obesity and reduces AHI.
Avoid alcohol, sedatives and opioids near bedtime; positional therapy for supine-predominant OSA; treat nasal obstruction and rhinitis.
Custom, titratable mandibular advancement device (made by a qualified dentist) for patients who cannot tolerate or prefer not to use PAP.
PAP intolerant or unaccepting: discuss referral to a sleep surgeon (upper airway surgery or hypoglossal nerve stimulation) if BMI is below 40, and to a bariatric surgeon if BMI is 35 or more.
# Driving and occupation
Ask every patient about sleepiness while driving and near misses; advise those with excessive sleepiness not to drive until symptoms are controlled on treatment, and document this advice.
Follow national licensing rules: the EU (Directive 2014/85/EU) and UK DVLA require notification and medical assessment for moderate or severe OSA with excessive sleepiness, with driving allowed once symptoms are controlled.
Commercial and professional drivers, pilots and machine operators face stricter standards: expedite diagnosis and confirm adherence with device data.
# Perioperative care
Screen surgical patients with STOP-Bang: 5 or more, or 3 or more with serum bicarbonate 28 mmol/L or higher, indicates high risk of moderate to severe OSA.
Suspected but undiagnosed OSA need not delay surgery unless there is uncontrolled systemic disease or impaired ventilation or gas exchange; manage as presumed OSA.
Known OSA: bring and use PAP before and after surgery; plan for difficult mask ventilation and intubation; favour regional anaesthesia and opioid-sparing multimodal analgesia; extubate fully awake and semi-upright.
After surgery: continuous pulse oximetry for high-risk patients, avoid the supine position, and minimise opioids and sedatives.
