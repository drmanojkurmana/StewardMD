Grounding notes: RET-driven thyroid cancer and the MEN 2 syndromes

Why RET matters
RET is a growth-signal receptor gene on chromosome 10. Germline changes in RET drive three related inherited states: MEN 2A, MEN 2B (also called MEN 3), and familial medullary thyroid carcinoma without other tumors. Nearly every family with these states carries a RET change, so testing relatives for the same change is the practical route to finding who is at risk.

Codon tiers (ATA 2015 revision)
- Highest tier: codon 918 in exon 16, the M918T change. Carriers have the most aggressive disease.
- High tier: codon 634 in exon 11, and codon 883 (A883F) in exon 15. A883F tends to have a better prognosis than M918T.
- Moderate tier: codons 609, 611, 618 and 620 in exon 10, plus others such as 630, 666, 768, 790, 804, 891 and 912.

Timing of prophylactic thyroidectomy
- Highest tier: surgery as soon as possible, and within the first year of life. Harrison's table gives a window ending between 6 months and 1 year. The ATA says the first months of life may be ideal.
- High tier: surgery by age 5, or earlier if calcitonin rises.
- Moderate tier: timing is set by calcitonin and a joint decision with the family, usually around age 5 to 6. If calcitonin is above normal yet still under 30 pg/mL, guidance favors proceeding with the operation.

Screening before surgery
- Pheochromocytoma must be ruled out before any thyroid operation. Screening starts at age 11 for the highest and high tiers, and at age 16 for the moderate tier.
- Parathyroid screening begins at 11 for the high tier and at 16 for moderate-tier carriers. Codon 918 carriers skip it entirely, since MEN 2B lacks this feature.

MEN 2A and MEN 2B features
MEN 2A: medullary thyroid carcinoma in 100 percent of carriers, pheochromocytoma in about half, and primary hyperparathyroidism in 20 to 30 percent. Cutaneous lichen amyloidosis and Hirschsprung disease can occur. Codon 634 accounts for most cases.

MEN 2B: medullary thyroid carcinoma in 100 percent of carriers, and pheochromocytoma in nearly all patients by the textbook text. Mucosal neuromas are usually on the tip of the tongue. Other features include a marfanoid build, corneal nerve changes, intestinal ganglioneuromatosis and megacolon. More than 95 percent of cases involve codon 918. Hyperparathyroidism is absent.

Cell of origin and iodine
Medullary thyroid carcinoma arises from parafollicular C cells, which make calcitonin. C cells do not take up iodine, so radioactive iodine gives no benefit after surgery.

Levothyroxine after thyroidectomy
Levothyroxine replaces the hormone lost with the thyroid and is continued for life. The goal is to keep thyroid function normal. The ATA sees no reason to suppress TSH after surgery for medullary cancer, because the tumor is not a follicular neoplasm. Suppression is a strategy for differentiated cancer, not for this tumor.

Follow-up
Post-operative checks of calcitonin and carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) take place between month 3 and month 6. Two undetectable results point to a low chance of recurrence. Each six-month interval, while the neck remains free of disease, pairs a clinical review with both markers and a neck ultrasound. Calcitonin levels that double faster than every half year flag patients at greatest risk of the disease persisting or returning.

Systemic therapy
TKIs such as vandetanib and cabozantinib act on several targets, while selpercatinib and pralsetinib are RET-selective. Side effects are significant, so clinicians hold these drugs for progressive or symptomatic metastatic disease. A rising calcitonin with negative imaging is managed by observation, periodic imaging and marker checks. Guidance from the ATA argues against systemic treatment when markers climb but no metastasis is documented.
