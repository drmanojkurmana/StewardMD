# Liver Abscess
Liver Abscess, Hepatobiliary.
# Pathogens
Pyogenic liver abscess in this region is frequently caused by Klebsiella pneumoniae, especially in diabetic patients, often via cryptogenic or biliary spread. Amebic liver abscess remains an important and treatable alternative diagnosis with a different specific therapy (metronidazole), requiring serology/aspirate testing to distinguish.

# Reason
Right upper quadrant pain with fever, in the absence of jaundice, raises suspicion for liver abscess (pyogenic or amebic). Empiric antibiotics covering both possibilities should be started pending imaging confirmation and aspiration, since clinical features alone cannot reliably distinguish pyogenic from amebic abscess, particularly in endemic regions.
# Pathophysiology
Pyogenic liver abscesses arise either from haematogenous spread of bacteria or from local spread from contiguous intraperitoneal infection. The organism reaches the liver by several routes: ascending biliary infection (currently the most common associated source), portal venous seeding via suppurative thrombosis of the portal vein (pylephlebitis, classically from pelvic or other intra-abdominal infection), direct contiguous spread, or systemic bacteraemic (haematogenous) seeding. Historically, ruptured appendicitis was the leading source.
# Prognosis
Mortality from pyogenic liver abscess averaged about 15% despite treatment in the data cited; outcome is poorer with delayed source control or factors predicting failed percutaneous drainage. For hypervirulent K. pneumoniae infection, survivors may suffer catastrophic sequelae from metastatic spread, including vision loss (endophthalmitis) and major neurologic deficits.
# Clinical pearls
The liver is the visceral organ most prone to abscess formation; in one series of intraabdominal abscesses, hepatic abscesses accounted for roughly half of all visceral abscesses.
Fever is the most common presenting feature; about half of patients with a liver abscess have no localizing signs (no hepatomegaly, right-upper-quadrant tenderness, or jaundice), so the liver may not be obviously implicated on examination.
In the elderly, liver abscess may present solely as fever of unknown origin, so abdominal imaging (especially of the right upper quadrant) should form part of any FUO workup.
An elevated serum alkaline phosphatase is the single most reliable laboratory abnormality, present in about 70% of patients; bilirubin and aminotransferases are often normal or only modestly raised.
Anaerobes are not typically involved in liver abscesses arising from biliary infection unless prior surgery has occurred.
Candida liver abscess characteristically follows fungaemia in patients receiving chemotherapy and often becomes clinically apparent as neutrophil counts recover after a period of neutropenia.
# Additional differentials
Splenic abscess (different clinical setting and organism profile, most often associated with bacterial endocarditis; high index of suspicion needed as it is frequently fatal if untreated)
Hepatic actinomycosis (Actinomyces israelii can produce multiple hepatic abscesses, sometimes with a contiguous splenic lesion)
Candidal (fungal) liver abscess in immunocompromised/post-chemotherapy patients
# Infection mimics
Amebic liver abscess (Entamoeba histolytica) — a treatable alternative with overlapping right-upper-quadrant pain, fever, and point hepatic tenderness; jaundice is rare and fewer than one-third have active diarrhoea at presentation
# Additional investigations
Imaging is the most reliable diagnostic modality: ultrasonography, CT, MRI, and indium-labelled-WBC or gallium scintigraphy; more than one study may be needed.
Chest radiography may give a clue — a newly elevated right hemidiaphragm, right basilar infiltrate, or right pleural effusion.
Diagnostic aspiration of abscess contents (with Gram stain and culture), together with blood cultures, ideally obtained before starting empirical antibiotics.
Amebic serology is positive in >95% of amebic liver abscess cases and PCR can also be used; negative results help exclude amebiasis. Note enzyme immunoassay for the Gal/GalNAc lectin and LAMP/PCR can also be applied directly to abscess aspirate/pus.
Supportive labs: leukocytosis (~77%), normochromic normocytic anaemia (~50%), hypoalbuminaemia (~33%); concomitant bacteraemia in one-third to one-half of patients.
For suspected hypervirulent K. pneumoniae, the string test for hypermucoviscosity is suggestive but not optimally sensitive or specific; the most accurate identification uses the biomarkers iucA, iroB, peg-344, rmpA, and rmpA2.
# Red flags
Features suggesting hypervirulent K. pneumoniae with metastatic spread — concurrent endophthalmitis (threatening vision loss), meningitis/brain abscess, or septic pulmonary nodules — mandate urgent evaluation of distant sites.
Multiple sizeable abscesses, viscous contents prone to plugging a catheter, presence of yeast, communication with an untreated/obstructed biliary tree, or no clinical response to percutaneous drainage within 4-7 days predict failure of percutaneous drainage and favour surgical intervention.
Mortality remains appreciable (around 15%) despite treatment.
# Pitfalls
Failing to distinguish pyogenic from amebic liver abscess: amebic serology is positive in >95% of amebic cases, amebic abscesses are classically a single right-lobe lesion, and most travellers with amebic abscess present within ~5 months of leaving an endemic area — so travel history, lesion location, and serology should be sought before committing to a purely bacterial diagnosis.
Aspirating an amebic abscess for trophozoites is low-yield: trophozoites reside in the abscess capsule, not in the readily aspirated necrotic ('anchovy-paste') centre, so their absence on aspirate does not exclude amebiasis.
Up to 10% of patients with acute amebic liver abscess have negative serology early on, usually seroconverting within a week — a single early negative test does not exclude the diagnosis.
Assuming anaerobic coverage is always required: anaerobes are generally not involved in abscesses of biliary origin (unless prior surgery), whereas pelvic/intraperitoneal-source abscesses are typically polymicrobial with B. fragilis.
Attributing a cryptogenic pyogenic liver abscess solely to the liver: because biliary, portal (pelvic/appendiceal), and other intra-abdominal infections are leading sources, the underlying source must be sought and addressed.
Overlooking that ampicillin/amoxicillin exposure within the prior 30 days has been associated with increased risk of the hypervirulent K. pneumoniae liver abscess syndrome, presumably via selection for the causative strain.
# Deescalation
If aspirate/serology confirms isolated amebic abscess, discontinue antibacterial agents and continue metronidazole alone (followed by a luminal agent to clear colonic cysts). If purely pyogenic with a known organism, narrow to the single most targeted antibacterial agent.

# Framework
Image-guided aspiration or drainage of abscesses >5cm (or any size with risk of rupture) is an important adjunct to antibiotics, not a replacement — pursue source control in parallel.
Once amebic serology/aspirate confirms a single diagnosis, narrow therapy accordingly (discontinue antibacterial agents if purely amebic; discontinue metronidazole's amebic-dose course if purely pyogenic and anaerobic coverage isn't separately needed).
In diabetic patients with Klebsiella liver abscess, screen for endophthalmitis (a recognized association), since this changes management and prognosis.
