# Acute Bacterial Meningitis
Acute Bacterial Meningitis, Central Nervous System.
# Pathogens
S. pneumoniae and N. meningitidis are the leading causes of community-acquired bacterial meningitis across most age groups. Listeria is specifically added in neonates, those over 50, pregnant patients, and the immunocompromised, since standard empiric regimens do not cover it.
Very likely: Streptococcus pneumoniae; Neisseria meningitidis.
Likely: Listeria monocytogenes (neonates, elderly, immunocompromised, pregnant).
Possible: Haemophilus influenzae (unvaccinated populations); Gram-negative bacilli (post-neurosurgery, immunocompromised).
Red flags: Immediate empiric antibiotics required.
# Investigations
Lumbar puncture with CSF analysis (cell count, protein, glucose, Gram stain, culture)
Confirms the diagnosis, identifies the organism, and guides definitive duration and choice of therapy — but should follow, not precede, empiric antibiotics if there is any delay.
Blood cultures (before antibiotics if no delay incurred)
Frequently positive in bacterial meningitis and may identify the organism if LP is delayed or contraindicated.
# Test
CT head (before LP only if specific indications: papilledema, focal neurological deficit, severely altered consciousness, seizures)
Screens for mass effect/herniation risk before LP in specific high-risk presentations — not required routinely and should not delay antibiotics.
