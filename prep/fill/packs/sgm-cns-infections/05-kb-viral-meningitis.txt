# Viral (Aseptic) Meningitis
Viral (Aseptic) Meningitis, Central Nervous System.
# Pathogens
Enteroviruses cause the large majority of viral meningitis, typically with a more benign, self-limited course than bacterial meningitis. However, clinical differentiation from early bacterial meningitis is unreliable, which is why CSF analysis (lymphocytic pleocytosis, normal glucose, negative Gram stain/culture in viral meningitis) is essential rather than optional.
Very likely: Enteroviruses (most common cause overall).
Likely: Herpes simplex virus type 2; Varicella zoster virus.
Possible: HIV (acute seroconversion); Mumps (in unvaccinated populations).
# Investigations
Lumbar puncture with CSF cell count, protein, glucose, Gram stain, culture
The decisive test distinguishing viral from bacterial meningitis and guiding the decision to continue or stop empiric antibacterial therapy.
CSF viral PCR (enterovirus, HSV)
Identifies the specific viral cause; HSV PCR specifically changes management since HSV meningitis/encephalitis is treated with acyclovir.
Blood cultures
Performed alongside LP workup to support the broader diagnostic picture, particularly if bacterial meningitis cannot yet be excluded.

# Reason
Fever with neck stiffness and headache, WITHOUT altered consciousness, petechial rash, or seizure, raises the possibility of viral (aseptic) meningitis rather than bacterial meningitis. However, this distinction cannot be made on clinical grounds alone with confidence — lumbar puncture with CSF analysis is required, and empiric antibacterial therapy should generally still be started pending results unless bacterial meningitis can be confidently excluded by an experienced clinician, since the consequences of missing bacterial meningitis are severe.
# Pathophysiology
Viral meningitis is inflammation of the meninges, most often caused by enteroviruses, with a self-limited course in immunocompetent hosts. Inflammation is largely confined to the meninges, producing meningeal irritation without the parenchymal brain involvement that characterizes encephalitis; preservation of higher cerebral function distinguishes meningitis from encephalitis. CSF oligoclonal IgG bands directed against viral proteins can occur, though they are nonspecific.
# Prognosis
In adults the prognosis for full recovery from viral meningitis is excellent; treatment of almost all cases is primarily symptomatic. Rare adult patients have persisting headache, mild mental impairment, incoordination, or generalized asthenia for weeks to months. Outcome is less certain in infants and neonates (<1 year), in whom intellectual impairment, learning disabilities, hearing loss, and other lasting sequelae have been reported. Enteroviral meningitis outside the neonatal period is usually benign with recovery without sequelae, but chronic/severe infection can occur in neonates and in people with hypo- or agammaglobulinemia.
# Severity classification
Immunocompetent patients with a classic CSF profile (lymphocytic pleocytosis, normal glucose, negative Gram stain), no focal signs, and no significant alteration in consciousness may not require hospitalization if home monitoring and follow-up are assured. Hospitalization is indicated for immunocompromised patients, those with significant alteration in consciousness, seizures, or focal signs suggesting encephalitis/parenchymal involvement, and those with an atypical CSF profile.
# Clinical pearls
Profound alterations in consciousness (stupor, coma, marked confusion), seizures, focal neurologic signs, or neuroimaging evidence of parenchymal involvement do NOT occur in viral meningitis; their presence points instead to encephalitis or another CNS process.
Headache is almost invariably present, often frontal or retroorbital, with photophobia and pain on eye movement; nuchal rigidity is usually present but may be mild, appearing only near the limit of neck anteflexion.
A specific viral cause is identified in 60-90% of cases using combined CSF PCR, culture, and serology; roughly two-thirds of culture-negative 'aseptic' meningitis cases have a viral etiology found by CSF PCR.
Enteroviruses account for >85% of cases with an identified etiology and predominate in summer and fall, especially in children <15 years.
HSV-2 causes most adult uncomplicated HSV meningitis (HSV-1 causes ~90% of HSV encephalitis); ~20% of HSV meningitis patients develop recurrent attacks, and most recurrent/'aseptic' meningitis, including cases formerly called Mollaret's meningitis, is due to HSV.
Many HSV meningitis patients give no history and show no active genital herpes lesions at presentation, so absence of genital lesions does not exclude the diagnosis.
VZV is increasingly recognized as a cause of meningitis even WITHOUT rash, so absence of chickenpox or shingles does not exclude it.
Cranial nerve palsies (most often V, VII, or VIII) are more common in HIV meningitis than in other viral meningitides; HIV seroconversion may be delayed, so seronegative suspected cases warrant repeat testing.
Mumps meningitis is suggested by parotitis, orchitis, oophoritis, pancreatitis, or raised serum amylase/lipase, but their absence does not exclude it; a documented prior mumps infection (lifelong immunity) does exclude it.
Consider LCMV when aseptic meningitis occurs in late fall/winter with exposure to house mice or pet/laboratory rodents or their excreta, particularly when accompanied by leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, or abnormal liver function tests.
# Additional differentials
Arthropod-borne (arbovirus) meningitis, notably West Nile virus; consider when meningitis/encephalitis clusters occur in a restricted region in summer/early fall
Epstein-Barr virus (may produce aseptic meningitis with or without infectious mononucleosis; atypical lymphocytes in CSF/blood are suggestive)
Human herpesvirus 6 (HHV-6)
Cytomegalovirus (notably in immunocompromised hosts, where it may cause PMN pleocytosis with low glucose)
HSV-1 (less common cause of meningitis than HSV-2)
Lymphocytic choriomeningitis virus (LCMV)
Mumps (late winter/early spring, male predominance ~3:1)
Zika and other non-WNV arboviruses
Human parechovirus (included on the FilmArray ME panel)
# Infection mimics
Untreated or partially treated bacterial meningitis (a key pitfall when CSF shifts toward lymphocytic predominance)
Early fungal meningitis (lymphocytic pleocytosis common; hypoglycorrhachia may be absent early)
Early mycobacterial / tuberculous meningitis
Neurosyphilis (Treponema pallidum)
Mycoplasma pneumoniae CNS infection
Listeria spp.
Brucella spp.
Coxiella spp.
Leptospira spp.
Rickettsia spp.
Parameningeal infections
# Non infectious mimics
Neoplastic meningitis
Medication-induced hypersensitivity (drug-induced) meningitis
Systemic lupus erythematosus and other rheumatologic diseases
Sarcoidosis
Behcet's syndrome
Uveomeningitic syndromes
# Additional investigations
CSF reverse transcriptase PCR (RT-PCR) for enteroviruses: diagnostic procedure of choice, highest sensitivity if done within 48 h of symptom onset, falling rapidly after day 5
FilmArray Meningitis/Encephalitis multiplex PCR panel covering HSV-1, HSV-2, enteroviruses, VZV, HHV-6, and human parechovirus (does NOT include CMV, WNV, LCMV, or EBV)
WNV-specific CSF IgM (more sensitive than CSF RT-PCR for WNV; RT-PCR ~70% sensitive, useful mainly in immunocompromised patients with poor antibody response)
Arboviral serology demonstrating CSF viral-specific IgM or seroconversion; repeat testing if early study is negative and suspicion is high
Throat washing and stool PCR/culture to support enteroviral (and Mycoplasma) diagnosis (stool/throat shedding may persist for weeks)
Demonstration of intrathecal HSV-specific or VZV-specific antibody synthesis (less sensitive/specific than PCR; may take >1 week to become positive)
Routine bloods: complete blood count with differential, liver and renal function tests, ESR, C-reactive protein, electrolytes, glucose, creatine kinase, aldolase, amylase, and lipase
Neuroimaging (MRI preferred over CT) when there is altered consciousness, seizures, focal signs, atypical CSF profile, or immunocompromise (not mandatory in uncomplicated viral meningitis)
Serum procalcitonin >0.5 ng/mL as a clue favoring bacterial over aseptic meningitis
# Red flags
Depressed or markedly altered consciousness (stupor, coma, marked confusion) — suggests encephalitis or alternative diagnosis, not simple viral meningitis
Seizures
Focal neurologic signs/symptoms or neuroimaging abnormalities indicating parenchymal brain involvement
CSF PMN pleocytosis without an established diagnosis — should prompt consideration of bacterial or parameningeal infection
Lymphocytic pleocytosis WITH low CSF glucose (hypoglycorrhachia) — should raise suspicion of fungal, tuberculous, or Listeria meningoencephalitis, or noninfectious causes such as sarcoid or neoplastic meningitis
Atypical CSF profile or immunocompromising condition/treatment — warrants hospitalization and neuroimaging
# Pitfalls
Failing to recognize partially or incompletely treated bacterial meningitis, which can produce a lymphocytic-predominant CSF that mimics viral meningitis
Up to 15% of enteroviral meningitis patients (most often young infants) have a NORMAL CSF leukocyte count, so a normal cell count does not exclude the diagnosis
PMNs may predominate during the first 48 h of viral meningitis (e.g., echovirus 9, WNV, EEE, mumps); WNV PMN pleocytosis occurs in ~45% and can persist a week or more before shifting to lymphocytes — do not assume early neutrophilia means bacterial infection
Treating a normal CSF glucose as ruling out viral meningitis is wrong, but conversely low CSF glucose can occur in 10-30% of mumps or LCMV and rarely with enteroviruses, HSV-2, or VZV
CSF viral culture has generally poor sensitivity for viral meningitis (unlike its utility in bacterial infection), so reliance on culture alone underdiagnoses
Enterovirus detected in stool is NOT diagnostic of acute CNS infection — it may reflect residual shedding from a prior infection or asymptomatic carriage during epidemics
Enteroviral RT-PCR has low sensitivity for EV71; specific testing may be required
EBV CSF PCR false-positives may reflect viral reactivation or latent viral DNA in recruited lymphocytes rather than true EBV meningitis
Routine serology is unhelpful for high-seroprevalence viruses (HSV, VZV, CMV, EBV)
# Deescalation
Discontinue empiric antibacterial coverage once CSF profile (lymphocytic pleocytosis, normal glucose) and negative Gram stain/culture support a viral cause, typically within 24-48 hours.
# Framework
Do NOT withhold empiric antibacterial coverage (ceftriaxone +/- vancomycin) on clinical grounds alone unless an experienced clinician is confident bacterial meningitis is excluded — clinical distinction from early bacterial meningitis is unreliable.
CSF analysis is the deciding test: bacterial meningitis typically shows neutrophilic pleocytosis with low glucose and high protein, while viral meningitis typically shows lymphocytic pleocytosis with normal glucose.
Once CSF profile and Gram stain/culture support a viral etiology, discontinue empiric antibacterial therapy — prolonged unnecessary antibiotics are a common stewardship failure once viral meningitis is confirmed.
HSV PCR should be considered on CSF if HSV is a possibility, as this would change management (add acyclovir) even though most viral meningitis is enteroviral and self-limited.
