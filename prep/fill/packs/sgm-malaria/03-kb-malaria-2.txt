# Malaria
Malaria, Tropical Fever.
# Pathogens
P. falciparum and P. vivax account for the large majority of malaria in India; species identification on smear/RDT is essential since management, monitoring for severity, and radical cure (primaquine for P. vivax) differ significantly by species.
Very likely: Plasmodium falciparum; Plasmodium vivax.
# Likely
Plasmodium malariae
Plasmodium ovale (less common in South Asia)
# Possible
Mixed infections (P. falciparum + P. vivax)
# Investigations
Peripheral blood smear (thick and thin film)
Gold standard for diagnosis, species identification, and parasitemia quantification — directly determines treatment choice and monitoring.
Rapid diagnostic test (malaria antigen)
Fast bedside screening tool, useful when smear is not immediately available, though species specificity is more limited than smear.
CBC
Thrombocytopenia is a common supportive finding; hemoglobin helps assess severity (severe anemia is a marker of complicated malaria).
Renal function tests, blood glucose, LFTs
Screens for organ dysfunction that would classify this as severe/complicated malaria requiring IV therapy and higher level of care.
Lactate / blood gas (if unwell), Acidosis is a severity marker in falciparum malaria with prognostic significance.
Antibiotics NOT indicated
# Reason
Fever with chills/rigors, thrombocytopenia and/or splenomegaly in a patient from or travelling through an endemic area is most consistent with malaria — a protozoal infection. Antibiotics have no role here; the priority is urgent peripheral smear/rapid diagnostic testing and prompt antimalarial therapy specific to the Plasmodium species identified, since delay (especially in P. falciparum) can progress rapidly to severe/complicated malaria.
# Pathophysiology
Infection begins when a female Anopheles inoculates sporozoites, which reach the liver and undergo preerythrocytic schizogony, each sporozoite yielding 10,000 to >30,000 merozoites. Released merozoites invade red cells, becoming trophozoites that multiply roughly 6- to 20-fold each asexual cycle (every 48 h for P. falciparum/P. vivax/P. ovale, 72 h for P. malariae, 24 h for P. knowlesi); symptoms begin around densities of ~50 parasites/uL. In P. vivax and P. ovale a fraction of liver forms persist as dormant hypnozoites for weeks to a year or more, causing the relapses characteristic of these species (P. falciparum, P. malariae, and P. knowlesi do not relapse). Disease results from red-cell invasion and destruction plus the host inflammatory response: schizont rupture releases material that activates monocytes/macrophages and proinflammatory cytokines, producing fever. The central pathology of severe falciparum malaria is cytoadherence: ~12-15 h after invasion 'knobs' on the infected red cell extrude the variant adhesin PfEMP1, which binds endothelial receptors (ICAM-1 and endothelial protein C receptor in brain, VAR2CSA/chondroitin sulfate A in placenta, CD36 elsewhere). Cytoadherence, rosetting (adherence to uninfected red cells), and agglutination sequester mature parasites in vital organs, especially the brain, obstructing microcirculatory flow and metabolism while escaping splenic clearance. Uninfected red cells also become less deformable in severe disease. P. falciparum invades cells of all ages and can reach very high densities (P. knowlesi can also, owing to its 24-h cycle), whereas other species spare most red cells and rarely exceed ~2% parasitemia. The spleen augments clearance and 'pits' damaged ring forms; severe lactic acidosis arises from anaerobic glycolysis in sequestered beds, parasite lactate production, gut-derived acids, and failed hepatic/renal clearance, and hypoglycemia from impaired gluconeogenesis with increased glucose consumption.
# Prognosis
Promptly and appropriately treated uncomplicated falciparum malaria has a mortality of <0.1%, but risk rises steeply once vital-organ dysfunction occurs or parasitemia exceeds 2%. Cerebral malaria carries roughly 20% mortality in adults and 15% in children even with treatment; about 10% of surviving children (especially with hypoglycemia, severe anemia, repeated seizures, or deep coma) have residual neurologic deficits, most of which improve or resolve within 6 months, though persistent language and cognitive deficits, increased epilepsy risk, and reduced life expectancy occur in some. Noncardiogenic pulmonary edema in adults has >80% mortality. Plasma bicarbonate and lactate are the best biochemical prognosticators. Among complications, poor prognosis is indicated by >20% mature pigment-containing parasites or >5% of neutrophils containing pigment, and by hyperparasitemia. In severe acute renal failure that survives, urine flow returns in a median of 4 days and creatinine normalizes in a mean of 17 days; early dialysis improves survival.
# Severity classification
Severe falciparum malaria is defined by one or more of: unarousable coma/cerebral malaria (no localizing response, coma >30 min after a generalized convulsion, Glasgow Coma Score <11 or Blantyre <3 in young children); acidemia/acidosis (arterial pH <7.25, base deficit >8 meq/L, or plasma bicarbonate <15 mmol/L; venous lactate >=5 mmol/L; deep labored 'respiratory distress' breathing); severe normochromic normocytic anemia (hematocrit <15% or Hb <50 g/L with parasite density >10,000/uL); renal failure (creatinine >265 umol/L / >3 mg/dL); noncardiogenic pulmonary edema/ARDS (or O2 sat <92% on room air with respiratory rate >30/min); hypoglycemia (glucose <2.2 mmol/L / <40 mg/dL); hypotension/shock (systolic BP <70 mmHg in children 1-5 y or <80 mmHg in adults with impaired perfusion); significant bleeding/DIC; convulsions (>2 generalized seizures in 24 h); hemoglobinuria (macroscopic black/brown/red urine, not due to oxidant drugs or G6PD deficiency); extreme weakness/prostration; hyperparasitemia (>5% in nonimmune, >10% in any patient); and jaundice (bilirubin >50 umol/L / >3 mg/dL) with parasitemia >100,000/uL or other vital-organ dysfunction. Note: criteria thresholds for renal failure are for adults; lower values define severe disease in children.
# Clinical pearls
Malaria is not a clinical diagnosis: any patient in or from an endemic area with fever needs thick and thin blood smears (or a rapid test if reliable microscopy is unavailable) to confirm infection and identify the species.
Early symptoms are nonspecific (malaise, headache, fatigue, abdominal discomfort, myalgia, then fever) and resemble a minor viral illness, so a high index of suspicion is essential.
The classic regular febrile paroxysms (chills, rigors, fever spikes at fixed intervals) are now unusual and, when present at presentation, suggest P. vivax or P. ovale (often a relapse); prompt effective treatment prevents the synchronized fever pattern from developing.
Mild thrombocytopenia is usual in malaria; a normal platelet count should call the diagnosis into question.
In falciparum malaria, peripheral parasitemia underestimates true parasite burden because mature parasites sequester in deep vascular beds and only younger ring forms circulate.
A PfHRP2-based rapid test can stay positive for weeks after treatment, which helps diagnose severe malaria in a patient who has already taken antimalarials and cleared peripheral parasites but is a drawback in high-transmission settings.
Persistent gametocytemia after a full treatment course does not indicate drug resistance or a need to re-treat, because mature P. falciparum gametocytes are unaffected by most antimalarials.
Any obtundation, delirium, abnormal behavior, or generalized seizure in falciparum malaria must be taken seriously as a possible herald of cerebral malaria.
Cerebral malaria is a diffuse symmetric encephalopathy without true meningism; focal neurologic signs are unusual and signs of meningeal irritation are absent.
Specific retinal changes (patchy retinal opacification/whitening, vessel decolorization, pale-centered hemorrhages) on funduscopy support a diagnosis of cerebral malaria.
In severe malaria the usual adrenergic warning signs of hypoglycemia (sweating, tachycardia, gooseflesh) are absent and its neurologic effects cannot be distinguished from the disease itself, so glucose must be checked actively.
Quinine can provoke hypoglycemia by stimulating pancreatic insulin secretion, compounding the hypoglycemia of severe malaria.
Noncardiogenic pulmonary edema may appear even after several days of effective antimalarial therapy and is readily precipitated by over-vigorous IV fluids; mortality exceeds 80%.
Acidotic ('respiratory distress') breathing in a child with severe anemia is usually due to metabolic acidosis, not 'anemic heart failure' as previously mislabeled.
Approximately 6% of children and <1% of adults with severe malaria have concomitant bacteremia (higher with >20% parasitemia in adults), and nontyphoidal Salmonella bacteremia is specifically associated with P. falciparum.
In high-transmission areas low-density parasitemia is a common incidental finding, so severe malaria is easily overdiagnosed and must be distinguished from sepsis with coincidental parasitemia.
Sudden delayed hemolysis can follow artesunate treatment of hyperparasitemia in nonimmune patients, from synchronous loss of once-parasitized 'pitted' red cells.
# Additional differentials
Acute viral illness / minor febrile viral syndrome (early nonspecific malaria mimics this)
Bacterial meningitis (severe headache in malaria, but neck stiffness and photophobia are absent in malaria)
Sepsis / bacteremia with incidental parasitemia, especially in high-transmission areas
# Infection mimics
Dengue fever (myalgia is prominent but usually more severe in dengue)
Leptospirosis (muscle tenderness, which malaria lacks; petechial hemorrhages a feature)
Typhus/rickettsial illness (tender muscles, unlike malaria)
Viral hemorrhagic fevers (petechial hemorrhages, which are very rare in malaria)
Nontyphoidal Salmonella and other bacteremia complicating or coinciding with malaria
# Non infectious mimics
Diabetic ketoacidosis / ketoacidosis (acidotic 'respiratory distress' breathing overlaps with severe malaria acidosis; ketoacidosis can contribute in children)
Hematologic malignancy / lymphoproliferative disorder (hyperreactive malarial splenomegaly with massive splenomegaly and pancytopenia can be mistaken for it)
# Additional investigations
Thick blood film (concentrates parasites ~40-100x over thin film, increasing sensitivity; examine 100-200 fields before calling negative)
Thin blood film for species identification and parasite quantification (parasitized cells per 1000 RBCs)
Giemsa (pH 7.2) or other Romanowsky stain; acridine orange for rapid detection of low-level parasitemia
Rapid diagnostic tests detecting PfHRP2, parasite LDH, or aldolase (note PfHRP2/3 gene-deletion false negatives and that RDTs do not quantify parasitemia)
PCR / ultrasensitive PCR for species confirmation and detection of low-density/asymptomatic infection (reference setting; too sensitive for primary diagnosis in endemic areas)
Plasma lactate and bicarbonate (best biochemical prognosticators in severe malaria)
Coagulation studies (PT, aPTT, fibrinogen) and platelet count in severe disease
Assessment of malaria pigment in neutrophils (>5% pigment-containing) and proportion of mature pigmented trophozoites/schizonts as severity/prognostic markers
# Red flags
Impaired consciousness, delirium, abnormal behavior, or unarousable coma (cerebral malaria)
Generalized or repeated convulsions (>2 in 24 h), or subtle seizure activity such as tonic-clonic eye movements
Acidotic deep ('respiratory distress') breathing, hyperlactatemia >=5 mmol/L, or acidemia (arterial pH <7.25)
Hypoglycemia (plasma glucose <2.2 mmol/L, <40 mg/dL)
Hypotension/shock with impaired perfusion
Severe anemia (hematocrit <15% or Hb <50 g/L with high parasitemia; Hb <=3 g/dL on presentation carries increased mortality)
Macroscopic hemoglobinuria / blackwater fever (black, brown, or red urine)
Acute kidney injury (creatinine >265 umol/L, >3 mg/dL) or anuria
Noncardiogenic pulmonary edema / ARDS or O2 saturation <92% with tachypnea
Spontaneous bleeding / DIC
Hyperparasitemia (>5% in nonimmune, >10% in any patient) or >100,000/uL
Prostration / inability to sit or stand unaided
Low core temperature <36.5C, marked agitation, jaundice (bilirubin >50 umol/L) with vital-organ dysfunction
# Pitfalls
Treating malaria as a clinical diagnosis; confirmation by smear or rapid test is required.
Accepting a normal platelet count without question, as mild thrombocytopenia is the rule in malaria.
Relying on peripheral parasitemia to gauge true burden in falciparum malaria, since mature parasites are sequestered.
Overdiagnosing severe malaria in high-transmission areas where low-density parasitemia is a common incidental finding, missing concurrent sepsis.
Over-vigorous IV fluid administration, which can precipitate noncardiogenic pulmonary edema.
Missing hypoglycemia because its usual autonomic signs are absent in severe disease and its neurologic effects mimic malaria itself.
Re-treating on the basis of persistent gametocytemia, which does not signify drug resistance after a full course.
False-negative RDTs from PfHRP2/3 gene-deleted parasites (notably in the Horn of Africa); RDTs also cannot quantify parasitemia.
Using serology to diagnose acute illness; serology has no role in acute diagnosis.
Mistaking acidotic breathing in a severely anemic child for 'anemic congestive cardiac failure'.
# Deescalation
Not applicable to antibiotics. Once species is confirmed, ensure species-appropriate antimalarial therapy is given (e.g., artemisinin-based combination therapy for P. falciparum per national guidelines; chloroquine plus primaquine for P. vivax in chloroquine-sensitive regions, with G6PD testing before primaquine).
# Framework
This is a parasitic, not bacterial, infection — antibiotics provide no benefit and should not be given for malaria itself.
Confirm species by peripheral smear (gold standard, also allows parasite density quantification) or rapid diagnostic test before starting species-specific antimalarial therapy.
Watch closely for severity criteria (P. falciparum): impaired consciousness, acidosis, hypoglycemia, severe anemia, renal impairment, pulmonary edema, significant bleeding, shock, or high parasitemia — any of these mandate IV artesunate and higher level of care, not oral therapy.
