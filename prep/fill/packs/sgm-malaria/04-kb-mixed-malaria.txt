# Mixed Malaria (P. falciparum + P. vivax)
Mixed Malaria (P. falciparum + P. vivax), Tropical Fever.
# Pathogens
By definition, mixed malaria involves confirmed co-infection with both species on smear/RDT/PCR — management must address the more dangerous P. falciparum component fully (as the primary driver of severe disease risk) while also providing radical cure for P. vivax (including primaquine for hypnozoite clearance after G6PD testing).
Very likely: Plasmodium falciparum; Plasmodium vivax.
# Red flags
Urgent antimalarial therapy required (NOT an antibiotic)
# Investigations
Peripheral blood smear (thick and thin film) confirming both species
Definitive method to confirm mixed infection and quantify parasitemia of each species, directly determining treatment approach and monitoring intensity.
Rapid diagnostic test (combination P.f/P.v if available), Can support or suggest mixed infection rapidly, though smear remains the definitive confirmatory test.
G6PD testing, Mandatory before primaquine administration to avoid precipitating hemolysis in deficient patients.
CBC, renal function, blood glucose, LFTs
Screens for organ dysfunction that would classify this as severe/complicated malaria requiring IV therapy and escalated care.
Urgent antimalarial therapy required (NOT an antibiotic)
# Reason
Confirmed mixed infection with both P. falciparum and P. vivax on peripheral smear/RDT requires therapy that addresses both species — treating only one (e.g., chloroquine alone, which would undertreat P. falciparum in most regions) risks treatment failure and progression of the untreated species. As with single-species malaria, this is a parasitic infection where conventional antibiotics play NO role. The "red" urgency here reflects the need for immediate antimalarial therapy given the P. falciparum component's severe disease risk — it does not mean antibiotics are indicated.
# Pathophysiology
Sporozoites inoculated by anopheline mosquitoes reach the liver and undergo pre-erythrocytic schizogony; a single sporozoite yields thousands of merozoites that invade RBCs and multiply (every 48 h for P. falciparum and P. vivax). In P. vivax (and P. ovale) infection, a fraction of intrahepatic forms do not divide but persist as dormant hypnozoites for weeks to a year or more, later reactivating to cause relapses — a feature absent in P. falciparum. In a mixed P. falciparum + P. vivax infection, the falciparum component drives the risk of severe disease through cytoadherence (PfEMP1 binding endothelial receptors), rosetting, and agglutination, causing sequestration of mature parasites in vital organs; only ring forms circulate, so peripheral parasitemia underestimates true burden. P. vivax preferentially invades young reticulocytes and produces lower parasitemia (seldom >2%), while P. falciparum invades RBCs of all ages and can reach very high densities. The simultaneous presence of a vivax hypnozoite reservoir means blood-stage cure of both species does not prevent later vivax relapse.
# Prognosis
Promptly treated uncomplicated falciparum malaria has a mortality of <0.1%, but risk rises steeply once vital-organ dysfunction occurs or parasitemia exceeds ~2%. In a mixed infection the falciparum component dictates the severe-disease prognosis; cerebral malaria carries ~20% mortality in adults and ~15% in children even with treatment, and ~10% of child survivors have residual neurologic deficits (most improving within 6 months). Poor-prognosis features include hyperparasitemia (>100,000/uL), predominance of mature pigment-containing parasites (>20%), pigment in >5% of neutrophils, hypoglycemia, hyperlactatemia, acidemia, and coagulopathy. The vivax component does not usually drive mortality but, if its hypnozoite reservoir is not addressed, predisposes to later relapse and recurrent anemia.
# Severity classification
Uncomplicated disease = patient can sit/stand unaided and swallow, with no vital-organ dysfunction and parasitemia <2%.
# Clinical pearls
Mixed infections (most commonly P. falciparum together with P. vivax) are frequently under-detected by light microscopy and rapid antigen tests; PCR is the most sensitive method and is increasingly used specifically for speciation and genotyping in mixed infections.
