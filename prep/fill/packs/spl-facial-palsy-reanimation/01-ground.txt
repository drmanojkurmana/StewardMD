Facial nerve injury robs the face of expression, eye closure, and oral competence, so management follows a strict ladder from protection through nerve repair to muscle replacement. The House-Brackmann system grades function from Grade I, which is normal symmetry in all movements, down through partial weakness grades to Grade VI, which is total paralysis without any tone.

Before any reconstruction is planned, the exposed cornea must be saved. Lagophthalmos leaves the eye unable to close, so corneal protection becomes the immediate priority through lubricating drops and ointment, moisture chambers at night, and upper eyelid loading with a platinum or gold weight that lets gravity close the lid.

When the nerve is sharply divided, time is short. Acute facial nerve transection within 72 hours is treated by primary direct epineurial end-to-end suture when the stumps meet without tension. Longer gaps need cable grafting with sural nerve or great auricular nerve segments bridging the defect.

If the nerve cannot be repaired directly but the mimetic muscles remain viable, nerve transfers borrow an expendable motor source. Within 18 to 24 months of denervation, the masseteric nerve, which is the motor nerve to masseter from V3, or the hypoglossal nerve XII can be rerouted to the facial branches, restoring a strong voluntary smile driven by biting or tongue movement.

Beyond 2 years the motor endplates are gone and no nerve transfer can wake the old muscles. Irreversible atrophy then requires functional free muscle transfer, and microvascular gracilis muscle transfer is the global gold standard, bringing its own blood supply and obturator nerve for coaptation.

Spontaneity needs input from the healthy side. A cross-face nerve graft uses a long sural nerve passed across the face and coapted to healthy contralateral buccal branches, so an emotional smile on the normal side drives the reconstructed side. Axons need 9 to 12 months to cross the graft before movement appears.

The modern workhorse combines both drives at once. Dual-innervation gracilis transfer wires the transplanted muscle to the masseteric nerve for immediate bite-driven power and to a cross-face graft for later emotional spontaneity. Patients bite to smile early, then the healthy side takes over the emotional component.

When dynamic options are exhausted or the patient cannot tolerate long surgery, static slings hold the face up.
