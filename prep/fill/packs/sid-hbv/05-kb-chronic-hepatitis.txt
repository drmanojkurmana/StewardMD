# Chronic Hepatitis
Chronic Hepatitis, Hepatology.
Aliases: chronic hepatitis B; chronic hepatitis C; autoimmune hepatitis.
# Pathophysiology
Chronic hepatitis comprises liver disorders of varying cause in which hepatic inflammation and necrosis persist for at least 6 months. Broad categories include chronic viral hepatitis (B, B plus D, C), drug- or toxin-induced chronic hepatitis, alcohol-associated liver disease, metabolic dysfunction-related (steatotic) liver disease, and autoimmune chronic hepatitis; idiopathic/cryptogenic cases are also believed largely autoimmune. In chronic hepatitis B, all forms can be progressive but progression occurs primarily in patients with active HBV replication, and HCC risk is highest with continued high-level replication. In autoimmune hepatitis, progressive injury results from a cell-mediated immunologic attack on hepatocytes in the setting of lost/failed tolerance for self-liver antigens; predisposition is inherited (HLA-B8, DR3, DR4, DRB1*0301/*0401) while liver specificity is triggered by environmental factors (chemicals, drugs such as minocycline, viruses, and reported triggers including COVID-19 and COVID-19 vaccination); molecular mimicry activates infiltrating CD4+ T cells and impaired regulatory CD4+CD25+ T-cell control contributes.
# Prognosis
Outcome depends on grade and stage. In untreated chronic hepatitis B, 5-year survival was 97% for mild, 86% for moderate-to-severe, and 55% for those with cirrhosis (15-year survival 77%, 66%, 40% respectively); all forms can progress, primarily with active HBV replication. In chronic hepatitis C, liver histology is the best prognostic indicator; mild disease has an excellent prognosis, while moderate-to-severe necroinflammation or bridging fibrosis makes cirrhosis highly likely over 10-20 years. In compensated HCV cirrhosis, 10-year survival is ~80%, with mortality 2-6%/year, decompensation 4-5%/year, and HCC 1-4%/year. In autoimmune hepatitis, 10-year survival is 80-98% for treated versus 67% for untreated patients; severe symptomatic disease carries up to ~40% 6-month mortality without therapy; in a Dutch cohort, mortality exceeded the general population only in patients with cirrhosis.
# Severity classification
Graded by necroinflammatory activity as mild, moderate, or severe using the histologic activity index (HAI, 0-18) or METAVIR (A0-A3). Staged by fibrosis: HAI 0-6 or METAVIR F0-F4, with cirrhosis defined when fibrous septa surround parenchymal nodules and alter lobular architecture.
# Clinical pearls
Chronic hepatitis is defined as hepatic inflammation and necrosis continuing for at least 6 months; milder forms are nonprogressive or slowly progressive, whereas more severe forms cause scarring and architectural reorganization that, when advanced, lead ultimately to cirrhosis.
Classification rests on three axes: (1) cause, (2) histologic activity or grade (necroinflammatory activity), and (3) degree of progression based on fibrosis or stage; neither clinical features alone nor histology alone are sufficient to characterize disease.
Grade reflects necroinflammatory activity (periportal/piecemeal necrosis or interface hepatitis, bridging necrosis, focal lobular necrosis, portal inflammation) and is scored by the histologic activity index (HAI, used in the U.S.) or METAVIR (used in Europe); chronic hepatitis is graded mild, moderate, or severe.
Stage reflects fibrosis on a numerical scale (0-6 HAI or 0-4 METAVIR); when fibrous septa surround parenchymal nodules and alter normal lobular architecture, the lesion is defined as cirrhosis.
Hyperglobulinemia and circulating autoantibodies are distinctly ABSENT in chronic hepatitis B, in contrast to autoimmune hepatitis; this helps distinguish viral from autoimmune disease.
Autoimmune hepatitis has a strong female predominance (~75% of cases) with a bimodal age distribution (one peak age <30, a second often >60); fatigue is the most common symptom (~85%) and ~25% are diagnosed when asymptomatic on abnormal liver tests.
Type I AIH (classic, North America/northern Europe, young women) is associated with ANA in a homogeneous pattern and anti-smooth-muscle antibody (ASMA); type II AIH (often children, Mediterranean) is associated with anti-LKM1 (directed against cytochrome P450 2D6) rather than ANA.
Polyclonal hypergammaglobulinemia (>2.5 g/dL) is common in AIH; smooth-muscle antibodies are detected in 63% of cases and, combined with a positive ANA, have high specificity.
Anti-LKM1 (against cytochrome P450 2D6) seen in type II AIH is the same antibody found in some patients with chronic hepatitis C; rarely AIH patients with hyperglobulinemia give false-positive anti-HCV immunoassays.
Cirrhosis from any cause of chronic hepatitis carries a risk of hepatocellular carcinoma; in cirrhotic hepatitis C the annual HCC rate is 1-4%, occurring primarily after 30 or more years of infection, warranting HCC surveillance.
Liver biopsy is imperative to diagnose AIH; classic features include interface hepatitis extending beyond portal tracts, plasma cells in the infiltrate, central vein perivenulitis, hepatocellular rosette formation, and frequent septal/bridging fibrosis and cirrhosis.
