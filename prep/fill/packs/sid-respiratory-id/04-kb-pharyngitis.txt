# Acute Pharyngitis
Acute Pharyngitis, Respiratory.
# Pathogens
Most pharyngitis is viral. Group A Streptococcus becomes a relevant consideration only with sufficient Centor criteria, and testing (rapid antigen detection or culture) should confirm before treating to avoid unnecessary antibiotic exposure.
Very likely: Respiratory viruses (most common overall).
# Likely
Group A Streptococcus (Streptococcus pyogenes) — when Centor score is high
# Possible
Fusobacterium necrophorum (consider in older adolescents/young adults with severe symptoms)
# Investigations
Rapid antigen detection test (RADT) or throat culture
Confirms or excludes Group A Streptococcus before committing to antibiotics, directly preventing unnecessary use in viral pharyngitis.

# Pathophysiology
Acute pharyngitis (symptoms generally present for days) is most often caused by respiratory viruses; group A beta-hemolytic streptococci (GAS, Streptococcus pyogenes) are a frequent bacterial cause, with other contributors including Neisseria gonorrhoeae, Epstein-Barr virus, HIV, non-group-A streptococci (groups C and G), Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Chlamydia pneumoniae, and Haemophilus influenzae. Complications of GAS pharyngitis arise from both suppurative spread (e.g., peritonsillar abscess, sinusitis, other invasive GAS infection) and nonsuppurative immune-mediated sequelae (acute rheumatic fever, poststreptococcal glomerulonephritis, scarlet fever).
# Prognosis
Both streptococcal and nonstreptococcal pharyngitis should resolve within 3-5 days. Nonsuppurative complications are rare, and even streptococcal pharyngitis is self-limited in the vast majority of cases; outside the U.S., because complications are rare, some guidelines do not recommend routine GAS testing or antibiotic treatment of sore throat.
# Severity classification
Centor criteria (4 findings, 1 point each): history of fever, absence of cough, tender anterior cervical lymphadenopathy, and tonsillar exudate or swelling. For an adult pretest probability of ~10%, post-evaluation probability of GAS is ~2% at 0 points, ~3% at 1, ~8% at 2, ~19% at 3, and ~41% at 4 points (area under the curve 0.79). Conceptually: 0-1 criteria -> neither test nor treat; 2-3 -> rapid antigen test to guide management; 4 -> rapid test or empirical treatment..
# Clinical pearls
Sore throat is not synonymous with pharyngitis: it may also arise from submandibular/retropharyngeal/peritonsillar space infections, thyroiditis, gastroesophageal reflux, tumors, or postnasal drainage.
Pharyngeal erythema is most commonly seen with viral infection (common cold, influenza), not bacterial disease.
Pharyngeal exudate can be mimicked by Candida (cottage-cheese appearance, scrapes off leaving a bleeding surface) or leukoplakia (cannot be scraped off) — do not equate exudate with bacterial pharyngitis.
GAS is the only common cause of sore throat that warrants antibiotics; most non-GAS pharyngitis is viral and self-limited.
About 10% of adults with sore throat have GAS; in children the prevalence may reach ~35%, peaking at ages 5-15 years, and is higher in winter and early spring.
Risk of GAS pharyngitis is elevated in health care and child care workers, teachers, parents of young children, and contacts of known strep cases; clinicians should watch for local outbreaks in military and institutional settings.
GAS carriage is common (up to ~20% of schoolchildren, ~5% of adolescents/young adults); rapid antigen tests and culture cannot distinguish true infection from carriage.
In adults meeting zero or one Centor criterion, a positive GAS test is very likely a false positive and/or represents carriage; indiscriminate testing of adults with sore throat is discouraged.
Lower the threshold to test/treat GAS in patients with a history of acute rheumatic fever, documented strep exposure in the past week, those in a community strep epidemic, and diabetic or immunocompromised patients.
In children the Centor criteria are less specific, so GAS pharyngitis should be confirmed by testing; children with pharyngitis but no viral signs (conjunctivitis, runny nose, cough, hoarseness, nonexudative oral lesions) warrant testing.
In developed countries the prevalence of rheumatic fever is extremely low, and antibiotic treatment does not prevent poststreptococcal glomerulonephritis.
Full-blown infectious mononucleosis (EBV) appears in only about one-fourth of cases and is a triad of clinical, hematologic, and serologic findings; new EBV causes 1-6% of pharyngitis in young adults and is rare over age 40.
Heterophil antibody is detectable in only ~40% of mononucleosis patients in the first week but in 80-90% by the third week, so an early negative test does not exclude EBV.
Gonococcal pharyngitis is more often asymptomatic than symptomatic; suspect it with persistent sore throat unresponsive to presumptive strep treatment and relevant sexual exposure.
Diphtheria produces only mild pharyngitis beneath its characteristic grayish pseudomembrane.
Acute HIV infection causes sore throat in roughly half of cases; consider it when sore throat accompanies headache, fever, myalgias, lymphadenopathy, anorexia, and rash.

Acute infectious mononucleosis (EBV) — marked cervical adenopathy, exudative pharyngitis, palatine petechiae, gelatinous uvula, splenomegaly

Gonococcal pharyngitis (Neisseria gonorrhoeae)
Mycoplasma pneumoniae pharyngitis (persistent sore throat with bronchopulmonary symptoms)
Chlamydia pneumoniae pharyngitis

# Infection mimics
Acute epiglottitis/supraglottitis — sore throat with dysphagia, odynophagia, hoarseness, stridor or tachypnea, drooling; airway emergency
Peritonsillar abscess — fever, dysphagia, profound throat pain with drooling, trismus, 'hot potato voice', unilateral palate bulge and uvular deviation
Retropharyngeal abscess — post-URI in children with sore throat, dysphagia, deep neck pain, neck stiffness, trismus, drooling; severe cases with dyspnea/stridor
Submandibular abscess / Ludwig angina — swelling of the floor of the mouth displacing the tongue posteriorly, usually after dental infection or extraction
Lemierre syndrome — septic thrombophlebitis of the internal jugular vein with metastatic infection
Candida pharyngitis — cottage-cheese exudate that scrapes off leaving a bleeding surface
# Non infectious mimics
Gastroesophageal reflux / laryngopharyngeal reflux
Postnasal drainage
Head and neck tumors
Thyroiditis
Leukoplakia mimicking exudate (cannot be scraped off)
# Additional investigations
Rapid GAS antigen test: sensitivity ~80%, specificity ~95%; proper technique (swabbing tonsils/pharynx and any exudate or ulceration) is essential to sensitivity
Throat culture: single-swab sensitivity ~85-90%; can be falsely positive in carriers; not recommended for routine adult evaluation given 24-48 h delay
Heterophil antibody test for EBV mononucleosis (low early sensitivity, rises by week 3)
CBC for atypical lymphocytosis in mononucleosis (absolute lymphocytes >4000/uL or relative >50% with >10% atypical morphology)
CT imaging for suspected head and neck abscess (except obvious peritonsillar abscess)
Lateral neck radiograph in epiglottitis may show the 'thumb sign' (not required for diagnosis)
# Red flags
Shaking chills/rigors, neck swelling beyond lymphadenopathy, trouble swallowing, drooling, or symptoms persisting >5 days without improvement should prompt urgent re-evaluation
Stridor, dyspnea, hoarseness, or tripod/forward-leaning posture with drooling — suggests epiglottitis and impending airway obstruction
Non-GAS pharyngitis that is not resolving in a teenager/young adult with rigors, neck pain or swelling, or extrapharyngeal symptoms — suspect Lemierre syndrome
Trismus, 'hot potato voice', and unilateral palate bulge with uvular deviation — peritonsillar abscess
Grayish pharyngeal pseudomembrane in a traveler from an endemic area — suspect diphtheria
# Pitfalls
Prescribing antibiotics to the majority of adults with sore throat despite only ~10% having GAS — most cases are viral.
Testing adults who meet zero or one Centor criterion: 40-60% of adults with pharyngitis fall here, and a positive test is highly likely to be false-positive or carriage.
Relying on throat culture or rapid test to distinguish active infection from GAS carriage — neither can.
Applying adult Centor criteria to children, in whom they are less specific.
Mistaking Candida or leukoplakia for bacterial pharyngeal exudate.
Attempting to examine or swab the posterior pharynx in suspected epiglottitis — this can provoke laryngospasm and should only be done in a controlled setting.
Assuming antibiotic treatment of GAS prevents poststreptococcal glomerulonephritis — it does not.
A negative early heterophil antibody test does not rule out EBV mononucleosis.
# Deescalation
If RADT/culture is negative, discontinue any antibiotics started and manage symptomatically. If positive, continue the targeted penicillin-class regimen for the full duration to prevent rheumatic fever.

# Drugs
Amoxicillin, 500 mg, Oral, every 12 hours, 10 days, Equally effective alternative to penicillin V with better palatability, especially in children.
Azithromycin (penicillin allergy), 500 mg day 1, then 250 mg, Oral, once daily, 5 days, Reserved for confirmed penicillin allergy; rising macrolide resistance in some regions should be considered.
# Framework
Do not treat empirically without testing unless Centor score is high (≥3) and rapid testing is unavailable.
A negative rapid strep test in a low-to-moderate pretest probability patient should not be treated with antibiotics.
Avoid azithromycin as a default first choice — reserve for confirmed penicillin allergy.
