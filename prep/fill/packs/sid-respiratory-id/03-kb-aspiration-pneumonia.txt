# Aspiration Pneumonia
Aspiration Pneumonia, Respiratory.
# Pathogens
Aspiration introduces oropharyngeal flora directly into the lower respiratory tract, making anaerobes a leading consideration alongside the typical community pathogens, particularly when foul-smelling sputum or dental disease/poor oral hygiene is present.
Very likely: Oral anaerobes (Peptostreptococcus, Prevotella, Fusobacterium); Streptococcus pneumoniae.
Likely: Staphylococcus aureus; Enterobacteriaceae (if hospital-associated aspiration).
# Possible
Klebsiella pneumoniae (classically associated with aspiration in alcoholics)

# Pathophysiology
Macroaspiration of oropharyngeal or gastric contents delivers a high microbial burden directly into the lower airways; lung involvement reflects this rather than invasion of a sterile space, since the lungs normally harbor a microbiota. Mechanical defenses (nasal hairs/turbinates, branching airways, mucociliary clearance, gag and cough reflexes) reduce but do not fully block bacterial access to the lower respiratory tract. Bacterial aspiration pneumonia develops over several days to weeks (not hours) through a combination of increased bacterial burden, increased virulence of aspirated organisms, and potential airway damage from aspirated gastric fluid. This contrasts with chemical pneumonitis (Mendelson syndrome), where aspirated acidic gastric juice causes rapid, direct destruction of the alveolar lining with fluid transudation into the alveolar space within hours.
# Clinical pearls
Aspiration pneumonia is best viewed as a point on a continuum that also includes community-acquired and hospital-acquired pneumonia, defined by a phenotype with macroaspiration risk factors and involvement of characteristic gravity-dependent lung locations.
Aspiration pneumonia is estimated to account for roughly 5-15% of community-acquired pneumonia cases; reliable figures for the hospital-acquired setting are not available.
The historical view that aspiration pneumonia is caused mainly by anaerobes has shifted: when acquired in the community or hospital, the likely pathogens mirror those of CAP or HAP, with anaerobes still relevant mainly in poor dentition, lung abscess, necrotizing pneumonia, or empyema.
Anaerobic aspiration infections tend to follow an indolent course, a clinical clue that helps separate them from more acutely presenting conditions such as chemical pneumonitis or pneumococcal pneumonia.
Sputum in bacterial aspiration pneumonia is characteristically NOT malodorous unless the process has been ongoing for at least about a week.
Expectorated sputum is unreliable for anaerobic culture because of inevitable contamination by normal oral microbiota; reliable anaerobic specimens historically required transtracheal or transthoracic aspiration, techniques rarely used now.
In CAP overall, no specific etiology is identified in more than half of cases despite careful history, examination, and imaging.
# Additional differentials
Foreign-body/food aspiration causing major-airway obstruction with atelectasis and nonspecific inflammation, managed by removal rather than antibiotics
Chemical (acid) pneumonitis, e.g., Mendelson syndrome from regurgitated gastric contents
Pneumococcal pneumonia, distinguished by its more acute presentation
Lung abscess (subacute anaerobic infection) as a related/overlapping entity
# Infection mimics
Pneumococcal pneumonia presenting more acutely than indolent anaerobic aspiration infection
# Non infectious mimics
Chemical pneumonitis (Mendelson syndrome) from aspiration of acidic gastric juices, with rapid alveolar injury and pulmonary edema, often without fever and frequently needing no antibiotics
Foreign-body/food aspiration with airway obstruction and atelectasis
# Additional investigations
Chest radiography looking for consolidation in dependent lung segments (basilar lower-lobe segments if aspiration occurred upright; posterior segment of the upper lobe or superior segment of the lower lobe if supine)
Gram stain of sputum, which characteristically shows a mixed bacterial population with many polymorphonuclear leukocytes
Bronchoscopic protected-brush specimens or bronchoalveolar lavage, which may recover anaerobes from the lower tract with less oropharyngeal contamination (role still being defined)
Assessment of dentition/oral hygiene, given the association of severe dental disease with aspiration pneumonia
# Red flags
Gross hemoptysis suggesting necrotizing pneumonia
Progression to respiratory failure; in chemical pneumonitis a chest x-ray can evolve from normal to a complete bilateral whiteout within 8-24 hours
Septic shock and organ failure in severe pneumonia
Weight loss and anemia indicating a more chronic process
Development of lung abscess or complicated/empyematous pleural effusion (pleural fluid pH <7.2, glucose <2.2 mmol/L, or LDH >1000 U/L, or organisms seen/cultured indicates need for drainage)
# Pitfalls
Failing to distinguish chemical pneumonitis (Mendelson syndrome) from bacterial aspiration pneumonia: chemical pneumonitis is a non-infectious acid injury that usually resolves with supportive care and does NOT warrant antibiotics unless bacterial superinfection develops.
Routinely covering anaerobes for all aspiration pneumonia: anaerobic coverage is unnecessary unless dentition is poor or there is lung abscess or necrotizing pneumonia.
Expecting an acute, foul-smelling presentation: bacterial aspiration pneumonia evolves over days to weeks and sputum is usually not malodorous until the process has lasted about a week.
Relying on expectorated sputum for anaerobic culture, which is invalid because of oral-flora contamination.
# Deescalation
Narrow or stop anaerobic-specific coverage if cultures (when obtained) do not support it and the patient is improving on a narrower-spectrum agent; reassess for evolving lung abscess if response is slow.

# Framework
Routine anaerobic coverage is appropriate for witnessed/strongly suspected aspiration — but not for typical CAP without an aspiration history, where it adds unnecessary spectrum.
Address the underlying aspiration risk (dysphagia evaluation, positioning, oral hygiene) as part of management, not antibiotics alone — recurrence is otherwise likely.
De-escalate based on clinical response; prolonged anaerobic coverage beyond 7 days is not routinely needed for uncomplicated aspiration pneumonia (distinct from lung abscess, which needs longer therapy).
