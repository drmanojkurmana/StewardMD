# Ventilator Associated Pneumonia
Ventilator Associated Pneumonia, Respiratory.
# Pathogens
VAP pathogens reflect the most resistant end of the nosocomial spectrum, given prolonged ICU exposure, prior antibiotics, and device-associated colonization. Local ICU antibiogram should directly inform empiric choice.
Very likely: Pseudomonas aeruginosa; Klebsiella pneumoniae (including ESBL/carbapenem-resistant strains); Acinetobacter baumannii.
Likely: MRSA; Escherichia coli.
Possible: Stenotrophomonas maltophilia in prolonged ICU stay.
Red flags: Immediate empiric antibiotics required.
# Investigations
Quantitative endotracheal aspirate or BAL culture, Gold standard to identify causative organism and guide de-escalation in VAP.
Blood cultures, Detects bacteremic spread.
Chest X-ray (serial), Tracks new or progressive infiltrate.
Procalcitonin (serial), Supports shortened duration of therapy when trending down with clinical improvement.
Immediate empiric antibiotics required
# Reason
New infiltrate with fever, purulent tracheal secretions and worsening oxygenation in a mechanically ventilated patient beyond 48 hours meets criteria for VAP. Given the high mortality risk of delayed therapy in this setting, empiric broad-spectrum antibiotics covering MDR organisms should be started immediately, guided by local ICU antibiogram.
# Pathophysiology
The endotracheal tube is the single most important risk factor for VAP. While it blocks large-volume aspiration, it worsens microaspiration of secretions that pool above the cuff, and the tube plus suctioning damages tracheal mucosa and promotes tracheal colonization. Pathogenic bacteria form a glycocalyx biofilm on the tube surface that shields them from antibiotics and host defenses; suctioning can dislodge organisms that reinoculate the trachea, and biofilm fragments can embolize to distal airways carrying bacteria. Ventilator circuit tubing can harbor organisms that wash back if manipulated, so circuits are changed only when soiled or with a new patient. In critically ill patients, normal oropharyngeal flora is replaced by pathogens, driven mainly by antibiotic selection pressure, cross-infection, severe systemic illness, and malnutrition. Severely ill patients with sepsis or trauma appear to enter a state of immunoparalysis several days after ICU admission, coinciding with peak VAP risk; the mechanism is unclear but hyperglycemia and frequent transfusions adversely affect immunity.
# Prognosis
Crude mortality rates for VAP are reported as high as 50-70%, but the clinically meaningful figure is attributable mortality, since many patients have underlying diseases that would be fatal regardless. Attributable mortality exceeded 25% in one matched-cohort study, while more recent estimates are lower (5-10%); VAP complicating COVID-19 has higher attributable mortality. Mortality varies with patient type and ICU; VAP in trauma patients is generally not associated with attributable mortality (these patients were often previously healthy). The causative pathogen matters: MDR pathogens carry significantly greater attributable mortality than non-MDR pathogens, and infection with organisms such as S. maltophilia mainly marks a severely immunocompromised host at high risk. Beyond death, the major complication is prolonged mechanical ventilation with longer ICU and hospital stays; long-term sequelae of necrotizing infection include bronchiectasis and parenchymal scarring with recurrent pneumonia, prolonged oxygen need, a catabolic state, and (in the elderly) loss of independent function.
# Severity classification
Empirical-therapy decisions are instead stratified by (1) the presence of risk factors for MDR pathogens and (2) the predicted risk of death, with a mortality risk of <=15% considered low risk.
# Clinical pearls
VAP is defined as pneumonia with onset at or beyond 48 h after the start of mechanical ventilation; the same principles extend to ventilated HAP and to non-ICU HAP.
About 35% of all HAP patients require mechanical ventilation, which marks ventilated HAP as a distinct entity; roughly 70% of HAP is acquired outside the ICU and 30% within it.
Three sequential steps drive VAP: oropharyngeal colonization with pathogens, aspiration of these organisms into the lower airway, and compromise of host defenses; almost all intubated patients are at least transiently colonized, yet only about one-third go on to develop VAP.
Colony counts often rise to high levels days before clinical pneumonia appears, supporting the idea that the final step is overwhelming of host defenses by a large bacterial inoculum.
Serial changes in oxygenation may detect VAP earlier than other clinical findings and can also be used to track response to therapy.
Clinical manifestations of HAP and VAP are nonspecific: fever, leukocytosis, increased respiratory secretions, consolidation on examination, and a new or changing radiographic infiltrate.
Absence of bacteria on a Gram stain of an endotracheal aspirate makes pneumonia an unlikely cause of fever or infiltrates, and absence of an MDR pathogen on culture supports de-escalation of empirical therapy.
Clinical improvement, when it occurs, is usually evident within 48-72 h of starting therapy, typically as improved oxygenation; chest radiographs often worsen early in treatment and are therefore less useful than clinical criteria for judging response.
P. aeruginosa essentially never causes infection in patients without prior antibiotic exposure; antibiotic exposure is by far the greatest risk factor for replacement of normal oropharyngeal flora by pathogens.
On any given day in the ICU, an average of about 10% of patients have pneumonia, the overwhelming majority of which is VAP; reported prevalence ranges from 6 to 52 cases per 100 patients depending on the population.

# Additional investigations
Quantitative deep respiratory tract cultures to separate colonization from true infection: more distal sampling is more specific and uses a lower growth threshold; endotracheal aspirate threshold is 10^6 cfu/mL and protected specimen brush threshold is 10^3 cfu/mL, but sensitivity falls with more distal blind sampling.
Gram stain of respiratory specimen, differential cell counts, staining for intracellular organisms, and detection of locally elevated infection-related protein levels to increase diagnostic yield.
Molecular diagnostic methods, which can exclude a suspected MDR pathogen as a therapy target when results are negative.
Serial procalcitonin and other biomarkers, used together with the initial clinical response to help guide therapy duration.
# Red flags
Worsening oxygenation, tachypnea, tachycardia, and increased minute ventilation in a ventilated patient.
Septic shock accompanying suspected VAP (favors broader empirical and combination coverage).
Colony counts above the diagnostic threshold while already on antibiotics, suggesting current therapy is ineffective.
Necrotizing pneumonia (e.g., P. aeruginosa or S. aureus) causing significant pulmonary hemorrhage or empyema.
VAP complicating COVID-19, which carries higher attributable mortality than other forms of VAP.
# Pitfalls
Applying clinical criteria designed for community-acquired pneumonia consistently overdiagnoses VAP, because of frequent tracheal colonization in intubated patients, multiple alternative causes of radiographic infiltrates, and the high frequency of other fever sources in critically ill patients.
No single set of criteria is reliably diagnostic of pneumonia in a ventilated patient, which undermines both prevention and treatment efforts and even estimates of VAP attributable mortality.
Recently effective antibiotics given in the 24-48 h before sampling can cause false-negative quantitative cultures; with susceptible organisms a single dose can drop colony counts below threshold, and operating characteristics only normalize after about 3 days.
Quantitative cultures can also fall below threshold if obtained very early in infection or if delayed until an effective host response has reduced bacterial counts; the specimen should ideally be taken as soon as VAP is suspected and before antibiotics are started or changed.
Recurrent VAP with the same pathogen can occur because endotracheal-tube biofilm allows persistence and reintroduction; however, about half of recurrent Pseudomonas cases are actually due to a new strain.
Aggressive early extubation can lead to reintubation and increased aspiration (raising VAP risk), while heavy continuous sedation also increases VAP risk and undersedation risks self-extubation, so tradeoffs must be balanced.
# Deescalation
Once culture and sensitivity are available, narrow to the single most targeted agent — e.g., Pseudomonas sensitive to cefepime allows discontinuation of meropenem and vancomycin if MRSA is not isolated.
