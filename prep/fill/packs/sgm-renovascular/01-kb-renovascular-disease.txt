# Renovascular Disease
Renovascular Disease, Renal/Genitourinary.
Aliases: Renal artery stenosis; Renovascular hypertension; Atherosclerotic renal artery stenosis; ARAS; Ischemic nephropathy.
# Pathophysiology
The renal cortex receives high arteriolar flow in excess of its metabolic needs for filtration, while the postglomerular medullary circulation has lower flow yet high oxygen consumption, leaving the deeper medulla near the margin of hypoxemia. Large-vessel occlusive disease (atherosclerosis, fibromuscular dysplasia, dissection, extrinsic compression, aortic stent graft) reduces perfusion pressure to the kidney. Once stenosis is hemodynamically significant (typically >70 to 80% lumen occlusion), the drop in perfusion pressure activates the renin-angiotensin system, reduces sodium excretion, and stimulates sympathetic adrenergic pathways, producing angiotensin-dependent systemic hypertension. Sustained pressure and ischemia drive endothelial injury (reflected by increased urinary albumin excretion), microvascular proliferation in early atherosclerosis and later microvascular rarefaction in chronic ischemia, with consequent tubulointerstitial fibrosis and loss of function. In atheroembolic disease, cholesterol crystals from fractured plaque occlude microvessels leaving a characteristic 'cleft' on biopsy.
# Prognosis
FMD patients are usually younger with otherwise normal vessels and long life expectancy and often respond well to angioplasty; FMD seldom threatens kidney function. Untreated ARAS progresses in nearly 50% of cases over 5 years, sometimes to total occlusion, though intensive BP and statin therapy slow progression. After endovascular stenting renal function recovers in only ~25%, is unchanged in ~50%, and deteriorates in the remainder. ARAS independently predicts cardiovascular events and death. Atheroembolic renal disease carries an ominous prognosis with 1-year mortality exceeding 38%; untreated malignant hypertension historically had 1-year mortality over 90% (and >50% at 6 to 12 months with target organ injury), but current 5-year survival exceeds 50% with effective antihypertensive therapy.
# Severity classification
No formal staging is given; severity is gauged by degree of luminal stenosis (hemodynamically critical at >70 to 80% occlusion), CKD stage (most revascularization candidates are stage 3 to 5 with eGFR <60), and clinical syndrome (stable medically managed disease versus rapidly progressive/resistant disease). # Clinical pearls
Renovascular disease is a specifically treatable 'secondary' cause of hypertension because restoring renal perfusion pressure can reverse the pressor pathways it activates.
Roughly 90% of renovascular hypertension is due to atherosclerotic renal artery stenosis (ARAS), which is typically unilateral with a discrete lesion near the renal artery origin in older patients with a broader atherosclerotic risk profile.
Fibromuscular dysplasia (FMD) accounts for a minority of cases and characteristically affects younger patients aged ~15 to 50, predominantly women; lesions are more diffuse with an irregular 'sawtooth/string-of-beads' appearance located in the distal renal artery.
FMD should be suspected with abrupt-onset hypertension (especially in young women) or resistant/recently worsening hypertension; ARAS should be suspected in older patients with other ASCVD evidence or otherwise unexplained decline in kidney function.
Critical stenosis (usually >70 to 80% luminal obstruction) reduces perfusion pressure, activating the renin-angiotensin system, reducing sodium excretion, and engaging sympathetic pathways.
Early renovascular hypertension is angiotensin-dependent and marked by widely varying pressures, loss of circadian BP rhythm, and accelerated target organ injury including left ventricular hypertrophy and renal fibrosis.
An abdominal renal artery bruit, or carotid/femoral bruits, may be audible and supports the diagnosis.
When large-vessel disease primarily threatens kidney function the entity is termed ischemic nephropathy; gradually reduced flow can leave function reduced but stable for years, whereas advanced disease produces cortical hypoperfusion and overt tissue hypoxia.
ARAS commonly coexists with preexisting small-vessel disease from hypertension, aging, and diabetes; nearly 85% of patients considered for revascularization already have stage 3 to 5 CKD (eGFR <60).
The presence of ARAS independently predicts cardiovascular morbidity and mortality regardless of whether revascularization is performed.
Atheroembolic renal disease most often follows arterial procedures (e.g., coronary angiography), with cholesterol crystals lodging in downstream microvessels; manifestations typically appear 1 to 14 days after the inciting event.
Thromboembolic renal disease can cause flank pain, fever, leukocytosis, nausea and vomiting, and a transient extreme rise in LDH if infarction occurs; bilateral involvement causes precipitous functional decline and reduced urine output.
Additional differentials: Essential (primary) hypertension; Renal parenchymal disease; Primary aldosteronism; Pheochromocytoma; Hypertensive nephrosclerosis / arteriolonephrosclerosis; Atheroembolic renal disease; Thromboembolic renal disease / renal infarction.
# Infection mimics
Renal infarction from septic emboli (e.g., subacute bacterial endocarditis vegetations)
Thrombotic renal occlusion precipitated by systemic infection such as COVID-19
# Non infectious mimics
Renal parenchymal disease causing secondary hypertension
Primary aldosteronism
Hypertensive nephrosclerosis
Cholesterol atheroembolism mimicking vasculitis (livedo reticularis, eosinophilia, elevated ESR, hypocomplementemia)
# Additional investigations
Duplex (Doppler) ultrasonography: peak systolic renal artery velocity >200 cm/s suggests >60% occlusion (some trials use >300 cm/s to reduce false positives); renal resistive index informs kidney viability but is operator-dependent
Contrast-enhanced CT angiography: excellent vascular images and assessment of accessory/distal vessels; stents do not cause artifacts; small contrast toxicity risk
Magnetic resonance angiography: non-nephrotoxic but gadolinium is avoided when eGFR <30 due to nephrogenic systemic fibrosis risk; cannot visualize stented vessels
Captopril renography (99mTc-MAG3): strong negative predictive value when entirely normal; limited utility with advanced atherosclerosis or creatinine >2.0 mg/dL
Intraarterial (digital subtraction) angiography: gold standard for large-vessel disease, often performed at time of planned intervention
Plasma renin activity is subject to timing, drugs, and sodium intake and does not reliably predict response to revascularization; renal venous renin sampling is now rarely used due to false results
Kidney biopsy: confirms atheroembolic disease (cholesterol-crystal cleft in microvessels)
# Red flags
Rapidly progressive or resistant hypertension failing optimal medical therapy
Progressive decline in GFR during BP treatment, or decline associated with ACE inhibitor/ARB therapy
Recurrent flash pulmonary edema / congestive heart failure not explained by LV dysfunction
Abrupt-onset hypertension in a young woman (suggests FMD) or worsening kidney function in an older patient with ASCVD (suggests ARAS)
New worsening hypertension and deteriorating renal function days after an arterial/angiographic procedure (atheroembolism), possibly reaching a malignant phase
Acute flank pain with fever and markedly elevated LDH suggesting renal infarction; anuria with bilateral acute occlusion
# Pitfalls
Plasma renin activity is transient and influenced by drugs/sodium/timing, so it does not reliably predict response to revascularization; over-reliance on it is misleading.
Multiple randomized trials show no compelling benefit of revascularization over optimal medical therapy for moderate ARAS regarding BP or renal function over 3 to 5 years; revascularization should not be assumed beneficial just because flow is restored.
Revascularization rarely normalizes BP alone because most patients have preexisting hypertension and need continued drug therapy.
Atheroembolic complications of revascularization can be catastrophic and accelerate the very hypertension and renal failure the procedure intended to prevent.
Atheroembolic renal disease is frequently underdiagnosed and is often a diagnosis of exclusion; systemic features (fever, abdominal pain, weight loss) are present in fewer than half of patients.
Hypertensive nephrosclerosis is often assigned without specific etiologic proof; nephrosclerosis changes also occur with aging, dyslipidemia, and glucose intolerance with only modest association with BP, and antihypertensive therapy does not alter its course.
# Management
Control blood pressure with renin-angiotensin system blockade using an ACE inhibitor or angiotensin receptor blocker, monitoring renal function and potassium closely, especially in bilateral disease
Slow atherosclerotic progression with a statin, antiplatelet therapy and smoking cessation in atherosclerotic renal artery stenosis
Offer percutaneous transluminal angioplasty for fibromuscular dysplasia, which is often curative in younger patients
Reserve angioplasty with stenting for atherosclerotic stenosis in selected cases such as resistant hypertension, flash pulmonary oedema or progressive renal impairment
Add further antihypertensive agents as needed and manage overall cardiovascular risk
Monitor blood pressure, renal function and stent patency, and involve vascular and interventional specialists for revascularisation decisions
