Donor surgery favors the left kidney. Laparoscopic donor nephrectomy preferentially takes the left kidney because its renal vein is significantly longer than the short right vein that drains directly into the inferior vena cava. Extra venous length makes the recipient anastomosis easier and safer, so the right kidney stays with the donor unless anatomy dictates otherwise.

The new kidney lives in the pelvis, not the loin. Heterotopic placement sets the allograft into the recipient iliac fossa outside the peritoneum, joining the allograft renal artery to the external or internal iliac artery and the vein to the external iliac vein. The native kidneys stay untouched, and the extraperitoneal pocket confines any leak or bleed away from the abdomen.

The ureter joins the bladder from outside. Extravesical Lich-Gregoir ureteroneocystostomy tunnels the donor ureter into the bladder wall with a double-J stent left across the join, and it carries the lowest leak and obstruction rates of the options. The anti-reflux tunnel plus temporary stenting protects the anastomosis while it heals.

Narrowing comes from ischemia at harvest. Ureteral stenosis in 2 to 5 percent of allografts arises when devascularization of the donor lower-pole ureteral blood supply during retrieval starves the distal ureter. The golden rule is preserving the periureteral blanket of tissue around the donor ureter, because the ureter lives on that adventitial blood supply.

Leaks declare themselves with creatinine. Urine leak in 1 to 4 percent presents with rising serum creatinine, pelvic pain, and wound drainage, and aspirated fluid shows creatinine far higher than serum levels. The comparison is diagnostic: lymph has serum-level creatinine while urine is concentrated manifold, so the number alone separates leak from lymphocele.

Lymph collections come from unsealed channels. Lymphocele in 1 to 15 percent follows unligated recipient iliac lymphatics divided during vessel exposure, and symptomatic collections compressing the allograft vein or ureter need laparoscopic peritoneal fenestration that marsupializes the cavity into the peritoneum for internal drainage. Meticulous clipping of lymphatics at exposure prevents most of them.

Artery narrowing mimics rejection. Allograft renal artery stenosis presents with refractory hypertension and unexplained graft dysfunction, and first-line treatment is percutaneous transluminal angioplasty with stenting rather than open revision. The presentation overlaps rejection and obstruction, so Doppler velocity elevation plus a suggestive MAG3 curve earns angiography.

Vein clots kill grafts in hours. Transplant renal vein thrombosis causes sudden oliguria, severe allograft swelling with pain over the graft, and graft loss, striking under 1 to 2 percent within the first 48 to 72 hours. Technical kinks, hypotension, and hypercoagulability are the triggers, and salvage demands immediate re-exploration with thrombectomy before cortical necrosis completes.
