Craniosynostosis means premature fusion of one or more cranial sutures, which occurs before the brain has finished growing. This early fusion restricts skull growth perpendicular to the fused suture, and the growing brain forces compensatory growth parallel to it, so the head takes on a characteristic abnormal shape.

Virchow law predicts this deformity pattern: growth is restricted across the fused suture while compensatory growth continues at the open sutures. The sagittal suture runs along the midline of the vault, so its fusion gives a long narrow scaphocephalic head with frontal and occipital bossing. Students apply Virchow law by matching the fused suture to the direction of restriction.

Sagittal synostosis is the commonest single-suture form and accounts for more than 50% of cases. It produces scaphocephaly, a long narrow boat-shaped head, which surgeons recognise from the elongated vault and ridged midline suture. Sagittal fusion presents in infancy with a cephalic index below normal.

Coronal synostosis causes anterior plagiocephaly when unilateral, and pressure on the orbit gives the harlequin eye deformity seen on skull radiographs. The ipsilateral forehead flattens while the contralateral side bosses forward. Bilateral coronal fusion gives a short wide brachycephalic head.

Metopic synostosis causes trigonocephaly, a triangular forehead with a keel-shaped ridge along the closed metopic suture. The forehead narrows to a pointed wedge with hypotelorism. Metopic ridging without true triangular shape can be a normal variant.

Crouzon syndrome is an autosomal dominant FGFR2 disorder combining craniosynostosis with midface hypoplasia but normal hands and feet. Patients show exorbitism with shallow orbits, a beaked nose, and relative mandibular prognathism from the retruded midface. The absence of limb anomalies distinguishes Crouzon from Apert and Pfeiffer.

Apert syndrome is an FGFR2 disorder defined by specific mutations, Ser252Trp and Pro253Arg, which produce severe symmetric syndactyly of the hands and feet called the mitten hand. It pairs bicoronal synostosis with turribrachycephaly, midface hypoplasia, and complex osseous syndactyly. The hand deformity separates Apert from Crouzon clinically.

Pfeiffer syndrome arises from FGFR1 or FGFR2 mutations and combines craniosynostosis with broad thumbs and broad great toes. The thumbs and halluces are wide and deviated, which marks Pfeiffer against the mitten hand of Apert. Cranial involvement is often bicoronal with midface hypoplasia.

Surgery is timed at 3 to 9 months, with open cranial vault remodelling for complex or multi-suture disease and endoscopic strip craniectomy with helmet therapy for suitable single-suture cases under 6 months. Operating in this window lets the rapidly growing brain reshape the vault while bone is still malleable.
