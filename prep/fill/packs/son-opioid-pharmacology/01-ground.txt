Opioid receptors and respiratory effects
- The mu receptor carries most of the clinically important opioid actions: analgesia, euphoria, slowed gut movement and breathing suppression.
- Breathing suppression comes from blunted brainstem sensitivity to rising carbon dioxide. Sedation tends to appear before it. A respiratory rate under 10 per minute is an early danger sign.
- Opioids are dose dependent in their respiratory effect, and the risk is higher in people with lung disease or critical illness.

Reversal with naloxone
- Naloxone is a pure antagonist at the mu receptor. It reverses opioid effects and is the usual rescue drug.
- In an unstable patient, give it intravenously in small titrated amounts. A common preparation is 0.4 mg diluted in 10 mL of saline. Further 1 to 2 mL boluses go in roughly every 30 to 60 seconds until breathing improves.
- Long-acting opioids, or patients who clear them slowly, may need a continuous naloxone infusion adjusted by a standard chart.
- A stable patient with mild depression may be managed by holding the opioid and supporting ventilation with a noninvasive mask.

Ceiling effect
- Full agonists here are morphine, fentanyl, oxycodone, hydromorphone and methadone. They have no ceiling on analgesia, so the dose is set by relief and tolerability.
- Buprenorphine is a partial agonist. It has a plateau: beyond a threshold dose, extra drug adds no analgesia.
- Buprenorphine binds the receptor tightly but with low intrinsic activity, so it can push out full agonists and precipitate withdrawal in dependent patients.
- Buprenorphine carries less abuse liability, has limited cancer pain data, and is not suited to chronic pain at end of life.
- NSAIDs and acetaminophen have ceilings. Opioids do not.

Transdermal fentanyl
- Each patch lasts 72 hours. Patches are often changed on that schedule.
- Analgesia starts late: an onset delay of about 12 to 15 hours after placement, then 24 to 72 hours to reach steady levels. A short-acting rescue opioid covers the gap.
- Do not change the dose sooner than every 72 hours. After removal, levels fall over about 24 hours.
- Heat over the patch, such as a fever or heating pad, increases absorption and can cause respiratory depression.
- Use only in opioid-tolerant patients with chronic pain.
- Advantages: less constipation than oral morphine in some studies, liver clearance without active metabolites, and usefulness when oral medicine is impossible. Suitable with reduced kidney function.
- Limit: patients with little subcutaneous fat, such as those with cachexia, may absorb poorly and fail to reach therapeutic levels.
- Patches with metal parts must be removed before magnetic resonance imaging.

Breakthrough pain and rescue dosing
- Breakthrough pain is severe acute pain on top of otherwise controlled chronic pain.
- Rescue dose on a fixed schedule: about 5 to 15 percent of the total 24-hour requirement.
- A different reference gives about 20 percent of the baseline dose for liquid morphine.
- Starting dose for an opioid-tolerant patient admitted with acute pain: 10 to 20 percent of the prior 24-hour oral morphine equivalent. This is an initial dose, not a rescue dose, so keep the two apart.
- Worked example: 300 mg oral morphine per day gives 5 percent (15 mg) to 15 percent (45 mg) per rescue dose.
