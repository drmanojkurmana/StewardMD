Opioid action and breathing
- Most opioid analgesics bind the mu receptor. That receptor carries the main clinical effects: pain relief, drowsiness, slowed bowel movement and suppressed breathing.
- Mu activation in brainstem rhythm networks cuts excitatory drive to the pre-Botzinger complex and related nuclei, which slows and shallows each breath.
- Breathing suppression is dose linked and reflects a blunted drive from the brainstem respiratory centres. Drowsiness usually comes first, so it works as an early warning. A respiratory rate under 10 breaths each minute is a danger sign that needs prompt action.
- Risk climbs with lung disease, critical illness and combined sedating drugs.

Reversal with naloxone
- Naloxone blocks the mu receptor without activating it. It reverses opioid effects, including breathing failure, and is the standard rescue agent.
- In an unstable patient it is given by vein in small titrated doses. A common preparation dilutes 0.4 mg into 10 mL of saline. Further doses of 1 to 2 mL follow about every 30 to 60 seconds until breathing recovers.
- Long-acting or slowly cleared opioids can call for a continuous naloxone infusion guided by a standard chart.
- A stable patient with mild depression may be managed by stopping the opioid and supporting breathing with a noninvasive mask.

Ceiling effect and agonist types
- Morphine, fentanyl, oxycodone, hydromorphone and methadone are full agonists. Opioids generally have no ceiling, so the dose is set by the degree of relief and by tolerability.
- Buprenorphine is a partial agonist with a plateau. Beyond a threshold dose, more drug adds no analgesia. It binds tightly with low intrinsic activity, so it can displace stronger opioids and precipitate withdrawal in dependent patients. Its abuse potential is lower, its cancer pain data are limited, and it is not suited to long-term pain at the end of life.
- Butorphanol and buprenorphine are described as mixed agonist-antagonists. Nalbuphine, another mixed agonist-antagonist, is used for persistent opioid-related itch.
- Pentazocine, butorphanol and nalbuphine are agonist-antagonists that show an analgesic plateau, which the 2026 review attributes to limited intrinsic efficacy. That review also notes the ceiling is debated, so it is taught as a reported finding.

Transdermal fentanyl
- A patch is typically changed every 72 hours, although some patients need changes every 48 hours.
- Analgesia lags by about 12 to 15 hours after placement. Plasma levels then need 24 to 72 hours to settle, and steady state is usually reached after roughly 3 to 6 days. A short-acting rescue opioid covers the gap. Dose changes should not come sooner than every 72 hours.
- Fentanyl is cleared by the liver without active metabolites, which makes it a promising option when kidney function is reduced. Some studies show less constipation than with oral morphine.
- Heat over the patch increases absorption and can cause respiratory depression. Patches containing metal must come off before magnetic resonance imaging.
- Cachexia removes adipose tissue, which lowers absorption and may leave a patient short of therapeutic levels. Patches suit only opioid-tolerant patients with chronic pain.

Breakthrough dosing
- Breakthrough pain is severe acute pain on top of controlled chronic pain and needs a short-acting rescue dose.
- When the schedule is fixed, the usual rescue amount is 5 to 15 percent of the 24-hour total need. For example, 300 mg of oral morphine a day gives 15 to 45 mg per rescue dose.
- An opioid-tolerant patient admitted with acute pain may start at 10 to 20 percent of the prior 24-hour oral morphine equivalent. That is an initial dose, not a rescue dose.
