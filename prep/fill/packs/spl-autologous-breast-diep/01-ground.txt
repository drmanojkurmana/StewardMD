Autologous breast reconstruction rebuilds the mound from the patient's own tissue, and the DIEP flap is the modern standard. The deep inferior epigastric perforator flap carries abdominal skin and fat to the chest while completely sparing the rectus abdominis muscle and its anterior sheath, so the abdominal wall stays strong.

The transplanted tissue needs recipient vessels on the chest. The internal mammary artery with its vein in the third or fourth rib interspace serves as the primary recipient, exposed by removing a cartilage segment for a size match with the deep inferior epigastric pedicle.

Understanding the older TRAM flap explains DIEP zone selection. Hartrampf zones map perfusion: Zone I sits over the pedicle and lives best, Zone II lies contralateral medial, Zone III ipsilateral lateral, and Zone IV, the contralateral lateral corner, is least perfused and routinely discarded.

The SIEA flap goes one step further by never entering the fascia at all. The superficial inferior epigastric artery flap avoids the rectus sheath entirely, but only about 20% to 30% of patients carry a superficial system with caliber over 1.5 mm strong enough to perfuse a whole breast.

When the abdomen has been used or cannot serve, the back provides. The latissimus dorsi myocutaneous flap, pedicled on the thoracodorsal vessels, swings reliable skin and muscle forward, often paired with an implant beneath for final volume in thinner patients.

Other donor sites cover special cases. Secondary free flaps include the transverse upper gracilis from the inner thigh, the profunda artery perforator flap from the posterior thigh, and the superior gluteal artery perforator flap from the buttock, each chosen when abdominal tissue is unavailable or insufficient.

Radiation changes the recommendation decisively. Post-mastectomy radiation therapy multiplies capsular contracture and implant failure rates by 3 to 4 times, so irradiated chests strongly prefer autologous tissue that brings its own blood supply and softens rather than hardens.
