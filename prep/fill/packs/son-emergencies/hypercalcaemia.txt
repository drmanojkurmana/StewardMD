# Hypercalcaemia (adult)
Rehydrate with IV 0.9% saline, stop calcium-raising drugs, give zoledronic acid or denosumab for hypercalcaemia of malignancy (adding calcitonin when severe), and use PTH to separate PTH-mediated from PTH-independent causes.
recognise
# Recognise and grade
Use albumin-adjusted or ionised calcium. Adjusted calcium (mg/dL) = measured calcium + 0.8 x (4.0 minus albumin in g/dL).
Severity (Endocrine Society 2023): mild below 12 mg/dL (3.0 mmol/L), moderate 12 to 14 mg/dL (3.0 to 3.5 mmol/L), severe above 14 mg/dL (3.5 mmol/L).
Below 3.0 mmol/L is often asymptomatic; 3.0 to 3.5 may be tolerated if slow in onset; above 3.5 mmol/L needs urgent correction because of arrhythmia and coma risk (SfE).
Features: polyuria, thirst, dehydration, nausea, constipation, abdominal pain, pancreatitis, confusion, drowsiness, short QT, AKI and renal stones.
Causes: primary hyperparathyroidism (commonest in outpatients), malignancy (commonest in inpatients: PTHrP, bone metastases, myeloma, calcitriol-producing lymphoma), granulomatous disease (sarcoidosis, TB), vitamin D toxicity, thiazides, lithium, milk-alkali syndrome, immobilisation, thyrotoxicosis.
immediate
# Immediate treatment
IV 0.9% saline: SfE suggests 4 to 6 L over 24 hours; Endocrine Society suggests a 1 to 2 L bolus then 200 to 500 mL/h to keep urine output 100 to 150 mL/h. Reduce in heart failure, renal impairment and older adults.
Stop thiazides, lithium, calcium and vitamin D supplements, and calcium-containing antacids.
Loop diuretics only for fluid overload after rehydration; they are not an effective calcium-lowering treatment.
ECG and cardiac monitoring for severe hypercalcaemia or ECG changes.
investigations
# Work-up: PTH first
Send PTH with calcium before treatment. Raised or inappropriately normal PTH: PTH-mediated (primary or tertiary hyperparathyroidism, lithium, familial hypocalciuric hypercalcaemia: check urine calcium to creatinine clearance ratio).
Suppressed PTH: PTH-independent. Check PTHrP, 25-hydroxy vitamin D, 1,25-dihydroxy vitamin D, serum protein electrophoresis and free light chains, TSH, and chest imaging.
Also: creatinine, electrolytes, phosphate, magnesium, ALP, CBC and ECG.
In India, ask specifically about high-dose vitamin D injections or supplements, and consider tuberculosis and sarcoidosis when 1,25-dihydroxy vitamin D is high.
treatment
# Antiresorptive and specific therapy
Hypercalcaemia of malignancy: IV bisphosphonate or denosumab is recommended; the Endocrine Society suggests denosumab over IV bisphosphonate.
Zoledronic acid 4 mg IV over at least 15 minutes; calcium reaches its nadir in 2 to 4 days. In reduced GFR, give more slowly (30 to 60 minutes) and consider dose reduction.
Denosumab 120 mg SC, repeated 1, 2 and 4 weeks later then monthly. Not renally cleared, so useful when bisphosphonates are unsuitable in renal failure, but hypocalcaemia risk is high with GFR below 30 mL/min: consider a lower dose, replete vitamin D and monitor calcium.
Severe hypercalcaemia (above 14 mg/dL): add calcitonin 4 to 8 units/kg SC or IM every 6 to 12 hours for 48 to 72 hours (rapid onset, tachyphylaxis limits use).
Calcitriol-mediated (lymphoma, granulomatous disease, vitamin D toxicity): glucocorticoids, for example prednisolone 40 mg daily, or hydrocortisone 200 to 400 mg IV daily for 3 to 5 days; add bisphosphonate or denosumab if symptomatic or severe despite steroids.
Parathyroid carcinoma or primary hyperparathyroidism unfit for surgery: cinacalcet 30 mg twice daily, titrated every 2 to 4 weeks; refer for parathyroidectomy when indicated.
escalate
# Escalate
Consider haemodialysis with a low-calcium bath for severe hypercalcaemia with renal failure or fluid overload that prevents saline therapy.
Reduced consciousness, arrhythmia or calcium above 3.5 mmol/L (14 mg/dL): HDU-level monitoring and early specialist input (endocrinology, oncology, nephrology).
Treating the underlying malignancy is central to controlling hypercalcaemia of malignancy and preventing recurrence: involve oncology early.
monitoring
# Monitoring
Adjusted calcium, creatinine and electrolytes daily during treatment; fluid balance and signs of overload.
After bisphosphonate or denosumab: watch for hypocalcaemia, hypophosphataemia and hypomagnesaemia, especially with vitamin D deficiency.
Bisphosphonates can cause an acute phase reaction; long-term antiresorptives carry a risk of osteonecrosis of the jaw.
pitfalls
# Pitfalls
Giving furosemide before the patient is rehydrated.
Missing primary hyperparathyroidism coexisting with cancer: always check PTH.
Using calcitonin alone: its effect wanes within 48 to 72 hours.
Full-dose bisphosphonate in advanced renal impairment, or denosumab without vitamin D repletion, causing severe hypocalcaemia.
