# Acute poisoning: general approach (international)
Adults
Emergency department, ICU
Resuscitate first (ABCDE, glucose, ECG), identify the toxidrome and give specific antidotes early. Give activated charcoal only with a safe airway and an adsorbable poison, usually within 1 hour and up to 6 hours for selected poisons; avoid routine lavage and ipecac; use extracorporeal removal per EXTRIP and seek poison centre advice.
Aliases: activated charcoal; gastric lavage; whole bowel irrigation; EXTRIP; haemodialysis in poisoning; toxic cardiac arrest; ECMO in poisoning.
# History and toxidromes
Establish the agent, dose, time, formulation (immediate or modified release), route, intent and co-ingestants from the patient, witnesses and containers; assume the worst credible exposure.
Opioid: coma, miosis, hypoventilation. Sedative-hypnotic: CNS depression with few other signs. Cholinergic (organophosphates, carbamates): miosis, secretions, bradycardia, fasciculation, weakness.
Anticholinergic: agitated delirium, mydriasis, dry flushed skin, tachycardia, hyperthermia, urinary retention. Sympathomimetic: agitation, mydriasis, tachycardia, hypertension, sweating, hyperthermia, seizures. Serotonin toxicity: clonus, hyperreflexia, tremor, hyperthermia.
Suspect poisoning in any unexplained coma, seizure, arrhythmia, metabolic acidosis or hyperthermia.
# Resuscitation and toxic cardiac arrest
ABCDE approach with continuous ECG and SpO2 monitoring; capillary glucose in every patient with altered consciousness; early 12-lead ECG and blood gas.
Cardiac arrest: standard BLS and ALS, giving indicated antidotes alongside (sodium bicarbonate, calcium, lipid emulsion, digoxin Fab). Consider VA-ECMO (ECPR) for refractory arrest or cardiogenic shock from poisoning where available (AHA 2023).
Opioid respiratory arrest with a pulse: ventilate and give naloxone. In opioid cardiac arrest, CPR and ventilation take priority over naloxone (AHA 2023).
Intubate for inability to protect the airway or ventilatory failure. Do not intubate solely to give charcoal to a patient not expected to develop significant toxicity (CTRC 2026).
Seizures: benzodiazepines first line; phenytoin is not recommended for toxic seizures. Sympathomimetic agitation: benzodiazepines, and rapid active cooling if hyperthermic.
Hypotension: fluid bolus, then vasopressors, while looking for a specific antidote. Skin or eye exposure: remove clothing and irrigate with water; staff wear protective equipment.
# Investigations
12-lead ECG: QRS above 100 ms (sodium channel blockade), QTc prolongation, AV block, bradycardia or tachyarrhythmia; repeat serially.
Electrolytes, renal and liver function, glucose, blood gas with lactate, anion gap, measured osmolality (osmolal gap for toxic alcohols), CK, and a pregnancy test in women of childbearing age.
Paracetamol concentration at 4 hours or later after every intentional overdose; salicylate, lithium, digoxin, iron, theophylline, valproate or carbamazepine levels when relevant.
Urine drug screens rarely change management: a positive result may reflect past use and a negative one does not exclude poisoning.
Chest X-ray for aspiration or pulmonary oedema; CT head for persistent unexplained coma or suspected head injury.
# Gastrointestinal decontamination
Single-dose activated charcoal is not routine (AACT/EAPCCT): consider it when a potentially toxic amount of a charcoal-adsorbed poison has been taken, ideally within 1 hour. The 2026 Clinical Toxicology Recommendations Collaborative supports use up to 6 hours for many poisons, and later if absorption is ongoing (modified release, pharmacobezoar).
Dose: 50 g (range 25 to 100 g) orally or by nasogastric tube in adults. Contraindicated with an unprotected airway; do not pass a nasogastric tube without intubation solely to give charcoal.
Charcoal has no role for iron, lithium, lead, arsenic, copper, caesium, ethanol, methanol, ethylene glycol or metformin, and must not be used after corrosives or hydrocarbons.
A second charcoal dose to complete decontamination is appropriate after large ingestions of carbamazepine, paracetamol, paraquat, phenobarbital, salicylates, thallium, theophylline, valproic acid and verapamil (CTRC 2026).
Gastric lavage should not be performed routinely, if at all (AACT/EAPCCT 2013). Do not induce vomiting with ipecac and do not use cathartics.
Whole bowel irrigation (polyethylene glycol electrolyte solution 1.5 to 2 L/h in adults by nasogastric tube until the rectal effluent is clear) can be considered for sustained-release or enteric-coated drugs, large iron, lithium or potassium ingestions, and drug packets. Contraindicated with ileus, obstruction, perforation, haemodynamic instability or an unprotected airway.
# Antidotes and toxin-specific therapy (AHA 2023)
Opioids: naloxone titrated to adequate breathing, not full arousal. Paracetamol: acetylcysteine by protocol. Benzodiazepines: supportive care; flumazenil is harmful in undifferentiated coma (seizures, arrhythmia).
Beta blocker or calcium channel blocker shock: high-dose insulin with dextrose, vasopressors, IV calcium (especially calcium channel blockers), glucagon (beta blockers), pacing for bradycardia, and VA-ECMO if refractory.
Sodium channel blocker cardiotoxicity (tricyclics and others) with wide QRS, ventricular arrhythmia or hypotension: hypertonic sodium bicarbonate boluses; lidocaine may be considered for refractory ventricular arrhythmia.
Digoxin with life-threatening arrhythmia, shock or severe hyperkalaemia: digoxin-specific Fab. Cyanide (including smoke inhalation with shock or high lactate): hydroxocobalamin. Symptomatic methaemoglobinaemia: methylene blue.
Local anaesthetic systemic toxicity: 20% lipid emulsion. For other lipophilic drugs, reserve lipid for refractory shock or arrest.
Organophosphates and carbamates: atropine until secretions are dry and the chest is clear, oximes per local protocol (not for pure carbamate poisoning), benzodiazepines for seizures. Sympathomimetics and cocaine: benzodiazepines, cooling, and vasodilators for hypertension.
# Enhanced elimination
Multiple-dose activated charcoal is appropriate for carbamazepine, cardiac glycosides, colchicine, dapsone, phenobarbital, phenytoin, thallium and theophylline (CTRC 2026), if the airway is protected and bowel sounds are present.
Urine alkalinisation (IV sodium bicarbonate to urine pH 7.5 or more, with potassium replacement) is first-line for moderately severe salicylate poisoning that does not need haemodialysis (AACT/EAPCCT).
EXTRIP: extracorporeal treatment, usually intermittent haemodialysis, for severe poisoning with dialysable toxins such as lithium, salicylates, methanol, ethylene glycol, metformin-associated lactic acidosis, valproic acid, theophylline, long-acting barbiturates and thallium; not for digoxin or tricyclics.
Salicylates (EXTRIP): dialysis if concentration above 100 mg/dL (7.2 mmol/L), or above 90 mg/dL (6.5 mmol/L) with impaired kidney function, or with altered mental status, new hypoxaemia, or pH 7.20 or less despite standard therapy.
Lithium (EXTRIP): dialysis if concentration above 4.0 mmol/L with impaired kidney function, or with reduced consciousness, seizures or life-threatening dysrhythmia at any level.
# Disposition and specialist advice
Observe for the expected time course of the agent, longer for modified-release preparations and long-acting opioids; admit symptomatic patients and those needing antidotes or monitoring.
Seek early advice from a poison centre or clinical toxicologist for unfamiliar, severe or mixed poisonings and before extracorporeal treatment.
Every intentional self-poisoning needs a psychosocial and suicide risk assessment before discharge, with advice on restricting access to medicines and pesticides.
India: pesticides (organophosphates, aluminium phosphide, paraquat) are the leading lethal poisons; Indian ISCCM guidance advises observation for at least 24 hours after symptoms resolve, and poisoning is a medicolegal case.
# Pitfalls
Decontamination before resuscitation and airway protection, or charcoal to a drowsy patient with an unprotected airway.
Routine gastric lavage, ipecac or charcoal for poisons it does not bind (iron, lithium, alcohols, corrosives).
Blind flumazenil for undifferentiated coma.
Missing a paracetamol co-ingestion, hypoglycaemia or head injury, or discharging too early after a modified-release ingestion.
# Drugs
Activated charcoal (single dose)
Adults 50 g (range 25 to 100 g) orally or via nasogastric tube, once
Only with a protected airway or an alert, cooperative patient; not for metals, alcohols, corrosives or hydrocarbons.
Activated charcoal (multiple dose)
# Dose
50 to 100 g initially, then at least 12.5 g/h or equivalent (for example 25 g every 2 hours or 50 g every 4 hours) until clinical and laboratory improvement
Stop if bowel sounds are absent; watch for aspiration and obstruction.
Naloxone
# Dose
0.04 to 0.4 mg IV, repeated or increased every 2 to 3 minutes to adequate breathing (total 2 mg or more may be needed); 0.4 to 2 mg IM or intranasal if no IV access
Short-acting: observe for renarcotisation and use an infusion if needed.
Sodium bicarbonate 8.4%
# Dose
1 to 2 mEq/kg (1 to 2 mL/kg) IV bolus, repeated to narrow the QRS; commonly target serum pH 7.45 to 7.55
Sodium channel blocker cardiotoxicity; also urine alkalinisation for salicylates. Monitor potassium.
High-dose insulin (euglycaemic)
Regular insulin 1 unit/kg IV bolus, then 1 unit/kg/h, titrated up to 10 units/kg/h
Give with dextrose and check glucose every 15 to 30 minutes and potassium hourly at first. Beta blocker or calcium channel blocker shock.
Calcium
# Dose
Calcium chloride 10% 10 to 20 mL (1 to 2 g) IV via a large or central vein, or calcium gluconate 10% 30 to 60 mL (3 to 6 g) IV; repeat as needed
Calcium channel blocker toxicity, hyperkalaemia, hydrofluoric acid; monitor ionised calcium.
Lipid emulsion 20%
# Dose
1.5 mL/kg IV over 1 minute, then 0.25 mL/kg/min; repeat the bolus up to twice and double the rate if unstable; maximum about 12 mL/kg
Local anaesthetic systemic toxicity; rescue only for other lipophilic drugs.
Hydroxocobalamin
5 g IV over 15 minutes (70 mg/kg); a second 5 g may be given
Cyanide, including smoke inhalation with shock or high lactate. Red skin and urine; interferes with colorimetric tests.
Methylene blue
# Dose
1 to 2 mg/kg IV over 5 minutes; repeat after 30 to 60 minutes if needed
Symptomatic methaemoglobinaemia. Avoid in G6PD deficiency; serotonin toxicity risk with serotonergic drugs.
Digoxin-specific antibody fragments (Fab)
# Dose
Acute ingestion of unknown amount: 10 vials IV; chronic toxicity in adults: 6 vials; or calculate from dose ingested or serum digoxin
Life-threatening arrhythmia, shock or severe hyperkalaemia.
