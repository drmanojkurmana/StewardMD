# Dysbaric osteonecrosis
Dysbaric osteonecrosis, Musculoskeletal, Diving and hyperbaric medicine / Orthopaedics.
Aliases: caisson disease of bone; diver's bone necrosis.
# Pathophysiology
Dysbaric osteonecrosis is thought to result from impaired blood supply to bone related to decompression, with proposed mechanisms including intraosseous or intravascular gas bubbles, fat embolism and altered coagulation compromising the end-arterial supply of bone. The resulting ischaemia causes death of bone and marrow, and subsequent attempted repair produces the characteristic lesions. Juxta-articular necrosis weakens the subchondral bone, leading to collapse of the articular surface and secondary degenerative joint disease.
# Prognosis
Shaft lesions are usually asymptomatic and stable, but juxta-articular disease can progress to disabling joint collapse requiring arthroplasty; prevention through safe decompression is key.
# Clinical pearls
Avascular necrosis of bone occurring in divers and compressed-air (caisson) workers exposed to repeated or inadequate decompression
It characteristically affects the humeral and femoral heads and the shafts of long bones
Shaft (medullary) lesions are typically asymptomatic, whereas juxta-articular lesions can progress to joint surface collapse and arthritis
There is often a latent period of months to years between exposure and radiological changes
It is associated with cumulative dysbaric exposure and with episodes of decompression sickness
Screening imaging is used in occupationally exposed groups, and established joint disease may require orthopaedic treatment
# Additional differentials
Avascular necrosis from other causes (corticosteroids, alcohol, sickle cell disease)
Osteoarthritis
Bone infarct of another aetiology
Bone tumour or metastasis
Osteomyelitis
# Additional investigations
Plain radiography and MRI to detect and characterise lesions
Occupational history of diving or compressed-air work and decompression incidents
Assessment for other causes of osteonecrosis
Joint imaging where articular collapse is suspected
Surveillance imaging in exposed workers
# Red flags
Joint pain suggesting articular surface collapse
Rapidly progressive joint destruction
Features suggesting an alternative cause such as malignancy or infection
Multiple or bilateral juxta-articular lesions
# Pitfalls
Attributing osteonecrosis to diving without excluding steroids, alcohol and other causes
Overlooking asymptomatic lesions that need monitoring
Missing early juxta-articular disease before collapse
Failing to review decompression practice in affected workers
# Management
Prioritise prevention through safe decompression practice, adherence to dive tables and limitation of cumulative dysbaric exposure, as there is no disease-specific drug therapy
Observe asymptomatic medullary shaft lesions, which are typically stable and require no intervention
Manage juxta-articular lesions before collapse with activity modification, analgesia and joint-preserving surgery such as core decompression where appropriate
Offer arthroplasty or joint replacement for advanced articular surface collapse and secondary degenerative disease
Undertake surveillance imaging with radiography and MRI in occupationally exposed divers and compressed-air workers
Refer to orthopaedics for established joint disease and to diving and hyperbaric medicine for fitness-to-dive assessment
Exclude other causes of osteonecrosis, such as corticosteroid use and alcohol excess, when the diagnosis is considered
