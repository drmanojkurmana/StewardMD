# The Bradyarrhythmias: Disorders of the Atrioventricular Node
The Bradyarrhythmias: Disorders of the Atrioventricular Node, Cardiovascular.
Aliases: AV block; Atrioventricular block; AV conduction block; Heart block; Bradyarrhythmia due to AV node disease.
# Pathophysiology
AV nodal myocytes sit at a relatively high resting membrane potential (~-60 mV), exhibit slow phase-0 upstrokes mediated by L-type calcium current (with a relative lack of fast sodium current), and show spontaneous phase-4 depolarization, conferring backup automaticity. Weak electrical coupling from sparse gap junctions (predominantly connexin-40) plus increased extracellular volume slows conduction and protects the ventricle. The AV junction comprises a transitional (atrionodal) cell zone showing decremental conduction, the compact node, and the penetrating His bundle; conduction continues through the His, right and left bundle branches, and the fast-conducting Purkinje network (1-3 m/s). Bradycardia and block arise when conduction across this axis is compromised by fibrosis/sclerosis, calcification (Lev, Lenegre disease), ischemia/infarction, infiltrative or inflammatory disease, drugs, heightened vagal tone, or congenital/genetic abnormalities; consequences range from fatigue and syncope to asystolic death if no reliable escape emerges. Functional (autonomic/metabolic/drug) causes are often reversible while structural fibrosis is typically permanent.
# Prognosis
Prognosis depends heavily on the level of block. AV-nodal (proximal) block - including Mobitz I and most inferior-MI-related block - tends to have a faster, stable narrow-complex escape and is often reversible with a favorable outcome. Infranodal (distal His-Purkinje) block, Mobitz II, and high-grade block in anterior MI carry unstable wide-complex escape rhythms, higher mortality, and a risk of sudden asystolic death, so pacing is indicated even when asymptomatic. Functional/reversible causes generally resolve; structural fibrotic disease is usually permanent and progressive.
# Severity classification
Electrocardiographic grading: First-degree AV block - all atrial impulses conduct but PR is prolonged (>200 ms), delay usually within the AV node. Second-degree AV block - intermittent failure of AV conduction; Type I/Mobitz I/Wenckebach (progressive PR prolongation until a dropped beat, usually AV-nodal) and Type II/Mobitz II (fixed PR before a dropped beat, usually infranodal, often with wide QRS). Third-degree (complete) AV block - complete AV dissociation with a ventricular (escape) rate slower than the atrial rate.
# Clinical pearls
The AV node is specialized for slow conduction, creating a delay between atrial and ventricular activation and acting as the electrical gatekeeper to the ventricle; its intrinsic backup pacemaker (escape) rate is roughly 20-60 beats/min, normally overridden by the faster sinus node (60-100 beats/min).
First-degree AV block means every atrial impulse still conducts but with an abnormally prolonged PR interval (>200 ms); the term block is a misnomer since conduction is delayed, not interrupted.
Mobitz I (Wenckebach) second-degree block shows progressive PR lengthening before a dropped beat with grouped beating and progressive RR shortening; it is typically within the AV node, hemodynamically stable, and usually does not warrant pacing when asymptomatic.
Mobitz II second-degree block shows a fixed PR interval before a dropped beat, is usually infranodal with a wider QRS, carries a risk of sudden death from an unreliable escape rhythm, and warrants permanent pacing even without symptoms.
In 2:1 AV block the ECG cannot reliably distinguish type I from type II; a PR <160 ms with a wide QRS before the conducted beat favors infranodal (type II) block.
QRS width of the escape rhythm localizes the block: a narrow QRS escape implies block in the AV node or proximal His with a faster, more stable junctional rhythm, whereas a wide QRS escape (without preexisting bundle branch block) implies distal His or bundle branch block and a slower, less reliable rhythm.
