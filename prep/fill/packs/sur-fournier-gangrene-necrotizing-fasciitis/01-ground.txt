Fournier gangrene is dying fascia, not dying skin. This rapidly progressive polymicrobial necrotizing fasciitis of the perineal, perianal, and external genital regions combines aerobic and anaerobic bacteria synergistically, with each species shielding the others from immunity and antibiotics. Pain beyond the visible erythema is the warning that fascia is already dead beneath intact skin.

Vessels clot before skin dies. Bacterial toxins cause endarteritis obliterans, thrombosing subcutaneous vessels, and the resulting ischemia advances tissue necrosis at 2 to 3 cm per hour. The process is self-accelerating: dead tissue feeds bacteria, bacteria release more toxin, and the front races outward until radical surgery interrupts it.

The testes characteristically survive. They are spared because testicular arteries arise directly from the aorta, independent of the thrombosed perineal fascial circulation. Finding viable testes in a sea of dead scrotal skin is diagnostic theater: it confirms Fournier gangrene and predicts that buried testes can be preserved or pocketed in thigh pouches.

Surgery cannot wait for morning. Immediate radical debridement of all necrotic tissue back to viable bleeding margins is the single most critical determinant of survival, and re-look operations follow until the wound stays clean. Antibiotics never substitute for the knife, because no drug reaches thrombosed dead tissue.

Severity is scored on admission. The Fournier Gangrene Severity Index scores 9 physiological parameters, temperature, heart rate, respiration, sodium, potassium, creatinine, hematocrit, leukocyte count, and bicarbonate, and a total above 9 predicts over 75 percent mortality. The score triages intensive care, counseling, and the threshold for diverting stomas.

Antibiotics cover everything plus toxin shutdown. Empiric broad-spectrum cover must include MRSA, gram-negative coliforms, Pseudomonas, and anaerobes, typically piperacillin-tazobactam plus vancomycin plus clindamycin, with clindamycin added specifically to switch off bacterial toxin synthesis. De-escalation follows cultures, but the first doses are deliberately maximal.

Diversion protects the wound. Urinary diversion by suprapubic or urethral catheter and fecal diversion by diverting colostomy or fecal management system keep urine and stool off the debrided wound. Without diversion, every void reseeds the field and grafts fail; with it, the wound granulates cleanly toward closure or grafting.

Urosepsis follows the sepsis bundle. Resuscitation adheres to Surviving Sepsis guidance: 30 mL per kg of crystalloids within 3 hours, blood cultures and broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics within 1 hour, and urgent surgical source control of the infected urinary focus. The bundle converts a chaotic presentation into timed actions, and each hour of antibiotic delay raises mortality.
