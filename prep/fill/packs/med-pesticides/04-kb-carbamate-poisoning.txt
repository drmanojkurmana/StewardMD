# Carbamate poisoning
Carbamate poisoning, Multisystem / Toxicology, Emergency medicine / Toxicology.
Aliases: carbamate insecticide poisoning; carbamate cholinergic toxicity.
# Pathophysiology
Carbamates bind and inhibit acetylcholinesterase, causing accumulation of acetylcholine at muscarinic and nicotinic synapses and producing the cholinergic syndrome. In contrast to organophosphates, the carbamate-enzyme bond does not undergo irreversible ageing and dissociates spontaneously within hours, so cholinesterase activity recovers relatively quickly. This reversibility explains the generally shorter course and the reduced role for oxime therapy compared with organophosphate poisoning.
# Prognosis
Because enzyme inhibition is reversible, prognosis is generally better than for organophosphates, and most patients recover within a day or two with prompt atropinisation and respiratory support.
# Clinical pearls
Carbamates inhibit acetylcholinesterase and cause a cholinergic toxidrome similar to organophosphate poisoning
Features combine muscarinic effects (salivation, lacrimation, urination, diarrhoea, bronchorrhoea and bronchospasm), nicotinic effects (fasciculations and weakness) and central effects
Bronchorrhoea and respiratory failure are the main causes of death
Unlike organophosphates, the enzyme inhibition is reversible and self-limiting, so recovery is usually faster
Atropine is the mainstay to counter muscarinic effects; routine use of an oxime is generally considered unnecessary
The classic intermediate syndrome and delayed neuropathy of organophosphates are not typical of carbamates
# Additional differentials
Organophosphate poisoning
Nerve agent exposure
Cholinergic mushroom (muscarinic) poisoning
Asthma or acute pulmonary oedema (for respiratory features)
Other causes of coma with excess secretions
# Additional investigations
Clinical recognition of the cholinergic toxidrome and exposure history
Red-cell (and plasma) cholinesterase activity where available
Oxygenation and airway assessment given bronchorrhoea risk
Cardiac monitoring
Assessment for co-ingestants, especially the solvent carrier
Red flags: Copious respiratory secretions or respiratory failure; Bradyarrhythmia or haemodynamic instability; Depressed consciousness with an unprotected airway; Seizures.
# Pitfalls
Assuming the prolonged course and oxime need of organophosphate poisoning
Under-atropinising and failing to dry pulmonary secretions
Overlooking the toxic hydrocarbon solvent carrier
Delaying airway support in a patient with bronchorrhoea
# Management
Atropine as the mainstay to reverse muscarinic effects, titrated to drying of secretions and adequate oxygenation
Airway protection, oxygen and mechanical ventilation for bronchorrhoea and respiratory failure, the main causes of death
Decontamination with removal of contaminated clothing and skin washing, using staff personal protection
Oximes generally unnecessary given the reversible, spontaneously dissociating enzyme inhibition
Benzodiazepines for seizures or agitation
Cardiac and respiratory monitoring during the expected short course of recovery
Consideration of co-ingestants, particularly the hydrocarbon solvent carrier
