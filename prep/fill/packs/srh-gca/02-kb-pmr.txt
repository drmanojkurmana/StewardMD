# Polymyalgia rheumatica
Polymyalgia rheumatica, Rheumatology.
# Red flags
Watch for GCA — visual symptoms need urgent steroids
# Investigations
ESR/CRP (markedly raised)
Assess for giant cell arteritis
# Pathophysiology
PMR sits on the same disease spectrum as giant cell arteritis, an antigen-driven panarteritis of medium- and large-sized vessels with mononuclear infiltrates and frequent giant cell formation. Activated CD4+ T lymphocytes, macrophages, and dendritic cells are central; restricted clonal T-cell receptor expansion suggests an antigen residing in the arterial wall. Two CD4 T-cell lineages (IFN-gamma-producing TH1 and IL-17-producing TH17) participate in vascular inflammation, with IL-6 signaling driving hepatic acute-phase protein (CRP, and effects on ESR) production. Familial aggregation and HLA-DR4 / HLA-DRB1*04 associations have been reported for the GCA end of the spectrum.
# Prognosis
Isolated PMR generally responds promptly to low-dose treatment, and most patients can be managed without IL-6-directed agents. Recurrent polymyalgia symptoms develop in the majority of patients during steroid tapering. The principal long-term concern is progression toward giant cell arteritis (10-20% of initially isolated PMR), which carries risks of visual loss and, late, thoracic aortic aneurysm/dissection.
# Clinical pearls
PMR causes stiffness, aching, and pain in the muscles of the neck, shoulders, lower back, hips, and thighs in a patient typically aged >50 years.
PMR and giant cell arteritis are regarded as different clinical expressions of a single underlying disease process, supported by clinical association and pathophysiologic data.
Although PMR most often occurs in isolation, it is found in roughly 40-50% of patients with giant cell arteritis.
About 10-20% of patients presenting initially with apparently isolated PMR later go on to develop giant cell arteritis, so reassessment for GCA features should occur at follow-up visits.
Inflammatory stiffness in PMR tends to be prolonged (lasting hours) and improves with activity, in contrast to the brief gel-phenomenon stiffness (<=45 min, worse with activity) of noninflammatory disorders such as osteoarthritis.
Fatigue can be profound in PMR, mirroring other inflammatory conditions.
Annual incidence of PMR in those aged >=50 years was 58.7 per 100,000 in Olmsted County, Minnesota.
# Additional differentials
Seronegative rheumatoid arthritis in the elderly, which can be difficult to distinguish from PMR; RA predominantly affects wrists/hands and ankles/feet at onset whereas PMR is girdle-predominant.
Remitting seronegative symmetrical synovitis with pitting edema (RS3PE) — prominent distal limb pitting edema, prompt response to low-dose prednisone.
Other inflammatory arthropathies / seronegative RA in patients who develop a worsening pattern of peripheral arthritis.
Fibromyalgia — listed among conditions confused with PMR; PMR appears in the fibromyalgia differential.
Osteoarthritis and other noninflammatory nonarticular conditions (bursitis, tendinitis).
# Non infectious mimics
Inflammatory myopathy/myositis (polymyositis): PMR is included among nonarticular conditions to consider, but unlike myopathy it is not associated with true muscle weakness, and serum CK, EMG, and muscle biopsy are normal in PMR.
Fibromyalgia: a painful, noninflammatory myofascial syndrome with normal CK, ESR, EMG, and muscle biopsy — distinguished from PMR by normal inflammatory markers.
Paraneoplastic syndromes (noted as mimics in the elderly inflammatory-arthritis context).
Chronic tophaceous gout and hepatitis C-related arthropathy (mimics of inflammatory arthritis in the elderly).
# Additional investigations
Rheumatoid factor and anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide (anti-CCP) should be negative; positive results point toward seronegative-RA spectrum or other inflammatory arthropathy.
Serum creatine kinase is NOT elevated in PMR; elevation should prompt consideration of a myopathic process.
Normochromic or slightly hypochromic anemia and elevated alkaline phosphatase (liver function abnormalities) may accompany the inflammatory state in the GCA/PMR spectrum.
If GCA is suspected, evaluate temporal arteries (biopsy, or ultrasonography showing a noncompressible 'halo' sign) and consider vascular imaging (MR/CT, PET investigational) for large-vessel disease; therapy should not be delayed pending these studies.
# Red flags
New or worsening peripheral arthritis pattern — reconsider seronegative RA or another inflammatory arthropathy rather than PMR.
Elevated serum CK or true muscle weakness — argues against PMR and suggests inflammatory myopathy.
Jaw or tongue claudication, scalp tenderness, new headache, or a tender/thickened/nodular temporal artery — signal evolution to GCA with risk of ischemic optic neuropathy and sudden blindness.
Arm claudication, diminished pulses, or bruits — may indicate large-vessel (e.g., subclavian) GCA involvement or aortic aneurysm risk.
# Pitfalls
Treating an IL-6-inhibitor-exposed patient: blockade of IL-6 signaling normalizes CRP and ESR, so these markers can no longer be used to monitor disease activity or detect infection.
Minor rises in ESR/CRP during tapering do not necessarily indicate disease exacerbation, particularly in an asymptomatic patient; ESR/CRP can also rise from infection.
Assuming PMR explains distal/peripheral arthritis — PMR can mimic RA, and persistent or worsening peripheral arthritis warrants reconsideration.
# Confirm
Bilateral shoulder/hip girdle pain and stiffness in age >50, raised ESR/CRP, dramatic steroid response. Screen for coexisting GCA.
Outpatient; rheumatology if atypical/relapsing.
Low-dose oral corticosteroid with slow taper
# Steps
Start prednisone 10-20 mg/day for isolated PMR — a prompt, near-complete response supports the diagnosis.
Taper gradually guided by symptoms and ESR/CRP; recurrent symptoms during tapering occur in the majority, so taper cautiously.
Screen for giant cell arteritis at every visit (headache, jaw/tongue claudication, visual symptoms) as PMR and GCA are a single disease spectrum.
Provide glucocorticoid-related risk management: bone protection (calcium/vitamin D +/- bisphosphonate) and glucose monitoring.
For glucocorticoid-resistant or relapsing disease use an IL-6 receptor inhibitor (sarilumab); methotrexate gives only marginal steroid-sparing benefit.
