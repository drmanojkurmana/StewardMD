Exam grounding notes for local flap geometry, written for grounding, not for display. Paraphrased from Plastic Surgery 5E Volume 1 on flap physiology and classification.

Surgeons group local flaps by their primary movement: transposition flaps that jump over intact skin, rotation flaps that pivot along an arc, advancement flaps that slide straight forward, and interpolation flaps that cross over or under a skin bridge. Each family has its own geometry rules.

The classic Z-plasty with a 60 degree central limb and 60 degree arms lengthens the scar by a theoretical 75 percent while swinging the scar axis through 90 degrees. Narrower designs trade lengthening for a smaller footprint: a 45 degree Z-plasty yields 50 percent lengthening, while a 30 degree Z-plasty yields only 25 percent lengthening of the central limb.

Drawn as a mirror image off one edge of the defect, the Limberg design rotates into an equilateral parallelogram whose corners measure 60 degrees and 120 degrees. Equal sides share tension evenly around the closure.

Dufourmentel redrew the rhomboid so the plan halves the angle formed by the defect extension line and its side. The tweak shrinks the standing cone of excess skin and eases donor closure compared with the classic drawing.

The bilobed flap shifts tissue through two consecutive lobes that rotate around a single shared pivot point. It is a workhorse for nasal tip and nasal sidewall defects under 1.5 cm, where a single lobe would distort the ala. The first lobe fills the defect and the second fills the first lobe donor site.

The V-Y advancement flap drives a triangular skin island forward in a straight line along its subcutaneous vascular pedicle. As the island advances, the trailing donor defect is closed as a straight Y stem behind it. No rotation means no pincushioning.

A rotation flap sweeps tissue along the arc of a circle centred on its pivot point. To close without tension, the flap perimeter needs to measure approximately four to five times the defect width, with a wide base preserving blood supply. Back-cuts extend reach but narrow the base.
