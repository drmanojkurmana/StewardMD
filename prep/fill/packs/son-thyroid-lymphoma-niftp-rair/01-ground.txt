Primary thyroid lymphoma, basics

- Rare. Around 1% to 5% of thyroid malignancies, and a small share of lymphomas that start outside lymph nodes.
- Commonly sits on a background of Hashimoto thyroiditis. About 55% of cases carry that diagnosis.
- Usual picture: a fast-growing goitre, with neck fullness, trouble swallowing and a hoarse voice.
- B symptoms (fever, weight loss, drenching night sweats) appear in roughly 11% of patients.
- On ultrasound it can look like Hashimoto, with low echogenicity and ill-defined edges. Heavy internal blood flow and no calcification favour lymphoma over anaplastic cancer.

Diagnosis

- Fine needle sampling alone is often not enough. When suspicion is high, take a core biopsy of the gland.
- Send the core for flow cytometry and immunohistochemistry to identify the lymphoma type.
- Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) is the most frequent subtype in the thyroid.
- A less common second type is MALT lymphoma, a marginal zone B-cell lymphoma arising in mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue. The WHO classification lists it as its own entity.

Treatment logic

- Surgery is mainly diagnostic. Resection as first treatment is avoided because it can spread a lymphoma that was still limited to the gland.
- Lymphoma here is often very sensitive to external beam radiation.
- Once staging shows disease outside the thyroid, follow the pathways used for other lymphomas.

Iodine and follicular carcinoma

- Follicular thyroid carcinoma (FTC) is more common where dietary iodine is low.
- Diagnosis rests on capsular or vascular invasion seen under the microscope.
- Nuclear appearance does not separate it from a follicular adenoma, so the call needs tissue.

NIFTP

- NIFTP is the acronym for a lesion once filed as an encapsulated follicular variant of papillary carcinoma; the full label describes a non-invasive tumour with papillary-like nuclei.
- The lesion lacks capsular and vascular invasion and has been reclassified as not malignant.
- In WHO grading it sits in the low-risk follicular group, not the cancer group.
- Radioactive iodine is kept for tumours with higher risk traits such as blood vessel invasion, spread beyond the gland, big nodal deposits, larger size or aggressive subtypes.

Radioiodine-refractory differentiated thyroid cancer

- Between 25% and 50% of people whose differentiated thyroid cancer has spread widely eventually stop responding to RAI.
- A tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) is used for progressive, symptomatic or rapidly growing disease.
- DECISION trial: sorafenib against placebo in progressive radioiodine-refractory disease.
- SELECT trial: lenvatinib against placebo in the same setting.
- COSMIC-311 trial (trial number 311): cabozantinib given after earlier vascular endothelial growth factor receptor (VEGFR) therapy.
- Sorafenib (approved in 2013) and lenvatinib (approved in 2015) both lengthened progression-free survival versus placebo.
- Airway invasion should be assessed before starting a TKI because bleeding can occur.

Targeted options by mutation

- BRAF V600E: dabrafenib with trametinib, used in anaplastic thyroid cancer.
- Dabrafenib alone can restore RAI uptake in about 60% of BRAF V600E-mutant RAI-refractory papillary cancers.
- RET fusion: selpercatinib or pralsetinib.
- NTRK fusion: larotrectinib or entrectinib, approved across solid tumours.
