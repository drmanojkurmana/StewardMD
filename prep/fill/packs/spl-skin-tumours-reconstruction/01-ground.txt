Exam grounding notes for skin malignancy margins and Mohs surgery, written for grounding, not for display. Paraphrased from Plastic Surgery 5E Volume 1 on cutaneous oncology.

Basal cell carcinoma is the most common human skin malignancy. Nodular and superficial variants behave indolently, while aggressive infiltrating and morpheaform patterns thread widely through dermis. Subtype predicts the needed margin.

A low-risk primary BCC is cleared with a standard 4 mm surgical margin, which achieves a 95 percent complete histological clearance rate. Well-defined nodular lesions on the trunk suit this rule; facial and recurrent lesions need more.

High-risk cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma earns wider surgery when it shows diameter over 2 cm, depth beyond 4 mm, perineural invasion, or poorly differentiated histology. Such tumours take 6 to 10 mm margins because microscopic spread outruns the visible edge.

Mohs micrographic surgery examines 100 percent of the peripheral and deep margins using horizontal frozen sections taken in mapped layers. The technique maximises cure while sparing tissue, which matters most in cosmetically sensitive facial zones.

Melanoma excision width keys to Breslow depth: melanoma in situ takes 5 mm, Breslow under 1.0 mm takes a 1 cm margin, Breslow 1.01 to 2.0 mm takes 1 to 2 cm, and Breslow over 2.0 mm takes a 2 cm margin. Depth measured in millimetres sets centimetres of surgery.

Sentinel lymph node biopsy is recommended for cutaneous melanomas of Breslow thickness 0.8 mm or greater, or under 0.8 mm when ulceration is present. Ulceration upstages thin lesions into the biopsy group.

Breslow tumour thickness, measured from the granular cell layer down to the deepest invasive tumour cell, stands as the single most important histological prognostic factor in primary localised melanoma. Every report leads with it.

Marjolin ulcer is an aggressive squamous cell carcinoma arising within a chronic non-healing burn scar, a chronic osteomyelitis sinus tract, or a venous stasis ulcer. Long-standing inflammation turns malignant, and these tumours metastasise early.
