# Catheter-Associated Urinary Tract Infection
Catheter-Associated Urinary Tract Infection, Genitourinary.
# Pathogens
Catheter-associated UTI involves a broader and more resistant organism spectrum than uncomplicated cystitis due to biofilm formation on the catheter surface and selection pressure from prior antibiotic exposure, with polymicrobial infection common in long-term catheterization.
Very likely: Escherichia coli; Klebsiella pneumoniae; Proteus mirabilis.
# Likely
Pseudomonas aeruginosa
Enterococcus species
Candida species (colonization common, true infection less so)
Possible: Polymicrobial flora, especially with long-term catheterization.
# Investigations
Urine culture (from newly placed catheter if replacing, or freshly collected sample)
Essential given the high resistance burden and biofilm-related polymicrobial colonization in this population — guides definitive therapy.
Blood cultures (if systemically unwell), CA-UTI can be a source of bacteremia/urosepsis; positive cultures confirm systemic involvement.
Renal function tests, Baseline assessment, particularly relevant if upper tract involvement (pyelonephritis) is suspected.
Antibiotics recommended
# Reason
Fever (often the only symptom, since dysuria is typically absent or unreliable with a catheter in place) in a catheterized patient, without an alternative explanation, is consistent with catheter-associated UTI. Critically, this diagnosis requires the presence of symptoms/signs attributable to UTI — asymptomatic bacteriuria in a catheterized patient is extremely common and should NOT be treated with antibiotics.
# Pathophysiology
Urinary catheters and other foreign bodies (stones, stents, nephrostomy tubes) provide an inert surface for bacterial colonization and formation of a persistent biofilm, and catheterization dramatically increases bacterial entry into the urinary tract. The catheter serves as a conduit allowing organisms to ascend into the bladder, making bacteriuria nearly universal during catheterization. Biofilm on the catheter shelters organisms and acts as a nidus for reinfection, which is why catheter exchange or removal is central to clearing infection. The microbiology is more diverse than in uncomplicated cystitis; while E. coli remains predominant, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, enterococci, Staphylococcus aureus, and Candida are important additional pathogens.
