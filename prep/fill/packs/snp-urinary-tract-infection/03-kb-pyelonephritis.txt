# Pathogens
Pyelonephritis pathogens largely mirror uncomplicated UTI but with greater clinical consequence of undertreatment, since ascending infection from the same uropathogens can progress to bacteremia and sepsis.
Very likely: Escherichia coli.
Likely: Klebsiella pneumoniae; Proteus mirabilis.
Possible: Enterococcus species; Pseudomonas aeruginosa (catheter/structural risk).

# Pathophysiology
Pyelonephritis usually develops as an ascending infection: uropathogens move from the urethra to the bladder and then continue up the ureter to the renal parenchyma, which is the route for most renal parenchymal infection. Its microbiology therefore mirrors that of acute cystitis. A minority arises hematogenously (<2% of UTIs overall), typically with relatively virulent organisms such as Staphylococcus aureus and Salmonella; isolation of either from urine should prompt consideration of concomitant bacteremia, and hematogenous seeding can produce focal renal abscesses. Anatomic factors causing urinary obstruction or retention (congenital anomalies, cystocele, pelvic-organ prolapse, neurogenic bladder, benign prostatic hypertrophy) impede the protective flushing action of micturition and predispose to renal involvement. A foreign body such as a stone or catheter provides an inert surface for colonization.
# Prognosis
Most pyelonephritis treated effectively resolves, though fever may take 5-7 days to clear completely even with appropriate therapy. Renal parenchymal damage and loss of renal function are uncommon and occur primarily in association with urinary obstruction (preexisting, or, rarely, de novo in diabetic patients with papillary necrosis). Chronic bacterial pyelonephritis is rarely a cause of chronic renal failure because it seldom affects both kidneys. Xanthogranulomatous pyelonephritis arises from chronic obstruction (often staghorn calculus) plus chronic infection causing suppurative destruction of renal tissue and frequently requires nephrectomy.
# Severity classification
Distinct severe/complicated entities include emphysematous pyelonephritis (gas-forming, almost exclusively in poorly controlled diabetes), xanthogranulomatous pyelonephritis (chronic obstruction plus infection causing suppurative renal destruction), and pyelonephritis complicated by papillary necrosis with obstruction or by intrarenal/perinephric abscess. Acute pyelonephritis is one of the systemic UTI presentations that may be grouped as urosepsis or febrile UTI, with or without bacteremia.
# Clinical pearls
Fever is the principal feature distinguishing pyelonephritis from simple cystitis; presence of fever, back/flank pain, rigors, nausea or vomiting signals infection that has extended beyond the bladder.
Severity spans a wide spectrum: mild disease may present only with low-grade fever with or without costovertebral angle pain, whereas severe disease manifests with high fever, rigors, nausea, vomiting, and flank pain; onset is generally acute and may or may not be preceded by cystitis symptoms.
Pyelonephritis represents tissue-invasive disease and patients are often systemically ill, so the threat of progression to bacteremia and urosepsis is real.
In a woman presenting with at least one localizing urinary symptom (dysuria, frequency, hematuria, or back pain) and no complicating factors, the combined probability of acute cystitis or pyelonephritis is about 50%.
Men with febrile UTI should undergo upper-tract imaging (CT or ultrasound) if not done previously, to evaluate for urinary tract abnormality or obstruction, and elevated PSA with enlarged prostate/seminal vesicles suggests prostatic involvement.
Pregnant patients are at unusually high risk of developing pyelonephritis, which can adversely affect pregnancy outcome (preterm delivery, low birth weight); this underlies routine prenatal screening for asymptomatic bacteriuria.
# Additional differentials
Acute prostatitis in men presenting with febrile UTI
Complicated UTI (fever in a patient with structural/functional urinary abnormality, immunocompromise, or instrumentation)
Sexually transmitted infection mimicking UTI, particularly Chlamydia trachomatis cervicitis/urethritis
Renal/ureteral calculus with obstruction (renal colic) presenting with flank pain and pyuria
# Infection mimics
Acute prostatitis
Cervicitis or urethritis due to Chlamydia trachomatis or Neisseria gonorrhoeae
Vaginitis (Candida albicans, Trichomonas vaginalis) or herpetic urethritis causing dysuria
# Non infectious mimics
Renal/ureteral stone with proximal obstruction, which may present exactly as acute pyelonephritis
Interstitial cystitis or noninfectious vulvovaginal irritation causing dysuria
Sloughed renal papilla or blood clot causing ureteral obstruction without infection
Musculoskeletal flank/back pain, herpes zoster, abdominal aortic aneurysm, duodenal ulcer (differential for flank pain)
# Additional investigations
Upper-tract imaging (CT or renal ultrasound) to evaluate for obstruction, abscess, or anatomic abnormality, especially in men, in patients not responding within 72 hours, or when complicated infection is suspected
Urine dipstick (nitrite, leukocyte esterase) and microscopic urinalysis as point-of-care tests; note nitrites detect only Enterobacterales and may be falsely negative with non-Enterobacterales organisms
Imaging for gas in renal/perinephric tissues when emphysematous pyelonephritis is suspected in a diabetic patient
# Red flags
Acute papillary necrosis with sloughed papillae obstructing the ureter, producing obstructive uropathy that requires intervention to relieve obstruction and preserve renal function
UTI in the setting of ureteral obstruction is a medical emergency requiring immediate restoration of drainage by ureteral stent or percutaneous nephrostomy
Emphysematous pyelonephritis: gas production in renal and perinephric tissues, occurring almost exclusively in patients with poorly controlled diabetes; a particularly severe form
Continued fever and/or bacteremia despite antibacterial therapy should raise suspicion for intraparenchymal, perinephric, or renal cortical abscess
Failure of clinical response within 72 hours warrants evaluation for an uncontrolled source of infection
Persistently elevated/increasing fever, flank pain, and rising neutrophil counts should prompt evaluation for intrarenal/perinephric abscess and/or obstruction
Bilateral papillary necrosis may present first as a rapid rise in serum creatinine
# Pitfalls
Treating presumed UTI when the true cause is an STI (e.g., C. trachomatis); vaginal discharge should prompt STI consideration since symptoms alone do not reliably distinguish them.
Stopping the workup at an abnormal urinalysis in a catheterized or febrile patient: pyuria and bacteriuria are expected with catheterization and do not by themselves establish UTI as the cause of fever.
Mistaking an obstructing stone with proximal infection for uncomplicated pyelonephritis and missing the need for emergent drainage.
Misattributing xanthogranulomatous pyelonephritis to renal malignancy because its mass effect can mimic tumor.
Delayed/withheld antimicrobial therapy in true UTI carries a small increased risk of progression to pyelonephritis.
# Deescalation
Once urine/blood culture results return, narrow to the most targeted oral agent matching susceptibility, and complete the course orally once afebrile for 24-48 hours.

# Framework
Send urine culture (and blood culture if febrile/systemically unwell) before starting therapy to allow de-escalation.
Reserve broad-spectrum agents (piperacillin-tazobactam, carbapenems) for patients with risk factors for resistant organisms, not as routine first-line.
Step down to oral therapy once afebrile and clinically improving, rather than completing the full course intravenously by default.
