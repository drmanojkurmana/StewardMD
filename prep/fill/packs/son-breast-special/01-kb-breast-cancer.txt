# Breast Cancer
Breast Cancer, Oncology.
Aliases: breast carcinoma.
# Pathophysiology
Breast cancer is predominantly a disease resulting from prolonged exposure of the breast to estrogen. Most breast cancers begin in epithelial cells within the lobules or ducts and proceed through a conceptual continuum of atypia and hyperplasia to in situ malignancy, then invasion into surrounding tissue, intravasation into lymph and blood channels, spread to regional lymph nodes, and distant metastases (though not all cancers progress through every stage). Hereditary breast cancer susceptibility genes account for 5-10% of cases; these genes participate in DNA damage repair, and inherited autosomal dominant mutations generally cause protein truncation and loss of function of DNA repair proteins. The most common are BRCA1 (chromosome 17q21) and BRCA2 (chromosome 13q12), conferring a 50-80% lifetime risk of invasive breast cancer by age 80 and a 30% risk of ovarian cancer; other germline mutations include TP53 (Li-Fraumeni), PALB2, ATM, STK11 (Peutz-Jeghers), and PTEN (Cowden). Ashkenazi Jews have a higher BRCA mutation incidence.
# Prognosis
5-year relative survival in the United States is currently about 91%. Outcomes are directly related to stage at presentation (and vary by race/ethnicity): 5-year relative survival is over 99% for stage I, about 93% for stage II, about 75% for stage III, and about 29% for stage IV. Less than 10% present with de novo metastatic (M1) disease. Luminal B tumors carry a poorer outcome than luminal A; TNBC carries a poorer prognosis; mucinous and pure medullary cancers have very favorable prognoses with local therapy only; higher Nottingham grade is associated with poorer prognosis. About 20-25% of patients treated for early breast cancer subsequently develop metastatic disease.
# Severity classification
Conceptually, stages I and II are early-stage disease confined to breast and ipsilateral nodes; stage III is locally advanced disease; and stage IV (M1) is metastatic disease. Staging may be clinical (c), pathologic after surgery (p), or post-neoadjuvant (y prefix). Common metastatic sites are soft tissues, lung, bone, liver, and brain.
# Clinical pearls
Most common nonskin cancer in women worldwide; predominantly a disease of prolonged estrogen exposure, with female gender and increasing age the most common risk factors (about 70% diagnosed at 55 years or older, median age 63).
Clinical signs and symptoms suggestive of breast cancer may include a breast lump, skin or nipple changes, or palpable regional nodes; with mammographic screening many patients are asymptomatic and present only with an imaging abnormality (mass, distortion, or suspicious microcalcifications).
Diagnosis rests on pathologic confirmation, generally by image-guided core biopsy, which also assesses tumor grade and morphology and performs biomarker evaluation for estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and HER2 protein expression and potentially HER2 gene amplification (the triple assessment of clinical/imaging/pathology with biomarkers).
Suspicious axillary nodes should be biopsied via image-guided techniques, and both breasts should be imaged because up to 3% of women with newly diagnosed breast cancer have unsuspected contralateral disease.
Invasive breast cancer is divided into at least four molecular subtypes: luminal A and luminal B (both ER positive; luminal B has reduced PR or higher Ki67 proliferation and worse outcome), HER2 subtype (high HER2 expression, benefits from HER2-targeted therapy), and basal/triple-negative (TNBC, lacking ER, PR, and HER2).
Most invasive breast cancers are ductal; about 10-15% are lobular, which are usually ER positive and HER2 negative, distinguished by absence of E-cadherin, and have a propensity to spread to serosal surfaces (omentum, pleura, meninges) and parenchyma (ovaries, GI tract).
Family history is a critical risk factor, yet only 20% of women diagnosed with breast cancer have a family history; a first-degree relative with breast cancer doubles the risk.
Radiotherapy / PMRT (postmastectomy radiotherapy, post-mastectomy radiation therapy) indications, risk-adapted by nodal status and tumour size: 4 or more positive axillary nodes (pN2-3): PMRT to chest wall plus comprehensive regional nodal irradiation is MANDATORY (category 1); 1-3 positive axillary nodes (pN1): strongly consider PMRT to chest wall plus comprehensive regional nodal irradiation; node-negative with pT3 (over 5 cm): consider PMRT; node-negative with pT2 or smaller (5 cm or less) and clear margins: PMRT is NOT indicated (Standard Guidelines, Invasive Breast Cancer, BINV-3; kb/oncotree/breast.json).
TCHP regimen for HER2-positive stage II-III breast cancer (neoadjuvant or adjuvant, anthracycline-free): TCHP = docetaxel + carboplatin + trastuzumab + pertuzumab. Docetaxel 75 mg/m2 IV, carboplatin AUC 6 IV, trastuzumab 8 mg/kg IV loading then 6 mg/kg, pertuzumab 840 mg IV loading then 420 mg, every 21 days for 6 cycles; trastuzumab and pertuzumab continue every 21 days to complete 1 year of HER2 therapy; monitor LVEF at baseline and every 3 months (Standard Guidelines, Invasive Breast Cancer, BINV-M 7 of 10; kb/protocols/breast-tchp.json, experimental, not clinically activated).
# Additional differentials
Inflammatory breast cancer is sometimes confused with mastitis or other breast infection.
Benign breast pathology such as atypical ductal or lobular hyperplasia (raises risk about four- to fivefold) and lobular carcinoma in situ (LCIS, raises risk 7- to 12-fold).
# Infection mimics
Mastitis or other breast infection can be mimicked by inflammatory breast cancer, which presents with rapid onset of erythema, swelling, and tenderness of the breast; breast or skin biopsy is critical when breast inflammation fails to resolve after antibiotics.
# Non infectious mimics
Evolving changes in the pregnant breast can make diagnosis of breast cancer during pregnancy difficult, so suspicious lumps or skin or nipple changes still require formal evaluation.
# Additional investigations
Image-guided core biopsy for pathologic confirmation, tumor grade/morphology, and biomarker testing (ER, PR, HER2 protein, and potentially HER2 gene amplification).
Diagnostic breast imaging: mammography, ultrasonography, and MRI if indicated; bilateral breast imaging because up to 3% have unsuspected contralateral disease.
Germline panel testing for breast cancer susceptibility mutations on DNA from peripheral blood or saliva after appropriate counseling (not recommended for the general population).
For symptoms or findings of advanced disease (large tumor, skin changes, extensive adenopathy): CT and radionuclide bone scanning to look for overt metastatic disease, with biopsy of suspicious lesions to confirm metastatic disease.
Genomic transcriptional profiling assays for early-stage ER-positive disease (Oncotype Dx 21-gene, MammaPrint 70-gene, ProSigna 50-gene, EndoPredict 12-gene, Breast Cancer Index) to inform adjuvant chemotherapy decisions; ordering more than one per case is discouraged.
Skin biopsy showing dermal lymphatic invasion with tumor cells supports diagnosis of inflammatory breast cancer.
# Red flags
Inflammatory breast cancer: rapid onset of erythema, swelling, and tenderness of the breast (a subset of locally advanced disease); breast inflammation failing to resolve after antibiotics warrants breast or skin biopsy, and up to one-third have detectable metastasis at diagnosis.
Any unexplained mass or lesion palpable on physical examination needs further assessment including biopsy where warranted; a normal mammogram does NOT rule out cancer in a woman with a palpable breast lump.
Locally advanced features: large primary tumor, involvement of skin or chest wall, or fixed tumor or axillary nodes.
# Pitfalls
A common disease-specific diagnostic pitfall is ordering a mammogram for a woman with a palpable breast lump and, when it returns normal, reassuring her that cancer has been ruled out; any unexplained palpable mass needs further testing and biopsy where warranted.
Lobular cancers can recur at unusual sites (serosal surfaces, ovaries, GI tract), producing atypical presentations that may delay diagnosis if metastatic disease is not considered.
Up to 3% of newly diagnosed patients have unsuspected contralateral disease, so both breasts must be imaged.
Inflammatory breast cancer may be mistaken for mastitis or infection; biopsy is critical when inflammation fails to resolve after antibiotics.
Germline panel testing can yield variants of unknown significance (VUS) and includes genes with poorly defined clinical risk, causing patient confusion and discomfort.
# Management
Achieve local control with breast-conserving surgery plus whole-breast radiotherapy or mastectomy, combined with sentinel lymph node biopsy or axillary clearance for nodal staging
Give adjuvant endocrine therapy for oestrogen-receptor-positive disease using tamoxifen or an aromatase inhibitor, adding ovarian suppression in selected premenopausal women
Add HER2-directed therapy such as trastuzumab and pertuzumab for HER2-positive tumours, combined with chemotherapy
Use cytotoxic chemotherapy for triple-negative, high-risk, or genomic-assay-selected tumours, with immunotherapy incorporated in triple-negative disease
Treat advanced or metastatic disease with palliative systemic therapy and bone-protective agents such as a bisphosphonate or denosumab for skeletal metastases
Coordinate multidisciplinary care and provide long-term surveillance with clinical review and surveillance mammography of the conserved and contralateral breast
Offer germline BRCA testing with genetic counselling and cascade family screening where a hereditary predisposition is identified
