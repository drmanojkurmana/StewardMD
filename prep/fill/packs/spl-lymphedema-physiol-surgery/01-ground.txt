Lymphedema is protein-rich interstitial fluid that the lymphatics cannot clear, and the International Society of Lymphology stages its march. Stage 0 is subclinical with impaired transport but no swelling, Stage 1 shows reversible pitting edema that elevates away overnight, Stage 2 brings irreversible non-pitting fibrotic edema, and Stage 3 is lymphostatic elephantiasis with warty skin overgrowth.

Mapping decides the operation. Indocyanine green lymphography with near-infrared imaging traces functional dermal lymphatics in real time: healthy channels draw linear patterns, while failing ones leak into splash, stardust, and finally diffuse dermal backflow patterns that mark dead zones.

Physiological surgery reconnects the plumbing while vessels still pump. Lymphovenous anastomosis joins functioning subdermal lymphatic channels of only 0.3 to 0.8 mm directly to adjacent venules under the high-power microscope, letting lymph drain into the low-pressure venous system through supermicrosurgery finer than a hair.

Timing decides LVA success. The operation works best in early disease, ISL Stage 1 and early Stage 2, where the lymphatic vessels keep their contractility and patent lumens. Severe intraluminal sclerosis turns the channels into fibrous cords that no anastomosis can revive.

When local channels are gone, importing nodes restarts drainage. Vascularized lymph node transfer microvascularly transplants whole lymph node basins, such as supraclavicular, omental, or lateral thoracic packets, into the affected limb on their own blood supply.

The transferred nodes work as living pumps and factories. They drive lymphangiogenesis through VEGF-C secretion that sprouts new channels, while an internal pump-sponge mechanism wicks pooled lymph into the venous circulation through the flap vessels.

Burnt-out fibro-fatty limbs need debulking, not plumbing. Late Stage 2 with heavy fibro-adipose deposition and Stage 3 elephantiasis are treated with circumferential suction-assisted lipectomy under tourniquet control, removing litres of diseased fat through small incisions.

Neglected lymphedema can turn malignant.
