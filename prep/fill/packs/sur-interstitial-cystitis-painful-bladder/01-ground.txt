Bladder pain syndrome is defined by time and exclusion. Interstitial cystitis or bladder pain syndrome means chronic pelvic pain, pressure, or discomfort perceived to come from the bladder, with lower urinary tract symptoms lasting over 6 weeks and no infection to explain them. The definition matters because urgency and frequency alone point elsewhere, while pain plus duration points here.

Hunner lesions mark the ulcer subtype. They appear in 5 to 10 percent of patients at cystoscopy as circumscribed reddened mucosal patches with radiating capillaries that tear and bleed when the bladder is distended with fluid. Finding one changes management completely, because lesion-directed burning or steroid injection brings rapid relief that diffuse disease rarely achieves.

Glomerulations do not diagnose the disease. These petechial submucosal bleeds appear after low-pressure hydrodistension and were once considered characteristic, but they also occur in healthy bladders and in overactive bladder. They are non-specific and no longer mandatory or pathognomonic, so a bladder full of glomerulations without pain history does not make the diagnosis.

The barrier theory explains the pain. Disruption of the protective urothelial glycosaminoglycan layer lets urinary solutes, especially potassium, seep into the submucosa where they excite sensory C-fibers and degranulate mast cells. Each void then bathes raw nerve endings in potassium-rich urine, which is why the pain tracks filling and eases after voiding.

One oral drug repairs the barrier specifically. Pentosan polysulfate sodium, Elmiron 100 mg three times daily, is the only oral disease-specific therapy approved by regulators, and it works by replenishing the glycosaminoglycan coating. Long-term use demands baseline and periodic eye screening because pigmentary maculopathy can develop silently and threaten vision.

Pain modulation comes from an antidepressant. Amitriptyline 25 to 75 mg at night relieves symptoms through anticholinergic, antihistaminic, and central pain-dampening actions, helping the urgency, frequency, and night-time voiding together. Sedation limits daytime dosing, so the whole dose is given at night, and the benefit builds over weeks rather than days.

Ulcer disease is burned or injected directly. Endoscopic fulguration with electrocautery or laser ablation of Hunner lesions, or triamcinolone injected into the submucosa beneath them, produces rapid and profound remission in ulcer-type disease. The effect outlasts systemic drugs because the diseased patch itself is destroyed, though new lesions elsewhere need repeat treatment.

Bladder instillations treat the surface repeatedly. Dimethyl sulfoxide, heparin, lidocaine, and sodium hyaluronate are instilled through a catheter and held in the bladder, where they restore the barrier, calm C-fiber inflammation, and numb the mucosa. Lidocaine gives immediate but brief relief that confirms the bladder as the pain source, while heparin and hyaluronate rebuild protection over courses of weeks.
