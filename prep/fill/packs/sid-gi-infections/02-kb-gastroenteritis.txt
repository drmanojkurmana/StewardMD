# Acute Gastroenteritis
Acute Gastroenteritis, Gastrointestinal.
# Pathogens
Watery non-inflammatory diarrhea is predominantly viral or toxin-mediated; invasive bacterial pathogens are more strongly associated with bloody stool, high fever, and systemic symptoms (see Dysentery).
Very likely: Norovirus; Rotavirus (in children); Enterotoxigenic E. coli.
Likely: Other viral enteropathogens.
# Investigations
None routinely required in mild self-limited cases, Most cases resolve without specific identification of organism.
Stool routine/microscopy (if symptoms persist beyond 7 days or in immunocompromised), Identifies parasitic causes (e.g., Giardia) that require specific therapy.
Antibiotics NOT indicated
# Reason
Watery, non-bloody diarrhea without high fever or systemic toxicity is most consistent with viral or toxin-mediated gastroenteritis. Antibiotics do not shorten illness duration in this setting and may prolong carriage of certain pathogens. Oral rehydration is the priority intervention.
# Pathophysiology
Acute infectious diarrhea results when an ingested pathogen overwhelms or bypasses host defenses (gastric acid, intestinal motility/peristalsis, mucus layer, secretory IgA, and the protective resident microbiota). Mechanistically there are two broad patterns. Noninflammatory disease arises in the proximal small bowel and is mediated by enterotoxins (e.g., cholera toxin, E. coli heat-labile [LT] and heat-stable [ST] toxins) or by viral injury; cholera toxin ADP-ribosylates a GTP-binding protein to persistently activate adenylate cyclase, raising cyclic AMP, increasing chloride secretion and decreasing sodium absorption, producing watery secretory diarrhea without mucosal destruction. Inflammatory disease affects the colon or distal small bowel through mucosal invasion (Shigella, enteroinvasive E. coli, Salmonella) or cytotoxins (S. dysenteriae type 1, Shiga toxin-producing E. coli, C. difficile), destroying epithelium and producing dysentery with fecal leukocytes. Viral pathogens (norovirus, rotavirus) injure the small-intestinal villous epithelium: rotavirus destroys mature absorptive enterocytes and the loss of brush-border enzymes leaves unmetabolized disaccharides, generating combined secretory and osmotic diarrhea, with the nonstructural protein NSP4 acting as an enterotoxin. Crucially, glucose-coupled sodium absorption in the small intestine remains intact even during toxin-driven secretion, which is the physiologic basis for oral rehydration therapy.
# Prognosis
Most acute infectious gastroenteritis is self-limited and resolves within days; viral norovirus/sapovirus illness typically lasts 1-3 days and rotavirus 3-7 days. Death is uncommon in healthy adults but occurs chiefly from dehydration in infants and debilitated elderly; globally acute diarrhea remains a leading cause of childhood death. Oral rehydration has dramatically reduced mortality (e.g., cholera case fatality from >50% untreated to <1%). Recognized post-infectious sequelae include reactive arthritis (after invasive Shigella/Salmonella/Campylobacter/Yersinia), Guillain-Barre syndrome (after Campylobacter), hemolytic-uremic syndrome (after Shiga toxin-producing bacteria), post-infectious IBS (notably after Campylobacter), and transient post-infectious lactase deficiency; recurrent childhood diarrhea in resource-poor settings causes environmental enteropathy with growth and developmental stunting.
# Severity classification
Severity is graded clinically by functional impact and dehydration rather than by a single score. Mild: activity unrestricted. Moderate: activities altered. Severe: incapacitated. Dehydration assessment is the key severity measure on examination - mild = thirst, dry mouth, reduced axillary sweat, decreased urine output, slight weight loss; moderate = orthostatic blood-pressure fall, skin tenting, sunken eyes (or sunken fontanelle in infants); severe = lethargy/obtundation, feeble pulse, hypotension, and shock. Frequency of stools forewarns impending dehydration. By duration, diarrhea is acute (<2 weeks), persistent (2-4 weeks), or chronic (>4 weeks).
