Kidney stone disease is dominated by one chemistry. Calcium oxalate forms 70 to 80 percent of all urinary calculi, and most of these stones start life on Randall plaques, which are calcium phosphate deposits laid down in the interstitium of the renal papilla. When a plaque erodes through the urothelial surface, it offers passing crystals a fixed anchor, and layered oxalate growth builds the familiar dense radio-opaque stone. High urinary calcium, high oxalate, and persistently low urine volume drive this pathway, so recurrent calcium stone formers earn a full 24-hour urine metabolic workup.

Uric acid stones play by different rules and need different tests. They precipitate in persistently acidic urine with pH below 5.5, the classic setting being gout, insulin resistance, and chronic diarrhoeal states with bicarbonate loss. Because they contain no calcium, they are radiolucent and invisible on plain radiography, yet they show up clearly on non-contrast computed tomography. Their invisibility on X-ray combined with visibility on CT is itself a diagnostic clue, and raising urine pH dissolves them.

Struvite stones are infection stones and behave aggressively. They consist of magnesium ammonium phosphate mixed with carbonate apatite and form only when urease-producing bacteria split urea into ammonia, alkalinising the urine. Proteus mirabilis and Klebsiella pneumoniae are the classic culprits. The alkaline ammonia-rich urine precipitates large branched staghorn calculi that fill the pelvis and calyces, trap bacteria inside the stone matrix, and recur unless every fragment is cleared and the infection eradicated.

Cystine stones are the genetic members of the family. They arise from autosomal recessive mutations in SLC3A1 or SLC7A9 that cripple dibasic amino acid transport in the proximal tubule and gut, spilling cystine, ornithine, lysine, and arginine into the urine. Cystine is poorly soluble, so hexagonal crystals precipitate in childhood and build recurrent faintly radio-opaque stones. A positive family history of childhood stones should trigger screening of siblings.

Bedside chemistry confirms cystinuria in seconds. The sodium nitroprusside test, the Brand test, turns urine magenta or purple when cystine exceeds 75 mg per litre. It is a screening reaction, not a measurement, so a positive result leads to quantitative 24-hour urinary cystine and stone analysis. Early detection matters because aggressive hydration and alkalinisation started in childhood prevents obstructive episodes and protects renal function.

Small distal ureteral stones often pass on their own, and medical expulsive therapy raises the odds. Tamsulosin 0.4 mg daily, an alpha-1 adrenergic blocker, relaxes distal ureteral smooth muscle and improves spontaneous passage of stones between 5 and 10 mm lodged in the lower ureter. Therapy runs for a few weeks with analgesia and a strainer for stone capture, and failure to progress, infection, or a solitary kidney mandates prompt ureteroscopic intervention instead.

Prevention targets the metabolic defect the workup finds. Hypocitraturia, defined as urinary citrate below 320 mg per day, removes a major inhibitor of calcium crystallisation and marks recurrent calcium stone formers. Potassium citrate supplementation restores inhibitor levels and aims for a urine pH between 6.5 and 7.0, a range that also discourages uric acid precipitation without pushing calcium phosphate formation. Citrate dosing follows repeat 24-hour urine results.

Thiazides close the loop on calcium handling. Chlorthalidone 25 mg daily or hydrochlorothiazide 25 to 50 mg daily reduces urinary calcium by enhancing calcium reabsorption in the proximal and distal tubule, directly correcting absorptive and renal hypercalciuria. They pair naturally with salt restriction, since sodium loading washes the effect out, and potassium citrate is added when thiazide-induced hypokalaemia lowers urinary citrate.
