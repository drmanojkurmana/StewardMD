Calcium oxalate dominates urinary stone chemistry, causing 70 to 80 percent of calculi. Crystals nucleate on Randall plaques, which are calcium phosphate deposits seated in the renal papillary interstitium. When a plaque erodes through the papillary urothelium, oxalate layers from concentrated urine and a stone is formed. High oxalate foods such as spinach and nuts, excess vitamin C intake, and low daily urine volume all push formation, so dilution through day and night is the cheapest prevention.

Uric acid stones are grown in persistently acid urine below pH 5.5. Gout, obesity, and insulin resistance cluster with this chemistry. The stones are radiolucent, meaning a plain KUB film misses them, while non-contrast CT detects every stone whatever its chemistry. Potassium citrate titrated to hold urine pH at 6.5 to 7.0 dissolves small uric acid stones, because alkaline urine keeps urate dissolved.

Struvite chemistry mixes magnesium ammonium phosphate with carbonate apatite and follows chronic infection by urease splitting organisms. Proteus mirabilis is the classic pathogen and Klebsiella pneumoniae behaves the same way. Released ammonia drives pH above 7.2 and rapid precipitation branches into staghorn casts filling pelvis and calyces. Complete surgical clearance plus infection cure is required, because left fragments regrow quickly.

Cystine stones are inherited through autosomal recessive mutations in SLC3A1 on chromosome 2 or SLC7A9 on chromosome 19 that waste dibasic amino acids remembered as COLA: cystine, ornithine, lysine, arginine. Poorly soluble cystine precipitates from childhood with recurrent bilateral stones, so siblings are screened. The Brand test adds sodium nitroprusside reagent to urine and a magenta purple colour above 75 mg per L of cystine is pathognomonic. Positive screens are confirmed by 24 hour urine quantification, and fluids with potassium citrate form the base of prevention.

Distal ureteral stones of 5 to 10 mm often pass spontaneously, and medical expulsive therapy with tamsulosin 0.4 mg daily relaxes the distal ureter and lifts passage rates. Analgesia with a strainer proves passage, while fever, unremitting pain, or a blocked solitary kidney forces intervention.

Urine citrate shortfall below 320 mg per day removes a brake on calcium crystallization and is seen in distal renal tubular acidosis, chronic diarrhoea, and salty diets. Potassium citrate replacement dosed to urine pH 6.5 to 7.0 restores protection, though over alkalinization favours calcium phosphate stones. Hypercalciuria is cut by thiazide diuretics, chlorthalidone 25 mg or hydrochlorothiazide 25 to 50 mg daily, which enhance tubular calcium reabsorption. Salt restriction with normal dietary calcium completes the prescription, and potassium, glucose, and uric acid are watched on follow up.
