# Heat stroke
Heat stroke, Environmental / Emergency, Emergency Medicine.
Aliases: exertional heat stroke; classic heat stroke; severe hyperthermia.
# Pathophysiology
Heat stroke occurs when heat gain overwhelms the body's ability to dissipate heat, causing core temperature to rise to a level that produces direct thermal injury to cells and tissues. Excessive environmental heat or intense muscular activity generates a heat load that exceeds losses through radiation, convection, and evaporation, especially in hot, humid conditions or when thermoregulation is impaired. The resulting hyperthermia denatures proteins and damages cell membranes, and triggers a systemic inflammatory response and endothelial injury resembling sepsis. This leads to loss of thermoregulatory control, capillary leak, coagulation activation, and progressive multi-organ dysfunction, with the brain being especially vulnerable, producing the characteristic encephalopathy.
# Prognosis
Outcome depends chiefly on the peak temperature and how quickly cooling is achieved: rapid recognition and cooling greatly improve survival, whereas prolonged severe hyperthermia carries high mortality and a risk of permanent neurological deficit. Multi-organ failure and delayed treatment worsen prognosis, and some survivors have lasting impairment of heat tolerance.
# Clinical pearls
Heat stroke is a life-threatening emergency defined by a markedly raised core temperature, typically above 40 degrees Celsius, together with central nervous system dysfunction
It is distinguished from heat exhaustion by the presence of neurological impairment such as confusion, agitation, seizures, or coma
Two forms are recognised: classic (non-exertional) heat stroke affecting vulnerable people during heat waves, and exertional heat stroke affecting young, active individuals such as athletes and military recruits
Sweating may be present in exertional heat stroke, so absence of dry skin does not exclude the diagnosis
Rapid cooling is the priority and takes precedence over detailed investigation; cold-water immersion is the most effective method, particularly for exertional cases
Multi-organ complications include rhabdomyolysis, acute kidney injury, liver injury, disseminated intravascular coagulation, and cardiovascular instability
The degree and duration of hyperthermia strongly influence outcome, so cooling should begin as early as possible, ideally before or during transport
# Additional differentials
Heat exhaustion without central nervous system dysfunction
Sepsis or severe systemic infection with fever
Neuroleptic malignant syndrome or serotonin syndrome
Malignant hyperthermia
Thyroid storm
Central nervous system infection such as meningitis or encephalitis
Drug toxicity from stimulants or anticholinergics
# Additional investigations
Core temperature measurement, ideally rectal, to confirm severe hyperthermia
Electrolytes, renal and liver function tests to assess organ injury
Creatine kinase and urinalysis for rhabdomyolysis and myoglobinuria
Coagulation studies and platelets to detect disseminated intravascular coagulation
Blood glucose and arterial blood gas for metabolic disturbance
Electrocardiogram and cardiac monitoring, with a septic and toxicological screen when the cause is unclear
# Red flags
Core temperature above 40 degrees with altered consciousness, seizures, or coma
Signs of shock, arrhythmia, or cardiovascular collapse
Evidence of rhabdomyolysis with acute kidney injury
Coagulopathy or disseminated intravascular coagulation
# Pitfalls
Waiting for laboratory results or transport before starting aggressive cooling
Excluding heat stroke because the patient is sweating, which can occur in exertional cases
Mistaking heat stroke for simple heat exhaustion and missing the neurological dysfunction
Using antipyretics, which are ineffective because the hypothalamic set point is not raised
Failing to monitor for and treat rhabdomyolysis, coagulopathy, and organ failure after cooling
# Management
Begin immediate rapid cooling as the top priority, using cold-water immersion for exertional cases or evaporative cooling, ahead of detailed investigation
Monitor a continuous core (rectal) temperature and stop active cooling at target to avoid overshoot hypothermia
Support airway, breathing, and circulation with intravenous fluids for hypovolaemia and haemodynamic instability
Manage multi-organ complications including rhabdomyolysis with fluid resuscitation, acute kidney injury with renal replacement where needed, disseminated intravascular coagulation, hepatic injury, and seizures
