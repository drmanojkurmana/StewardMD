# Fournier's gangrene
Fournier's gangrene, Infectious Diseases, Infectious Diseases.
Aliases: Fournier gangrene; necrotising fasciitis of the perineum; perineal necrotising soft-tissue infection; scrotal gangrene.
# Pathophysiology
Fournier's gangrene is a necrotising infection of the fascial planes of the genitalia, perineum, and perianal area. Mixed aerobic and anaerobic organisms act synergistically, generating enzymes and toxins that thrombose subcutaneous vessels and cause progressive ischaemic necrosis of skin, subcutaneous tissue, and fascia. Bacterial gas production leads to crepitus, and infection tracks rapidly along fascial planes, often outpacing the overlying skin changes, culminating in systemic sepsis and multi-organ failure.
# Prognosis
Mortality is significant and rises steeply with delayed surgery, extensive disease, advanced age, and multi-organ dysfunction; timely aggressive debridement and organ support improve survival, though survivors often need multiple operations and reconstructive procedures.
# Clinical pearls
A fulminant necrotising fasciitis of the perineal, genital, and perianal region that constitutes a surgical emergency
Usually polymicrobial, mixing aerobic and anaerobic organisms from the gut and skin flora, producing synergistic tissue destruction
Diabetes mellitus, obesity, alcohol excess, immunosuppression, and local trauma or anorectal or urogenital infection are major predisposing factors
Severe pain, systemic toxicity, and rapidly spreading swelling with skin discolouration, crepitus, or foul-smelling discharge should raise immediate suspicion
Diagnosis is clinical and surgical; imaging may show subcutaneous gas but must never delay operative exploration
Treatment principles are immediate wide surgical debridement of all necrotic tissue, broad-spectrum antibiotics covering Gram-positives, Gram-negatives, and anaerobes, and haemodynamic resuscitation
Repeat debridement is commonly required, and diversion of urine or faeces may be needed to protect the wound
Delay in surgery is the strongest driver of mortality, so early recognition and theatre are paramount
Additional differentials: Perianal or ischiorectal abscess without necrosis; Scrotal or epididymo-orchitis; Simple cellulitis of the perineum; Strangulated inguinoscrotal hernia; Cutaneous or Bartholin abscess.
# Infection mimics
Testicular torsion presenting with acute scrotal pain
Severe contact or irritant dermatitis of the groin
Pyoderma gangrenosum of the perineum
Extensive hidradenitis suppurativa flare
# Additional investigations
Blood cultures and cultures of operative tissue and any discharge
Full blood count, renal and liver function, glucose, lactate and coagulation to gauge sepsis severity
CT or plain imaging to define the extent of gas and tissue involvement when it will not delay surgery
Surgical exploration for definitive diagnosis and debridement
Glycaemic assessment and screening for underlying immunosuppression
# Red flags
Rapidly spreading perineal swelling with crepitus or skin necrosis demanding immediate surgical debridement
Pain out of proportion to visible findings suggesting deep fascial necrosis
Systemic signs of septic shock requiring urgent resuscitation and critical care
Subcutaneous gas on examination or imaging indicating gas-forming necrotising infection
# Pitfalls
Mistaking early disease for a simple abscess or cellulitis and delaying theatre
Waiting for imaging before surgical exploration in a deteriorating patient
Underestimating the extent of necrosis and performing inadequate initial debridement
Neglecting planned re-exploration to remove ongoing necrotic tissue
# Management
Undertake emergency wide surgical debridement of all necrotic fascia and tissue, with planned repeat exploration and source control, as the definitive life-saving intervention
Give broad-spectrum empirical antibiotics covering Gram-positive, Gram-negative and anaerobic organisms, with clindamycin added for toxin suppression, then narrow according to tissue and blood cultures
Deliver haemodynamic resuscitation and sepsis care with intravenous fluids, vasopressors and multi-organ support in critical care
Control the predisposing condition — tighten glycaemic control in diabetes and address obesity, alcohol excess or immunosuppression
Consider faecal or urinary diversion with a colostomy or suprapubic catheter to protect the healing perineum
Support recovery with nutritional optimisation and adjunctive hyperbaric oxygen where accessible
Arrange staged reconstructive and plastic surgery after sepsis resolution through a multidisciplinary team
