# Necrotizing Fasciitis
Necrotizing Fasciitis, Skin & Soft Tissue.
# Pathogens
Necrotizing fasciitis is classified as type I (polymicrobial, typically in diabetics or after abdominal surgery) or type II (monomicrobial, classically Group A Streptococcus, can occur in healthy individuals). Empiric coverage must be broad enough to address both patterns until surgical/microbiological findings narrow the picture.
# Very likely
Streptococcus pyogenes (Group A Streptococcus) — monomicrobial type II
Polymicrobial flora including anaerobes and Enterobacteriaceae — type I (more common, often in diabetics/immunocompromised)
Likely: Staphylococcus aureus (including community-associated MRSA); Clostridium species (if gas gangrene/myonecrosis).
Possible: Vibrio vulnificus (if marine/brackish water exposure); Aeromonas species (if freshwater exposure).
Red flags: Immediate empiric antibiotics required.
# Investigations
Urgent surgical exploration (diagnostic AND therapeutic)
The definitive diagnostic test — direct visualization of necrotic fascia confirms the diagnosis and simultaneously allows debridement; imaging should not delay surgery when clinical suspicion is high.
Blood cultures and intraoperative tissue/fluid cultures, Identifies the causative organism(s) to guide de-escalation.
CT or MRI (only if diagnosis uncertain and patient stable enough to tolerate delay)
May show fascial thickening/gas, but should never delay surgery in a patient with high clinical suspicion.
CPK, Markedly elevated levels suggest muscle involvement (myonecrosis), affecting the extent of surgical debridement needed.
LRINEC score (Laboratory Risk Indicator for Necrotizing Fasciitis)
Can support clinical suspicion using CRP, WBC, hemoglobin, sodium, creatinine, and glucose, though it should not override strong clinical suspicion if score is low.
Immediate empiric antibiotics required

# Pathophysiology
NF is infection of the superficial and/or deep fascia investing the muscles of an extremity or trunk, with extensive necrosis of subcutaneous tissue and fascia. Infection spreads rapidly along fascial planes and via venous channels and lymphatics, typically sparing the muscle itself in pure fasciitis. Two main microbiologic patterns: a mixed aerobic-anaerobic (type I) process beginning with a breach of a mucosal barrier (GI/GU tract), and a monomicrobial group A streptococcal (type II) process. In GAS disease, organisms reach deep fascia either by transient bacteremic seeding at a site of minor non-penetrating trauma (no portal) or by spread from cutaneous infection/penetrating trauma. Thrombosis of dermal vessels produces progressive skin necrosis; infarction of cutaneous nerves causes the characteristic late anesthesia. NF caused by Clostridium perfringens or extending into muscle (clostridial/streptococcal myonecrosis) carries very high systemic toxicity with hemolysis. PVL-producing MRSA strains have been reported as a cause.
# Prognosis
Mortality for NF is roughly 11-34% overall and rises substantially when associated with toxic shock syndrome. GAS/clostridial fascial-muscle infection historically carried 20-50% mortality with penicillin alone. Survivors of GAS NF often require repeated surgeries including amputation. Clostridial NF/myonecrosis is far more lethal: spontaneous (nontraumatic) myonecrosis mortality exceeds 65% (often C. septicum with underlying malignancy); trunk infection ~63% vs limb ~12% mortality. Any delay in surgery increases the risk of death; early aggressive surgery markedly improves outcomes.
# Severity classification
Microbiologic/clinical typing: Type I = mixed aerobic-anaerobic, begins at a mucosal-barrier breach (GI/GU), common in diabetes, peripheral vascular disease, malignancy, surgery, or abdominal penetrating injury; Fournier gangrene is the perineal/scrotal form. Type II = monomicrobial group A streptococcal (GAS implicated in ~60% of NF cases), can occur in otherwise healthy hosts, two presentations (with vs without a defined portal of entry). PVL-producing MRSA is a recognized monomicrobial cause. NF qualifies as soft-tissue necrosis under the CDC streptococcal toxic shock syndrome case definition (hypotension plus >=2 of: renal impairment, coagulopathy, hepatic impairment, ARDS, generalized erythematous desquamating rash, soft-tissue necrosis/NF/myositis/gangrene). (LRINEC score not described in this source.)
