# ACE-inhibitor / hereditary angioedema
ACE-inhibitor / hereditary angioedema, Allergy / Emergency.
# Red flags
Airway swelling — secure airway; not always histamine-mediated
# Investigations
Airway assessment
C1-esterase inhibitor / C4 if recurrent
# Pathophysiology
ACE inhibitors block angiotensin-converting enzyme, which normally also degrades the vasodilator peptide bradykinin; inhibition leads to delayed bradykinin breakdown and accumulation, increasing vascular permeability in the deep dermis and subcutaneous/submucosal tissues and producing angioedema. This is mechanistically the same bradykinin pathway implicated in hereditary angioedema, where deficient or dysfunctional C1 inhibitor (C1INH) fails to restrain activated factor XII (Hageman factor) and kallikrein, generating excess bradykinin. Because the mechanism is bradykinin- rather than histamine-/IgE-mediated, there is no associated urticaria or pruritus, and the process is fundamentally distinct from allergic mast-cell-driven angioedema. The same bradykinin-accumulation mechanism explains the increased angioedema risk seen with combined ACE/neprilysin inhibition, since neprilysin also degrades bradykinin.
# Prognosis
Bradykinin-mediated angioedema typically takes hours to days to resolve and persists >24 h, in contrast to the shorter-lived allergic form. The leading hazard is laryngeal/upper-airway obstruction, which can be life-threatening. No residual scarring occurs unless there is an underlying vasculitis.
No formal angioedema severity-grading scheme is given for ACE-inhibitor angioedema in this source.
# Clinical pearls
ACE-inhibitor angioedema occurs in roughly 0.2-0.7% of exposed patients and is driven by impaired (delayed) degradation of bradykinin rather than by an IgE/mast-cell allergic mechanism.
Recognized risk factors for ACE inhibitor-related angioedema include Black race, prior organ transplant, female sex, smoking, and increasing age.
Bradykinin-mediated angioedema (whether from an ACE inhibitor or from C1-inhibitor deficiency) is characterized by the conspicuous ABSENCE of accompanying urticaria or pruritus.
Compared with histamine-/mast-cell-driven urticaria-angioedema, bradykinin-mediated swelling tends to be more painful than pruritic, with minimal surrounding erythema, and is slower to resolve, persisting >24 h.
The most common sites of angioedema are periorbital and perioral; lesions are neither symmetric nor dependent in distribution.
Gastrointestinal-tract involvement is a frequent feature of bradykinin-mediated angioedema and may present as abdominal colic with or without nausea and vomiting; this can mimic an acute abdomen and lead to unnecessary surgery.
ACE inhibitors are a recognized (if uncommon) cause of intestinal angioedema, which can present with diarrhea/abdominal symptoms (Ch. on diarrhea, GI section).
No residual scarring follows urticaria or angioedema unless an underlying vasculitic process is present.
# Additional differentials
Allergic (histaminergic, IgE/mast-cell-mediated) angioedema, typically with concurrent urticaria/pruritus and a more rapid onset and resolution.
Hereditary angioedema (HAE) due to C1-inhibitor deficiency (type 1) or dysfunctional C1INH (type 2), suggested by childhood/adolescent onset, family history, recurrent attacks, and failure to respond to H1 antihistamines.
Type 3 (normal-C1INH) HAE, sometimes associated with a mutant factor XII and normal complement protein levels.
Acquired C1-inhibitor deficiency from excessive C1INH consumption or an anti-C1INH autoantibody in the setting of lymphoproliferative or autoimmune disease, lacking a family history.
Idiopathic angioedema without an identifiable trigger.
Urticarial vasculitis when lesions are painful rather than pruritic, last >36 h, or scar.
Angioedema from other bradykinin-affecting or culprit drugs listed alongside ACE inhibitors: NSAIDs/aspirin, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) inhibitors, radiocontrast media, opioids, antimicrobials, and angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitor (ARNI/sacubitril-valsartan).
# Non infectious mimics
Anaphylaxis / allergic angioedema with airway involvement (histamine-mediated) — important to distinguish because the bradykinin form responds poorly to anaphylaxis therapy.
Hereditary or acquired C1-inhibitor-deficiency angioedema presenting with laryngeal edema or recurrent abdominal colic.
An acute surgical abdomen, mimicked by gastrointestinal angioedema causing abdominal colic, nausea, and vomiting.
# Additional investigations
Diagnosis of bradykinin-mediated angioedema is largely clinical; the lack of pruritus/urticaria and failure to respond to H1 antihistamines point away from histaminergic causes.
For isolated angioedema without urticaria: basic chemistries with liver function tests and complement levels.
C1INH antigen level (deficient in type 1 HAE) and a catalytic C1INH functional assay (abnormal in type 2 HAE) to evaluate for C1-inhibitor deficiency.
C4 and C2 levels: chronically depleted in C1INH-deficiency states and fall further during attacks; in acquired forms, C1q and C1 function are also reduced.
Consider evaluation for paraproteinemia / underlying lymphoproliferative or autoimmune disease when acquired C1INH deficiency is suspected.
# Red flags
Upper-respiratory-tract / laryngeal angioedema can cause transient airway obstruction and is potentially life-threatening.
Tongue, oropharyngeal, or laryngeal swelling indicating threatened airway.
Progressive or persistent swelling lasting >24 h, characteristic of bradykinin-mediated angioedema and warning against assuming a self-limited allergic course.
# Pitfalls
Because the swelling is bradykinin- and not histamine-mediated, it responds poorly to the standard allergic-angioedema/anaphylaxis measures (adrenaline, antihistamines, glucocorticoids); relying on these can give false reassurance.
Onset is not necessarily immediate after starting the drug — ACE-inhibitor angioedema reflects DELAYED bradykinin degradation and can appear well after therapy has begun, so a long-standing ACE inhibitor should not be dismissed as the cause.
Absence of urticaria/pruritus can lead clinicians to overlook angioedema or misattribute it; isolated angioedema without hives is a hallmark of the bradykinin mechanism, not evidence against it.
Gastrointestinal angioedema mimicking an acute abdomen may prompt unnecessary surgical intervention.
Switching to or overlapping with an angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitor (ARNI) is hazardous: neprilysin inhibition further blocks bradykinin breakdown, ARNI is contraindicated in anyone with prior angioedema, and a washout gap is required when transitioning from an ACE inhibitor.
# ACE-inhibitor / hereditary angioedema: treatment
Non-pitting swelling of lips/tongue/face/airway WITHOUT urticaria; ACE-inhibitor or hereditary (bradykinin-mediated). Assess airway, Admit/observe; ICU if airway threatened.
First-line — stop ACEi, protect airway, empirical anaphylaxis therapy if any doubt
# Steps
Assess and protect the AIRWAY early — low threshold for senior anaesthetic/ENT involvement and early intubation.
Stop the ACE inhibitor permanently.
Bradykinin-mediated angioedema responds poorly to adrenaline/steroids/antihistamines, but give them if any doubt about anaphylaxis.
Hereditary angioedema: specific therapy (C1-esterase-inhibitor concentrate, icatibant).
Observe for delayed/progressive swelling; avoid ACEi/ARB in future.
Bradykinin-targeted / hereditary angioedema therapy (specialist)
Stop ACEi; protect airway
