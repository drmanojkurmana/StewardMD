Exam grounding notes for wound healing biology and abnormal scars, written for grounding, not for display. Paraphrased from Plastic Surgery 5E Volume 1 chapters on wound healing and scar management.

Normal healing passes through four overlapping phases. Haemostasis forms the platelet and fibrin clot within minutes. Inflammation follows with neutrophil influx. Proliferation builds granulation tissue from about day 3 to day 21. Remodelling and maturation then continue for many months.

Platelets trapped in the clot release platelet-derived growth factor, called PDGF, and transforming growth factor beta, called TGF-beta. These mediators recruit inflammatory cells first and stimulate fibroblasts later. A dried or disrupted clot loses this signalling function.

Macrophages arrive in the wound at 48 to 72 hours. They digest debris and bacteria, clear spent neutrophils, and release the growth signals that move the wound from inflammation into proliferation. The macrophage is the key regulatory cell of this transition. A wound held in prolonged inflammation by infection or necrotic tissue never receives the handover.

Proliferation lays down Type III collagen, which is thin and weak, inside vascular granulation tissue. During maturation, enzymes remove Type III collagen and stronger Type I collagen takes its place. Normal adult unwounded dermis carries a Type I to Type III collagen ratio of about 4 to 1. A mature pale flat scar has completed this exchange.

Tensile strength returns slowly. At 3 weeks the wound holds only about 20 percent of normal skin strength. By 3 months it plateaus at approximately 70 percent to 80 percent of uninjured strength and never reaches 100 percent. Early loading, haematoma, and infection flatten this curve, so protection and timed suture removal matter.

Hypertrophic scars are raised red scars that stay strictly within the original wound boundaries. They typically regress spontaneously over 12 to 24 months. Pressure therapy and silicone help them settle. Keloids are firm nodular scars that extend beyond the original wound margins into normal adjacent dermis. Keloids rarely regress on their own and show a high recurrence rate after simple surgical excision, so excision alone is avoided.

First-line conservative therapy for established hypertrophic and keloid scars combines silicone gel sheeting with intralesional triamcinolone acetonide injections. The steroid is injected as triamcinolone acetonide suspension. Lesions that fail several months of this combination move on to pressure garments, laser, radiotherapy, or carefully planned excision with adjuvant therapy.

Necrotic tissue and slough stall healing at the inflammatory stage. Sharp debridement back to bleeding viable edges, relief of pressure, moist dressings, and protection from loading let the clean granulating bed proceed into proliferation.
By 6 weeks strength is rising but still fragile, well short of the 3 month plateau.
