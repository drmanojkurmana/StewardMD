# Acute Pyelonephritis
Acute Pyelonephritis, Genitourinary.
# Pathogens
Pyelonephritis pathogens largely mirror uncomplicated UTI but with greater clinical consequence of undertreatment, since ascending infection from the same uropathogens can progress to bacteremia and sepsis.
Very likely: Escherichia coli.
Likely: Klebsiella pneumoniae; Proteus mirabilis.
Possible: Enterococcus species; Pseudomonas aeruginosa (catheter/structural risk).
# Investigations
Urine culture and sensitivity, Confirms organism and susceptibility, essential for targeted therapy and de-escalation.
Blood cultures, Pyelonephritis carries meaningful bacteremia risk; positive cultures confirm systemic involvement and guide duration.
Renal ultrasound or CT (if no improvement in 48-72 hours), Evaluates for obstruction or abscess if the patient fails to improve on appropriate therapy.
Antibiotics recommended

# Pathophysiology
Pyelonephritis usually develops as an ascending infection: uropathogens move from the urethra to the bladder and then continue up the ureter to the renal parenchyma, which is the route for most renal parenchymal infection. Its microbiology therefore mirrors that of acute cystitis. A minority arises hematogenously (<2% of UTIs overall), typically with relatively virulent organisms such as Staphylococcus aureus and Salmonella; isolation of either from urine should prompt consideration of concomitant bacteremia, and hematogenous seeding can produce focal renal abscesses. Anatomic factors causing urinary obstruction or retention (congenital anomalies, cystocele, pelvic-organ prolapse, neurogenic bladder, benign prostatic hypertrophy) impede the protective flushing action of micturition and predispose to renal involvement. A foreign body such as a stone or catheter provides an inert surface for colonization.
