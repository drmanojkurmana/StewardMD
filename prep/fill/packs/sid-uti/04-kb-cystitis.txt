# Acute Uncomplicated Cystitis
Acute Uncomplicated Cystitis, Genitourinary.
# Pathogens
E. coli accounts for the large majority of uncomplicated community-acquired cystitis. The other organisms listed account for most of the remainder, with S. saprophyticus particularly relevant in young sexually active women.
Very likely: Escherichia coli.
Likely: Klebsiella pneumoniae; Staphylococcus saprophyticus.
Possible: Proteus mirabilis; Enterococcus species.
# Investigations
Urinalysis, Confirms pyuria and supports the diagnosis before starting therapy.
Urine culture (only if treatment failure, recurrence, or atypical features), Not routinely needed in straightforward uncomplicated cystitis, but useful if symptoms persist or recur.
Antibiotics recommended
# Reason
Dysuria and urinary frequency without flank pain, fever, or complicating factors are consistent with acute uncomplicated cystitis. Short-course empiric therapy is appropriate without need for urine culture in straightforward presentations in non-pregnant women.
# Pathophysiology
Most cystitis arises by ascending infection: gut flora (typically E. coli) colonize the vaginal introitus and periurethral area, then ascend the urethra into the bladder. Introduction of bacteria into the bladder does not inevitably cause sustained symptomatic infection; normal voiding and innate bladder defenses often clear organisms (e.g., after intercourse). Outcome reflects the interplay of host, organism, and environmental factors. Anything that increases bacterial entry or retention raises risk. The shorter female urethra is considered the primary anatomic reason cystitis predominates in young women over young men. Uropathogenic E. coli express virulence factors including type 1 pili (fimbriae) that bind mannose on bladder uroepithelium and initiate bladder infection (P fimbriae are more relevant to pyelonephritis/bloodstream invasion); toxins, iron-acquisition systems, biofilm formation, and capsules also contribute. Disruption of the normal vaginal lactobacilli (e.g., by spermicide/nonoxynol-9, or estrogen loss after menopause) permits Gram-negative colonization and bacteriuria. Hematogenous spread accounts for under 2% of UTIs. Intracellular bacterial communities within bladder epithelium are demonstrated in models but their clinical significance in humans is unclear.
# Prognosis
Uncomplicated cystitis generally responds promptly and favorably to appropriately chosen modern antimicrobials. About 20-30% of women who have one UTI develop recurrent episodes, and recurrences may cluster after an initial infection; recurrence likelihood decreases as time since the last infection increases. Withholding effective antimicrobial therapy (e.g., treating with NSAIDs alone, or strict delayed therapy) prolongs discomfort and carries a small increased risk of progression to pyelonephritis.
# Severity classification
Uncomplicated UTI is infection confined to the bladder in a woman or man without a urinary catheter; complicated UTI is infection extending beyond the bladder (prostatitis, pyelonephritis, bacteremia) or occurring with intermittent/indwelling catheterization (CAUTI). Recurrent UTI is not inherently complicated, since individual episodes may be uncomplicated and treated as such. This contrasts with the older approach that automatically classified UTI in men, diabetics, those with anatomic abnormalities, or the immunocompromised as complicated.
# Clinical pearls
Bacteria in the urine without related symptoms is asymptomatic bacteriuria (ASB), not UTI; ASB usually needs no treatment and the diagnosis of cystitis requires symptoms, not merely a positive culture or pyuria.
In an otherwise healthy non-pregnant woman with typical symptoms and no complicating factors, cystitis can be diagnosed and treated on history alone, with no dipstick or culture required.
Patient self-diagnosis of recurrent cystitis is highly accurate, which underlies the success of patient-initiated treatment for recurrent UTI.
Fever, rigors, or unilateral back/flank pain are inconsistent with uncomplicated cystitis and should prompt evaluation for infection beyond the bladder (kidney, prostate, or bloodstream).
A dipstick negative for both nitrite and leukocyte esterase should prompt consideration of non-UTI causes for symptoms, but a small fraction can be falsely negative and a negative dipstick does not fully exclude UTI in a symptomatic woman.
Avoid sending urine cultures in patients lacking urinary or systemic symptoms (diagnostic stewardship) to reduce overdiagnosis of UTI and inappropriate treatment of ASB.
In older patients presenting only with altered mentation and chronic cognitive deficits, distinguishing true UTI from incidental ASB is difficult; clinicians should look for sepsis, seek collateral history, and rule out other time-sensitive causes of delirium before attributing it to UTI.
A reported history of recurrent UTIs in an elderly patient may actually reflect persistent ASB rather than true infection.
# Additional differentials
Cervicitis (Chlamydia trachomatis, Neisseria gonorrhoeae) presenting with dysuria
Urethritis, including gonococcal/chlamydial and herpetic urethritis
Sexually transmitted infection mistaken for UTI, especially with multiple partners or inconsistent condom use; vaginal discharge should prompt STI consideration
# Infection mimics
Vaginitis due to Candida albicans or Trichomonas vaginalis causing dysuria
Sexually transmitted urethritis/cervicitis (gonococcal, chlamydial, Mycoplasma genitalium)
Acute or chronic bacterial prostatitis in men presenting as cystitis-like symptoms
# Non infectious mimics
Interstitial cystitis / bladder pain syndrome
Noninfectious vaginal or vulvar irritation
Chronic pelvic pain syndrome in men (not associated with bacteriuria, not improved by antibiotics)
# Additional investigations
Urine dipstick for nitrite and leukocyte esterase as point-of-care testing, used mainly to help rule out UTI when the clinical diagnosis is unclear
Microscopic urinalysis (white and red cell counts); pyuria is present in nearly all cystitis and hematuria in about 30%
Urine culture when a resistant organism is suspected, in recurrent UTI, after prior treatment failure, or in all male UTI; a colony count threshold of >10^2 bacteria/mL is more sensitive than 10^5/mL for diagnosing cystitis in symptomatic women
STI screening / pelvic examination in women with risk factors for STI or with vaginal discharge
# Red flags
Fever, rigors, or unilateral back/costovertebral or flank pain suggesting pyelonephritis or other extension beyond the bladder
Perineal, pelvic, or prostatic pain in a man, suggesting prostatitis
Recurrent cystitis in a man, warranting evaluation for a prostatic focus and for urinary retention
Vaginal discharge raising concern for STI rather than UTI
Early recurrence within 2 weeks (relapse), suggesting a sequestered focus of ongoing infection
# Pitfalls
Treating ASB as if it were UTI; the degree of pyuria in an asymptomatic patient should not drive a UTI diagnosis, and very high pyuria is common with urinary stasis (e.g., end-stage renal disease, obstructed catheters).
Relying on nitrite: only Enterobacterales convert nitrate to nitrite, so it can be falsely negative with non-Enterobacterales organisms or with frequent voiding from high fluid intake, and can be positive in Enterobacterales ASB.
Treating an STI (especially C. trachomatis) as UTI because symptoms overlap; women at risk for UTI are often also at risk for STI.
Assuming a negative dipstick excludes infection; it is not sensitive enough to rule out bacteriuria in pregnancy, where culture-based ASB screening is required.
The >10 WBC/HPF pyuria cutoff has poor specificity (about 36%) for UTI; a much higher count may correlate better with symptoms, though >10/HPF remains the accepted standard.
Misinterpreting a mixed-flora culture as infection; mixed growth usually indicates contamination except with long-term catheterization, chronic retention, or a urinary-enteric/genital fistula.
# Deescalation
Not typically needed given short empiric courses; if symptoms persist beyond 48-72 hours, obtain urine culture and adjust based on susceptibility.

# Framework
Avoid fluoroquinolones as first-line for uncomplicated cystitis — reserve for complicated infection or treatment failure.
Routine urine culture is not required before starting empiric therapy in a straightforward, non-pregnant, non-complicated presentation.
Use the shortest effective duration (3-5 days) rather than longer courses, which do not improve cure rates but increase resistance pressure.
