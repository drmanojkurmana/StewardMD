# Complicated Urinary Tract Infection
Complicated Urinary Tract Infection, Genitourinary.
# Pathogens
Complicating factors (catheters, structural abnormality, diabetes, male sex) select for a broader and more resistant organism profile than uncomplicated cystitis, including a meaningfully higher chance of ESBL-producing organisms.
Very likely: Escherichia coli (often with resistant phenotypes).
Likely: Klebsiella pneumoniae; Proteus mirabilis; Enterococcus species.
Possible: Pseudomonas aeruginosa (catheter-associated, prior instrumentation).

# Pathophysiology
Most UTIs arise by ascent of bacteria from the urethra to the bladder, with further ascent up the ureter producing renal parenchymal infection; the hematogenous route accounts for under 2% of cases and typically involves virulent organisms such as S. aureus and Salmonella (isolation of either from urine should prompt consideration of concomitant bacteremia). Whether bladder entry leads to symptomatic infection depends on the interplay of host, pathogen, and environmental factors. Conditions causing urinary stasis or obstruction (vesicoureteral reflux, ureteral obstruction from prostatic hypertrophy, neurogenic bladder, urinary-diversion surgery) and foreign bodies that support biofilm all predispose to infection. Uropathogenic E. coli virulence factors include P fimbriae (mediating binding to renal epithelial receptors and important in pyelonephritis and bloodstream invasion) and type 1 pili (mediating mannose binding and initiating bladder infection), plus toxins, iron-acquisition systems, biofilm formation, and capsules. Candiduria is pathogenetically distinct in that the hematogenous route is more common, and in non-instrumented immunocompetent patients its presence implies either genital contamination or possible widespread visceral dissemination.
# Prognosis
Most simple cystitis carries a low risk of serious complication, but tissue-invasive complicated disease (pyelonephritis and beyond) carries greater risk and patients are often systemically ill. Pyelonephritis may be complicated by acute papillary necrosis with obstruction (requiring relief of obstruction to preserve renal function), perinephric or renal cortical abscess (often requiring percutaneous drainage), emphysematous pyelonephritis (a severe form, almost exclusive to poorly controlled diabetes), and xanthogranulomatous pyelonephritis (suppurative renal destruction associated with chronic obstruction, often by staghorn calculi, that can mimic renal malignancy and frequently requires nephrectomy). Bilateral papillary necrosis is rare but may present with a rapid rise in creatinine.
# Severity classification
The key bedside questions are whether the patient is stable for outpatient management, whether a recalcitrant/recurrent source (e.g., obstructing calculi) must be identified, and whether the chosen agent must reach adequate blood and renal-tissue levels in addition to urine. Pyelonephritis itself ranges from mild (low-grade fever +/- back or costovertebral angle pain) to severe (high fever, rigors, nausea, vomiting, flank pain).
# Clinical pearls
Recurrent UTI is not inherently complicated; individual episodes may be uncomplicated and managed as such.
Fever, rigors, and unilateral back or flank pain are incompatible with infection confined to the bladder and should trigger evaluation for infection beyond the bladder (kidney, prostate, or bloodstream).
In men, UTI is uncommon outside the context of catheterization/instrumentation or obstructive uropathy from prostatic hypertrophy; a urine culture should be obtained in every case of male UTI.
Men with febrile UTI, even at first episode, should undergo imaging (CT or ultrasound) if not done previously, specifically to assess for urinary tract abnormalities or obstruction.
Predictors of a surgically correctable disorder in men with febrile UTI include urinary retention symptoms, early UTI recurrence, hematuria at follow-up, and voiding difficulties; men with none of these had normal urinary tracts on workup.
Early recurrence within 2 weeks is regarded as relapse rather than reinfection and may signal a sequestered focus of ongoing infection that needs evaluation.
Foreign bodies such as stones, stents, and catheters provide an inert surface for bacterial colonization and persistent biofilm formation, serving as a nidus for recurrent infection.
Initial evaluation of recurrent cystitis should include imaging to identify renal calculi or another nidus of infection and assessment for urinary retention.
# Additional differentials
Acute bacterial prostatitis (consider in men with UTI plus systemic symptoms and no other clear anatomic source)
Chronic bacterial prostatitis (consider with recurrent UTI in men, especially outside catheterization)
Pyelonephritis with or without urinary obstruction
Chronic pelvic pain syndrome in men (not associated with bacteriuria, not improved by antibiotics)
# Infection mimics
Sexually transmitted infection / cervicitis (Chlamydia trachomatis, Neisseria gonorrhoeae) presenting with dysuria
Vaginitis (Candida albicans, Trichomonas vaginalis)
Herpetic urethritis
Urethritis from STI pathogens (gonococcal, chlamydial, Mycoplasma genitalium); urethral pain, pruritus, or discharge should prompt STI evaluation
# Non infectious mimics
Asymptomatic bacteriuria misclassified as UTI, especially in older patients with isolated altered mentation; degree of pyuria should not drive the distinction
Interstitial cystitis
Noninfectious vaginal or vulvar irritation
Chronic pelvic pain syndrome in men
# Additional investigations
CT or ultrasound imaging in men with febrile UTI to evaluate for anatomic abnormality or obstruction
Blood cultures when complicated UTI, pyelonephritis, or systemic features are present
Urine dipstick (nitrite and leukocyte esterase) and microscopic urinalysis as point-of-care tools mainly useful for ruling out UTI when the clinical diagnosis is unclear
Imaging to identify renal calculi or other nidus, plus assessment for urinary retention, in recurrent UTI
Serum creatinine monitoring (a rapid rise may be the first sign of bilateral papillary necrosis)
# Red flags
Fever, rigors, nausea/vomiting, and flank pain suggesting severe pyelonephritis or infection beyond the bladder
Continued fever and/or bacteremia despite antibacterial therapy, suggesting intraparenchymal renal abscess
Failure to respond clinically within 72 h, prompting evaluation for an uncontrolled source of infection
Gas in renal/perinephric tissues (emphysematous pyelonephritis), almost exclusive to poorly controlled diabetes
Acute papillary necrosis with sloughed papillae obstructing the ureter complicating pyelonephritis
Isolation of S. aureus or Salmonella from urine, raising concern for concomitant bacteremia / hematogenous seeding
Symptoms of urinary retention, early UTI recurrence, hematuria at follow-up, or voiding difficulty in men with febrile UTI (predict surgically correctable disease)
# Pitfalls
Treating asymptomatic bacteriuria outside pregnancy or pre-urologic-procedure settings: it does not reduce future UTI and may even increase risk, possibly by promoting colonization with more virulent/resistant pathogens.
Ending the workup of a febrile catheterized patient at an abnormal urinalysis: bacteriuria and pyuria are essentially inevitable with catheterization, so other infectious and noninfectious causes must be systematically excluded before diagnosing CAUTI.
Premature diagnostic closure in elderly patients with isolated delirium; collateral history is needed and a reported history of recurrent UTIs may actually reflect persistent ASB.
Relying on a negative urine dipstick to rule out bacteriuria in pregnancy; it is not sensitive enough and culture-based ASB screening is required.
Using nitrofurantoin or fosfomycin as if they treat tissue-invasive disease; they do not achieve adequate renal tissue levels and should not be used for pyelonephritis, and nitrofurantoin is inactive against many Proteus, Providencia, Morganella, Pseudomonas, Serratia, and some Klebsiella/Enterobacter (knowledge of intrinsic resistance).
Interpreting a mixed-flora urine culture as infection; it usually represents contamination except in long-term catheterization, chronic retention, or urinary-enteric/genital fistula.
# Deescalation
Narrow to the most targeted oral agent once urine culture and sensitivity are available, and shorten duration if clinical response is rapid and the complicating factor has been addressed.
