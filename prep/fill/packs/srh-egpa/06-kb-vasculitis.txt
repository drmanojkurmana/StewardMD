# Systemic vasculitis
Systemic vasculitis, Rheumatology.
# Red flags
Rapidly progressive renal/pulmonary involvement is an emergency
# Investigations
ANCA, complement
Urinalysis + renal function
Biopsy of affected organ
# Pathophysiology
Vasculitis is inflammation of and damage to blood vessel walls, usually compromising the lumen and causing ischaemia of supplied tissues; any size, type, or location of vessel may be affected, and disease may be primary/single-organ or secondary to another illness. Most syndromes are at least partly immune-mediated through three broad mechanisms: (1) Immune-complex formation/deposition (e.g., IgA vasculitis, lupus vasculitis, serum sickness, hepatitis-B-associated vasculitis, hepatitis-C cryoglobulinaemic vasculitis) - resembling serum sickness, complexes deposit in vessel walls, activate complement (notably C5a, chemotactic for neutrophils), and neutrophils infiltrate and release enzymes that damage the wall. (2) ANCA production (GPA, MPA, EGPA) - when neutrophils/monocytes are primed by TNF-alpha, IL-1, or alternative-complement activation, PR3 and MPO translocate to the cell membrane where ANCA can engage them, triggering degranulation, reactive oxygen species, endothelial injury, and release of IL-1/IL-8. (3) Pathogenic T-lymphocyte responses and granuloma formation (GCA, Takayasu, GPA, EGPA) - cytokine (IFN-gamma) activated endothelium expresses HLA class II and interacts with CD4+ T cells, with IL-1/TNF-alpha inducing adhesion molecules (ELAM-1, VCAM-1). The 1992/2012 Chapel Hill Consensus nomenclature underpins the classification of these syndromes by predominant vessel size.
# Prognosis
Untreated GPA was historically fatal within months; with cyclophosphamide-based therapy >90% improve, ~75% achieve complete remission, and 5-year survival exceeds 80%, but 50-70% of remissions later relapse and many accrue irreversible damage (renal insufficiency, hearing loss, subglottic stenosis, saddle-nose deformity, chronic sinus dysfunction). Treated MPA has a ~74% 5-year survival, with deaths from alveolar haemorrhage or GI/cardiac/renal disease, and relapse in at least ~34%. Untreated EGPA has a ~25% 5-year survival; treated, prognosis is favourable (~72% at 78 months), but myocardial involvement causes ~39% of deaths. Untreated PAN has a poor 5-year survival of 10-20% (deaths from GI and cardiovascular causes); prednisone plus cyclophosphamide markedly improves survival, with relapse in ~10-20%. In GCA acute death is uncommon, but patients face late mortality from aortic aneurysm rupture/dissection (18x more likely to develop thoracic aortic aneurysm than the general population). Takayasu outcomes vary widely (overall survival >=94% in some North American series; 5-year mortality 0-35% elsewhere), and the disease is usually chronic and relapsing. IgA vasculitis has an excellent prognosis with rare mortality and only 1-5% of children progressing to end-stage renal disease.
# Severity classification
Vasculitides are classified by predominant vessel size per the Chapel Hill Consensus nomenclature: LARGE-VESSEL (giant cell/temporal arteritis, Takayasu arteritis); MEDIUM-VESSEL (polyarteritis nodosa, Kawasaki disease); SMALL-VESSEL, which splits into ANCA-associated (GPA, MPA, EGPA) and immune-complex (IgA vasculitis/Henoch-Schonlein, cryoglobulinaemic vasculitis). The chapter also separates PRIMARY syndromes from SECONDARY vasculitis (associated with probable etiology e.g. drug-induced, hepatitis-B/C-related, cancer-associated; or with systemic disease e.g. lupus, rheumatoid, sarcoid vasculitis), and single-organ vasculitis (cutaneous leukocytoclastic angiitis, cutaneous arteritis, primary CNS vasculitis, isolated aortitis). For management stratification, ANCA-associated vasculitis is divided into SEVERE (life- or organ-threatening; e.g., alveolar haemorrhage, and active glomerulonephritis is severe even with normal renal function) versus NONSEVERE/limited disease - severity depends on both extent and the specific organ involved (e.g., nodular pulmonary disease without respiratory compromise is nonsevere, whereas alveolar haemorrhage is severe).
# Clinical pearls
Suspect vasculitis in any patient with an unexplained multisystem illness; specific clues include palpable purpura, pulmonary infiltrates with microscopic hematuria, chronic sinusitis, mononeuritis multiplex, unexplained ischemic events, and glomerulonephritis with multisystem disease.
The first step in suspected vasculitis is to exclude conditions that mimic it - especially infections - particularly before starting empirical immunosuppression in a deteriorating patient.
Definitive diagnosis usually rests on biopsy of clinically involved tissue; 'blind' biopsy of organs with no clinical evidence of involvement has very low yield and should be avoided. Arteriography is used when PAN, Takayasu, or primary CNS vasculitis is suspected.
ANCA-associated vasculitides (GPA, MPA, EGPA) share small-vessel involvement and ANCA positivity but have distinct clinical phenotypes and should be regarded as separate entities.
cANCA targets proteinase-3 (PR3) - present in >90% of active GPA; pANCA targets myeloperoxidase (MPO) - only anti-MPO antibodies are convincingly linked to vasculitis (MPA, EGPA, GPA, isolated necrotizing crescentic GN).
A pANCA pattern NOT due to anti-MPO can occur in nonvasculitic conditions: rheumatic/nonrheumatic autoimmune disease, inflammatory bowel disease, certain drugs, and infections including endocarditis and bacterial airway infection in cystic fibrosis.
ANCA titres do not reliably track disease activity - GPA can be active despite negative ANCA, titres correlate poorly with activity, and patients in remission may keep high ANCA for years.
Predictive value of a positive ANCA in GPA is ~90% with active glomerulonephritis but falls to 30-60% in sinus/lung disease without renal involvement; tissue biopsy is then needed to exclude infection or malignancy that may give a false-positive ANCA.
In GPA the renal lesion is a pauci-immune (no immune-complex deposition) focal segmental glomerulitis that can evolve into rapidly progressive crescentic glomerulonephritis.
Saddle-nose deformity (from nasal septal perforation) and subglottic stenosis (~16% of GPA, may cause severe airway obstruction) are characteristic upper-airway sequelae of GPA.
Mononeuritis multiplex is a recurring vasculitic clue - common in EGPA (up to ~72%) and PAN (~51%), and also seen in GPA and MPA.
EGPA (Churg-Strauss) requires asthma, peripheral blood eosinophilia (>1000 cells/uL in >80%), and clinical features of vasculitis; eosinophilic tissue infiltration and extravascular granulomas are characteristic.
Cardiac involvement (myocarditis, pericarditis, coronary vasculitis) is the leading cause of death in EGPA, accounting for ~39% of mortality; echocardiography is advised in all newly diagnosed patients.
PAN does NOT involve pulmonary arteries and does NOT cause glomerulonephritis, granuloma, or significant eosinophilia; venule involvement points away from PAN and toward microscopic polyangiitis.
PAN classically causes microaneurysms of small- and medium-sized renal, hepatic, and visceral arteries; catheter-directed dye arteriography (MR/CT lack resolution) can be diagnostic when accessible tissue is unavailable.
Giant cell arteritis occurs almost exclusively in those over 50; jaw/tongue claudication, scalp tenderness, and headache precede the dreaded complication of ischemic optic neuropathy with sudden blindness - therapy should not be delayed for diagnostic testing.
GCA and polymyalgia rheumatica are considered spectrums of one disease - PMR occurs in 40-50% of GCA, and 10-20% of isolated PMR later develop GCA.
Takayasu arteritis should be strongly suspected in a young woman with diminished/absent peripheral pulses, an inter-arm blood pressure discrepancy, or arterial bruits.
Hemoptysis may be the first sign of alveolar haemorrhage in MPA (~12%); upper-airway disease and pulmonary nodules are atypical for MPA and instead suggest GPA.
GPA carries an increased risk of venous thrombotic events (DVT/PE); maintain a heightened awareness even though routine anticoagulation is not recommended.
# Additional differentials
Granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA) - upper + lower respiratory tract granulomatous vasculitis plus pauci-immune glomerulonephritis; PR3/cANCA in >90% of active disease
Microscopic polyangiitis (MPA) - pauci-immune small-vessel necrotizing vasculitis with frequent glomerulonephritis and pulmonary capillaritis but no granulomas; MPO/pANCA in ~75%
Eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis / Churg-Strauss (EGPA) - asthma, eosinophilia, extravascular granuloma, vasculitis; ANCA (anti-MPO) in ~48%
Polyarteritis nodosa (PAN) - necrotizing vasculitis of small/medium muscular arteries with microaneurysms, sparing lungs and glomeruli; associated with hepatitis B (and C); consider DADA2 in early-onset disease
Giant cell (temporal) arteritis with polymyalgia rheumatica - medium/large-vessel arteritis in patients over 50
Takayasu arteritis - large-vessel arteritis of the aorta and its branches in young women
IgA vasculitis (Henoch-Schonlein) - small-vessel immune-complex vasculitis with palpable purpura, arthralgia, GI symptoms, glomerulonephritis; IgA/C3 on biopsy
Cryoglobulinaemic vasculitis - strongly associated with hepatitis C virus infection
Anti-glomerular basement membrane disease (Goodpasture's syndrome) - other cause of pulmonary-renal syndrome to distinguish from ANCA-associated vasculitis
Lymphomatoid granulomatosis - EBV-positive angiocentric B-cell proliferation with lung/skin/CNS/kidney involvement that mimics GPA but is not a true vasculitis
# Infection mimics
Bacterial (infective) endocarditis - can produce a false-positive pANCA and a vasculitis-like multisystem picture
Disseminated gonococcal infection
Pulmonary histoplasmosis
Coccidioidomycosis
Syphilis
Lyme disease
Rocky Mountain spotted fever
Whipple's disease
Bacterial airway infection in cystic fibrosis - associated with a non-anti-MPO pANCA pattern
Mucocutaneous leishmaniasis and rhinoscleroma - midline destructive infections that mimic GPA upper-airway disease
# Non infectious mimics
Atheroembolic (cholesterol embolisation) disease - listed as a mimic of vasculitis
Antiphospholipid syndrome
Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura
Atrial myxoma
Lymphoma / carcinomatosis
Extranodal NK/T-cell lymphoma (nasal type) - EBV-associated cause of midline destructive disease mimicking GPA; never irradiate true GPA airway lesions
Drug toxicity - cocaine (cocaine-induced midline destructive lesions can show anti-human-neutrophil-elastase ANCA), levamisole (cocaine adulterant; causes cutaneous infarction, granulocytopenia, vasculitis-mimicking serology), amphetamines, ergot alkaloids, methysergide, arsenic
Sarcoidosis
Anti-GBM disease (Goodpasture's)
Amyloidosis
Migraine
Fibromuscular dysplasia
Heritable disorders of connective tissue
Segmental arterial mediolysis (SAM)
Reversible cerebral vasoconstriction syndrome
IgG4-related disease - cause of aortitis/periaortitis distinguished histologically from Takayasu (IgG4+ plasma cells, storiform fibrosis, obliterative phlebitis)
# Additional investigations
ANCA with antigen specificity - anti-proteinase-3 (cANCA) and anti-myeloperoxidase (pANCA) testing, interpreting in light of pretest probability
Tissue biopsy of a clinically involved organ for definitive diagnosis; in GPA, lung biopsy has the highest yield (necrotizing granulomatous vasculitis), upper-airway biopsy often shows only inflammation, renal biopsy shows pauci-immune GN
Skin biopsy with immunofluorescence in IgA vasculitis (leukocytoclastic vasculitis with IgA and C3 deposition)
