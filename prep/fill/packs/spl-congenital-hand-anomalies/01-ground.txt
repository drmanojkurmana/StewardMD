Congenital hand differences range from fused web spaces to missing radial columns, and the Oberg-Manske-Tonkin system brings order. OMT sorts anomalies into malformations from abnormal formation, deformations from outside forces on normal tissue, and dysplasias from faulty cellular organization.

Syndactyly is the commonest anomaly, a failure of the fingers to separate in utero. Simple syndactyly joins skin only, while complex syndactyly fuses bone as well. Complete webbing runs to the fingertip, whereas incomplete webbing stops short of the tip.

Release is timed to growth and function, usually around 12 to 18 months when the hand is large enough for grafting. Surgeons open the web with zigzag volar and dorsal incisions and resurface the raw sides with full-thickness skin grafts, because straight scars would contract and drag the web forward as web creep.

One iron rule protects the circulation. Adjacent web spaces, such as both borders of the middle finger, must never be released in a single operation, since operating both sides together strips the digit of its blood supply and risks ischemic loss.

Radial polydactyly duplicates the thumb, and the Wassel system describes seven patterns by the level of the split. Wassel Type IV, a bifid proximal phalanx with a duplicated distal segment, is the commonest duplicate thumb surgeons meet.

Small balanced duplications can merge into one good thumb. The Bilhaut-Cloquet procedure takes symmetrical central wedges from both duplicate thumbs and joins the outer halves, creating a single normal-width thumb in Wassel I and II deformities where both parts are hypoplastic.

A short radially deviated forearm points to radial longitudinal deficiency, the radial club hand. Associations include VACTERL, Holt-Oram syndrome with its cardiac septal defects, and Fanconi anemia with marrow failure, so every child needs baseline blood counts and cardiac evaluation at presentation.

Constriction ring syndrome comes from a different mechanism: amniotic bands wrap the developing limbs.
