# Variceal bleeding
Variceal bleeding, Hepatology / GI.
# Red flags
Major haemorrhage — resuscitate; antibiotics reduce mortality in cirrhotic GI bleed
# Investigations
Urgent upper GI endoscopy
Crossmatch, coagulation
Vasoactive (terlipressin) + antibiotic prophylaxis
# Pathophysiology
Oesophageal varices are a consequence of portal hypertension, defined as an HVPG >5 mmHg. Portal hypertension arises from two simultaneous haemodynamic processes: (1) increased intrahepatic resistance to portal blood flow (from cirrhosis, regenerative nodules and microthrombi) and (2) increased splanchnic blood flow secondary to splanchnic vasodilation. Cirrhosis is associated with decreased intrahepatic endothelial nitric oxide synthase and nitric oxide, causing intrahepatic vasoconstriction, while systemically nitric oxide, VEGF and TNF rise, driving splanchnic vasodilation. In advanced stages, neurohumoral and vasoconstrictor activation causes sodium/water retention, increased blood volume and a hyperdynamic circulation that further raises portal pressure. Intrahepatic causes (mainly cirrhosis) account for >95% of portal hypertension; pre-hepatic (portal/splenic vein thrombosis) and post-hepatic (Budd-Chiari, right-heart congestion) causes also occur. Elevated portal pressure decompresses into portosystemic collaterals, producing gastro-oesophageal varices that can rupture and bleed.
# Prognosis
Variceal haemorrhage carries a 20-30% mortality rate with each bleeding episode and patients with variceal haemorrhage have poorer outcomes than those with other UGIB sources. Control of acute bleeding is achievable in the vast majority of cases, but bleeding recurs in the majority of patients unless definitive endoscopic therapy is instituted; oesophageal varices reflect clinically significant portal hypertension (HVPG ≥10 mmHg), and once cirrhosis decompensates the median survival is <2 years. Prophylactic antibiotics, vasoactive drugs and a restrictive transfusion strategy each independently reduce mortality.
# Severity classification
Bleeding/decompensation risk is stratified by Child-Pugh class and MELD score, by HVPG/wedged-hepatic-vein pressure (CSPH ≥10 mmHg; variceal bleeding risk at >12 mmHg), by varix size and location, and by endoscopic high-risk stigmata (red wale signs, cherry-red spots, etc.). For triage of UGIB generally, the Glasgow-Blatchford score (0-23) identifies very-low-risk patients (score 0-1) suitable for outpatient management. Advanced-disease thresholds favouring early TIPS: Child-Pugh B 8-9 with active bleeding at endoscopy, or Child-Pugh C 10-13.
# Clinical pearls
Portal hypertension is defined as a hepatic venous pressure gradient (HVPG) >5 mmHg; compensated cirrhosis typically has an HVPG of 5-10 mmHg, while clinically significant portal hypertension (CSPH), defined as HVPG ≥10 mmHg, carries substantial risk of decompensation (variceal bleeding, ascites, encephalopathy). Patients with a gradient >12 mmHg are at risk for variceal haemorrhage.
Each episode of variceal haemorrhage carries a 20-30% mortality rate, making it an immediate life-threatening complication of portal hypertension.
In cirrhotic patients presenting with upper GI bleeding, prophylactic antibiotics (typically ceftriaxone) started before endoscopy reduce bacterial infections, recurrent bleeding AND mortality — this is a mortality-altering intervention, not merely supportive.
A vasoactive/vasoconstricting agent (somatostatin, octreotide, terlipressin, vapreotide) improves initial bleeding control, reduces transfusion needs and reduces all-cause mortality; combined endoscopic plus pharmacologic therapy is superior to either alone.
A restrictive transfusion strategy (transfuse when haemoglobin <7 g/dL, target 7-9 g/dL) reduced early rebleeding and mortality compared with a liberal threshold in randomised trial; haemoglobin may be normal at initial presentation because plasma and red-cell volumes fall proportionately ("people bleed whole blood") and may take up to 72 h to fall.
Roughly one-third of patients with histologically confirmed cirrhosis have varices; about 5-15% of cirrhotics develop varices per year, and roughly one-third of patients with varices will bleed.
IV erythromycin given shortly before endoscopy is suggested as a promotility agent to improve endoscopic visualisation in UGI bleeding, reducing the need for repeat endoscopy.
Routine correction of an elevated prothrombin time with fresh frozen plasma is NOT recommended in acute variceal bleeding unless there is evidence of coagulopathy such as bleeding at other sites (e.g. IV-line sites).
Bleeding gastric varices, especially when entirely within the stomach or extending into the proximal stomach, are often not amenable to band ligation; tissue-adhesive injection (e.g. cyanoacrylate), TIPS, or balloon-occluded retrograde transvenous obliteration (BRTO) are alternative principles.
# Additional differentials
Peptic ulcer disease — the most common cause of upper GI bleeding (~50% of UGIB hospitalisations)
Mallory-Weiss tear — ~2-10% of UGIB, classically preceded by vomiting/retching/coughing, usually on the gastric side of the gastro-oesophageal junction; stops spontaneously in ~80-90%
Bleeding gastric varices, intestinal varices, and portal hypertensive gastropathy/enterocolopathy — other portal-hypertension-related sources distinct from oesophageal varices
Erosive disease — erosive oesophagitis, gastritis, duodenitis (up to ~30% of UGIB), commonly NSAID-related
Stress-related gastric mucosal injury in the critically ill
Less common: neoplasm, vascular ectasias (including hereditary haemorrhagic telangiectasia, gastric antral vascular ectasia), Dieulafoy's lesion, aortoenteric fistula, haemobilia/haemosuccus pancreaticus
# Non infectious mimics
Portal hypertensive gastropathy — snakeskin-like mosaic mucosal pattern on endoscopy; can cause acute or chronic (iron-deficiency) GI bleeding and mimics variceal bleeding as a cause of haematemesis/melaena in the same cirrhotic patient
Gastric varices — also driven by portal hypertension but managed differently from oesophageal varices
# Additional investigations
Upper endoscopy within 24 h is first-line both to diagnose the bleeding source and to treat acutely; more urgent endoscopy (6-12 h) does not improve outcomes and may worsen them in high-risk patients
Endoscopic surveillance for varices: recommended at diagnosis of compensated cirrhosis, then every 2 years (active liver disease) or every 3 years (inactive); also at the time of hepatic decompensation
Liver stiffness by transient elastography — if <20 kPa and no thrombocytopenia, high-risk varices are rare and screening endoscopy may be deferred
HVPG measurement by interventional radiology (hepatic vein cannulation) in selected cases to confirm/quantify portal hypertension
Complete blood count (thrombocytopenia from hypersplenism is often the first marker of portal hypertension), liver biochemistry, albumin, and prothrombin time/INR to assess hepatic function and severity
Endoscopic stigmata predicting bleeding risk: red wale signs, hematocystic spots, diffuse erythema, bluish colour, cherry-red spots, white-nipple spots; larger varices and worse Child-Pugh/MELD also predict bleeding
# Red flags
Variceal haemorrhage is immediately life-threatening with 20-30% mortality per bleeding episode — treat as a major haemorrhage with airway/haemodynamic priority
Hemodynamic compromise (tachycardia, hypotension), increasing age and comorbidities predict rebleeding and death
Increased frequency of spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP) in patients with variceal haemorrhage — SBP prophylaxis is recommended at presentation with upper GI bleeding
Persistent or recurrent bleeding despite endoscopic and medical therapy, and advanced liver disease (Child-Pugh B score 8-9 with active bleeding, or Child-Pugh C score 10-13), mark patients who benefit from early/salvage TIPS
# Pitfalls
Assuming a normal initial haemoglobin excludes severe bleeding — haemoglobin may be normal or only minimally reduced at presentation and can take up to 72 h to fall as extravascular fluid re-equilibrates
Reflexively correcting a prolonged prothrombin time with FFP — not recommended in variceal bleeding without evidence of true coagulopathy, and FFP can raise portal pressure and cause volume overload
Over-liberal transfusion — a liberal threshold (Hb 9 g/dL) increased early rebleeding and mortality versus a restrictive (<7 g/dL) threshold
Omitting antibiotics in a cirrhotic UGI bleed — prophylactic antibiotics reduce infection, rebleeding and mortality and are a core, not optional, intervention
Attempting band ligation on gastric or proximal-stomach varices, where it is often unsuccessful — consider tissue adhesive, TIPS or BRTO
Forgetting that TIPS causes new or worsened hepatic encephalopathy in as many as 20% of patients, especially the elderly and those with pre-existing encephalopathy
# Variceal bleeding: treatment
Haematemesis/melaena in portal hypertension; haemodynamic compromise. Confirmed and treated at endoscopy, ICU/HDU + gastroenterology.
# Steps
Resuscitate: large-bore IV access; restrictive transfusion (transfuse when Hb <7 g/dL); correct marked coagulopathy/thrombocytopenia before endoscopy.
Give cirrhotic UGIB patients an IV vasoactive drug (octreotide/somatostatin/terlipressin) AND an antibiotic (e.g. ceftriaxone) on presentation — both reduce rebleeding and mortality.
Consider tracheal intubation for airway protection in repeated haematemesis; IV erythromycin ~30-90 min pre-scope to improve visualisation.
Endoscopy within 24 h: variceal band ligation for oesophageal varices, tissue-adhesive injection for gastric varices.
Persistent/recurrent bleeding or advanced liver disease (Child-Pugh B 8-9 with active bleeding, or C 10-13): TIPS; secondary prophylaxis with nonselective beta blocker + band ligation.
