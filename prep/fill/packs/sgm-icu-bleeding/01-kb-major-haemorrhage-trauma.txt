# Major haemorrhage in trauma (massive transfusion)
Adults
Emergency department, operating theatre, ICU
Damage control resuscitation: stop the bleeding fast, activate the major haemorrhage protocol, replace crystalloid with blood products in a high plasma ratio, give tranexamic acid within 3 hours, accept permissive hypotension, and correct fibrinogen, platelets, calcium and temperature guided by lab or viscoelastic tests.
Aliases: MTP; massive transfusion protocol; MHP; major haemorrhage protocol; haemorrhagic shock; damage control resuscitation; trauma-induced coagulopathy; TXA; 1:1:1; ROTEM; TEG.
# Recognise and activate
Activate the major haemorrhage protocol on physiology and the response to initial resuscitation, not on a single-time-point score (NICE NG39).
Features of major bleeding: shock index (heart rate divided by SBP) 0.9 to 1.0 or more, narrow pulse pressure, rising lactate or base deficit, obvious blood loss, or a positive FAST with hypotension.
ABC score of 2 or more (penetrating mechanism, SBP 90 mmHg or less, heart rate 120 or more, positive FAST) predicts the need for massive transfusion.
An initial normal haemoglobin does not exclude major bleeding; repeat measurements. About one-third of severely injured patients arrive coagulopathic.
# Stop the bleeding
External bleeding: direct pressure, tourniquet for life-threatening limb bleeding, haemostatic packing of junctional wounds; pelvic binder for suspected pelvic fracture; splint long-bone fractures.
Obvious source or shock in extremis: immediate bleeding control in theatre or interventional radiology. Unknown source without need for immediate control: find it fast (eFAST, whole-body CT if responding).
Damage control surgery for haemorrhagic shock, ongoing bleeding, coagulopathy, hypothermia or acidosis; pelvic packing or angioembolisation for pelvic bleeding; REBOA may bridge non-compressible bleeding where available.
Transfer early to a trauma centre if definitive haemorrhage control is not available locally.
# Damage control resuscitation
Do not use crystalloid in hospital for active bleeding (NICE NG39). If fluid is needed before blood arrives, give small boluses of 0.9% sodium chloride or balanced crystalloid; avoid Ringer lactate in severe TBI and avoid colloids.
Give blood products early in a fixed ratio: plasma to red cells 1:1 (NICE NG39) with platelets, often packaged as 1:1:1. The European guideline accepts plasma to red cells of at least 1:2, or a fibrinogen concentrate-based strategy, and suggests a high platelet ratio.
Use emergency group O red cells (RhD negative for girls and women of childbearing potential) until group-specific or crossmatched blood is available; switch to group-specific as soon as possible.
Tranexamic acid 1 g IV over 10 minutes then 1 g over 8 hours, as soon as possible and within 3 hours of injury. Do not wait for viscoelastic results. Do not start more than 3 hours after injury: in CRASH-2, later treatment increased deaths from bleeding.
Permissive hypotension until major bleeding stops: SBP 80 to 90 mmHg (MAP 50 to 60 mmHg) if there is no brain injury; keep MAP 80 mmHg or more in severe TBI (GCS 8 or less). Add noradrenaline if targets are not met despite volume.
Warm every fluid and product, use active warming, and aim for core temperature 36 to 37°C.
# Goal-directed coagulation support
Move from fixed ratios to therapy guided by PT/INR, APTT, Clauss fibrinogen, platelet count and viscoelastic tests (ROTEM or TEG) as soon as results are available.
Fibrinogen: give fibrinogen concentrate or cryoprecipitate if Clauss fibrinogen is 1.5 g/L or less or viscoelastic tests show a functional fibrinogen deficit. Initial dose 3 to 4 g (3 to 4 g concentrate or 15 to 20 single-donor units of cryoprecipitate).
Plasma: further FFP if PT or APTT is more than 1.5 times normal or viscoelastic tests show a factor deficiency. Do not use FFP to correct low fibrinogen when cryoprecipitate or concentrate is available.
Platelets: keep above 50 x 10^9/L while bleeding and above 100 x 10^9/L with TBI; initial dose 4 to 8 single units or 1 apheresis unit.
Red cells: once bleeding is controlled, target haemoglobin 7 to 9 g/dL.
Ionised calcium: monitor on every blood gas and keep in the normal range (1.1 to 1.3 mmol/L), especially during massive transfusion. Give calcium when it falls below 1.1 mmol/L; calcium chloride is the preferred agent. Below 0.9 mmol/L is urgent (arrhythmia risk below 0.8).
Recombinant factor VIIa is not first line; consider only if bleeding and coagulopathy persist despite surgical control and all other measures.
# Anticoagulated patients
Warfarin or other vitamin K antagonists: 4-factor PCC immediately plus vitamin K 5 to 10 mg IV; do not use plasma for reversal (NICE NG39).
Dabigatran: idarucizumab 5 g IV for life-threatening bleeding; measure dilute thrombin time if available.
Apixaban or rivaroxaban with life-threatening bleeding (especially TBI): andexanet alfa if available; otherwise, or for edoxaban, PCC 25 to 50 units/kg.
Antiplatelet agents: do not give routine platelet transfusion; ask about antiplatelet and anticoagulant use in every trauma patient.
# Monitoring
Repeat blood gas (pH, lactate, base deficit, ionised calcium, potassium, haemoglobin) and coagulation tests frequently during active transfusion, for example every 30 to 60 minutes.
Watch for hyperkalaemia, hypocalcaemia, hypothermia, citrate toxicity, transfusion-associated circulatory overload and TRALI.
Stand down the protocol once bleeding is controlled; tell the blood bank and return unused products.
Thromboprophylaxis: intermittent pneumatic compression early; add pharmacological prophylaxis within 24 hours after bleeding is controlled.
# Pitfalls
Waiting for the haemoglobin to fall before transfusing, or using crystalloid as the main resuscitation fluid.
Forgetting calcium, fibrinogen and warming (the lethal triad of hypothermia, acidosis and coagulopathy).
Giving tranexamic acid more than 3 hours after injury.
Continuing fixed-ratio transfusion long after lab or viscoelastic results are available.
Delaying surgery or embolisation to chase normal coagulation numbers.
# Drugs
Tranexamic acid
1 g IV over 10 minutes, then 1 g IV infusion over 8 hours
Within 3 hours of injury, as early as possible. Do not start after 3 hours.
Fibrinogen concentrate
3 to 4 g IV initial dose
If Clauss fibrinogen is 1.5 g/L or less or viscoelastic evidence of deficit; repeat guided by levels.
Cryoprecipitate, 15 to 20 single-donor units IV (about 3 to 4 g fibrinogen), Alternative to fibrinogen concentrate.
Calcium chloride 10%
# Dose
10 mL IV (6.8 mmol, 270 mg elemental calcium) slowly via a large or central vein, repeat guided by ionised calcium
Calcium gluconate 10% 30 mL IV is an equivalent alternative.
Prothrombin complex concentrate (4-factor)
25 to 50 units/kg IV
Vitamin K antagonist reversal (with vitamin K 5 to 10 mg IV), or factor Xa inhibitor bleeding when andexanet is unavailable.
Idarucizumab, 5 g IV, Dabigatran reversal.
Noradrenaline (norepinephrine), Start 0.05 to 0.1 mcg/kg/min IV, titrate to the target pressure, Only when restricted volume replacement fails to reach target blood pressure.
