# Massive transfusion
Massive transfusion, Haematology / Transfusion, Transfusion & Haemostasis Medicine.
Aliases: massive transfusion protocol; MTP; massive haemorrhage protocol.
# Pathophysiology
Massive haemorrhage and its replacement produce a combination of dilutional and consumptive coagulopathy: clotting factors and platelets are lost with bleeding and diluted by crystalloid and red cell replacement. Hypoperfusion drives metabolic acidosis and, together with infusion of cold products, causes hypothermia; both impair enzymatic clotting and platelet function. Citrate load from banked products chelates ionised calcium and magnesium, further impairing coagulation and cardiac contractility, while potassium leaked from stored red cells can accumulate. The result is a self-perpetuating cycle of bleeding, impaired haemostasis, and shock unless actively interrupted.
# Prognosis
Outcome depends chiefly on control of the bleeding source and speed of resuscitation; early balanced transfusion, prevention of the lethal triad, and timely antifibrinolytic therapy improve survival, whereas delayed correction of coagulopathy and shock markedly increases mortality.
# Clinical pearls
Commonly defined as replacement of roughly one whole blood volume within about 24 hours, or transfusion of around ten or more red cell units in that period; rapid-loss definitions (several units within an hour) are also used in trauma
Modern resuscitation favours a balanced ratio of red cells, plasma, and platelets that approximates whole blood rather than red cells alone, to limit dilutional coagulopathy
The lethal triad of hypothermia, acidosis, and coagulopathy compounds bleeding and must be actively prevented with warmed fluids and blood products
Citrate anticoagulant in stored products binds calcium, so ionised hypocalcaemia is common during rapid transfusion and should be monitored and corrected
Hyperkalaemia can occur, especially with older stored red cells and rapid infusion in neonates or patients with renal impairment
Point-of-care viscoelastic testing (thromboelastography or ROTEM) helps guide targeted product replacement in preference to fixed ratios once bleeding is controlled
Tranexamic acid given early in traumatic haemorrhage reduces mortality; early activation of an institutional protocol streamlines product delivery
Additional differentials: Disseminated intravascular coagulation; Pre-existing anticoagulant or antiplatelet effect; Inherited or acquired factor deficiency; Hyperfibrinolysis; Surgical or mechanical bleeding requiring intervention.
# Additional investigations
Serial full blood count, prothrombin time/INR, activated partial thromboplastin time, and fibrinogen
Ionised calcium, potassium, and arterial blood gas with lactate and pH
Viscoelastic haemostatic assays (TEG/ROTEM) for goal-directed replacement
Core temperature monitoring
Type and crossmatch with repeat sampling to detect ongoing loss
# Red flags
Ongoing uncontrolled haemorrhage with haemodynamic instability requiring emergency surgical or interventional haemostasis
Refractory hypocalcaemia with cardiovascular compromise during rapid transfusion
Rising potassium with electrocardiographic changes
Persistent coagulopathy despite balanced product resuscitation, suggesting DIC or hyperfibrinolysis
# Pitfalls
Over-reliance on crystalloid resuscitation, worsening dilutional coagulopathy and tissue oedema
