Cancer pain scheduling
- Persistent cancer pain is managed with a background opioid on a timed plan, dosed around the clock.
- Short-acting rescue doses handle flares on top of that background. A rescue dose is typically 5 to 15 percent of the total daily amount.
- If pain keeps breaking through, the scheduled amount can rise by 30 to 100 percent of what the patient took in the previous 24 hours.
- Increases should wait until the drug reaches steady state, roughly five to six half-lives.

Analgesic ladder
- Tier one is a nonopioid for mild pain. Tier two is a weak opioid for moderate pain. Tier three is a strong opioid for severe pain.
- When pain still is not controlled after optimal opioid therapy, a fourth tier applies: interventional pain management.
- Interventional options include nerve blocks and intrathecal opioid infusions, given into the fluid around the spinal cord. Access depends on specialist services.

Opioid-induced constipation
- Opioids slow the gut and reduce secretions, so constipation is very common.
- Tolerance develops to many opioid effects, but many patients never adapt to the constipation.
- Every patient starting an opioid needs a standing bowel regimen.
- Stimulant laxatives such as senna and bisacodyl are first line. Polyethylene glycol is an osmotic option often paired with a stimulant.
- Stimulants act on peristalsis, the rhythmic squeezing of the gut.
- Methylnaltrexone is a peripherally acting mu opioid antagonist. It reaches gut receptors but not the brain, so pain relief stays intact.
- Methylnaltrexone is given by subcutaneous injection and produces laxation within about 24 hours in trials. It is reserved for constipation that resists standard laxatives. It is contraindicated with bowel obstruction, recent bowel surgery, or a breach in the bowel wall.

Trigger point injections
- Myofascial pain comes from muscle and fascia. Palpable nodules within taut bands of muscle are the hallmark.
- Trigger point injections may ease spasm by improving local blood flow.
- No particular injectate, such as a local anesthetic, steroid, or botulinum toxin, has been shown superior.
- Data for cancer patients are limited.

Dyspnoea
- Breathlessness is treated mainly by addressing causes when possible, then by opioids.
- Opioids are the most effective drug for dyspnoea.
- In opioid-naive patients a small dose of morphine helps. Patients already on an opioid have the baseline increased by 25 percent.
- Anxiety often accompanies breathlessness. Benzodiazepines may be used alongside an opioid for anxiety.
- Benzodiazepines should not be used alone as first-line therapy for dyspnoea.

Bupropion
- Bupropion is an antidepressant that also aids smoking cessation.
- Starting dose 75 milligrams. Usual range 150 to 300 milligrams per 24 hours.
- It carries a seizure risk.
- It inhibits cytochrome P450 2D6 (CYP2D6). That enzyme converts tamoxifen to its active metabolite endoxifen, so bupropion can reduce endoxifen levels.
- Cancer fatigue: one review found fatigue reduced in six of seven studies, with few side effects. Other guidance advises against antidepressants for fatigue alone in non-depressed patients, since no benefit beyond placebo was shown.

Emetogenic risk
- Cisplatin is highly emetogenic, with emesis risk greater than 90 percent without prophylaxis, at any dose.
- Anthracycline combined with cyclophosphamide is also high risk.
- Cyclophosphamide is high risk above 1500 milligrams per square metre; below that it is moderate.
- Carboplatin is moderate risk. An area under the curve of 4 or more adds a neurokinin-1 antagonist to the usual prophylaxis.
