Scheduled cancer pain control
- Persistent cancer pain is handled with a background opioid on a timed plan, dosed around the clock rather than when pain flares.
- A short-acting rescue dose covers breakthrough spikes. Its size is usually 5 to 15 percent of the total daily amount.
- If pain keeps breaking through the schedule, the scheduled amount can rise by 30 to 100 percent of what was taken over the previous 24 hours.
- Escalation should wait for steady state, which takes roughly five to six half-lives.

Analgesic ladder
- Tier one is a non-opioid agent aimed at mild pain. Tier two adds a milder opioid for moderate pain. Tier three uses a potent opioid for severe pain.
- When pain remains uncontrolled despite optimal opioid therapy, a fourth tier of interventional pain management is used.
- Interventional options include nerve blocks and intrathecal opioid infusions, delivered into the fluid around the spinal cord. Specialist services limit access.

Opioid-induced constipation
- Opioids slow the gut and reduce secretions, so constipation is among the commonest effects.
- Tolerance develops to many opioid effects, but many patients never adapt to constipation.
- A standing bowel regimen should begin with the first opioid dose.
- Stimulant laxatives such as senna and bisacodyl are first line. Polyethylene glycol is an osmotic agent often combined with a stimulant.
- Bulk laxatives, including psyllium, are not recommended for this problem.
- Methylnaltrexone is a peripherally acting opioid antagonist. It acts on gut receptors and does not reach the brain, so analgesia survives.
- Administration is subcutaneous. Dosing depends on body weight: 8 milligrams in the 38 to 61 kilogram band, 12 milligrams in the 62 to 114 kilogram band.
- It produces laxation within about a day in trials. It is reserved for constipation that resists standard laxatives.
- Contraindications: a current obstruction, surgery within recent weeks, or a wall defect that could perforate.

Myofascial pain and trigger points
- Myofascial pain arises from muscle and the fascia around it. Palpable nodules within taut muscle bands are a hallmark of the condition.
- Trigger point injections may relieve spasm through improved local blood flow. Clearance of inflammatory mediators is also proposed.
- No specific injectate, such as saline, a local anesthetic, a steroid, or botulinum toxin, has been shown superior.
- Evidence in cancer patients is limited.

Dyspnoea
- Opioids are the most effective drug for breathlessness. Small morphine doses help opioid-naive patients.
- Patients already on a baseline opioid have that dose raised by about 25 percent.
- Benzodiazepines relieve anxiety that accompanies breathlessness. They are not first-line therapy for breathlessness itself.

Bupropion
- Bupropion is an antidepressant that also supports smoking cessation.
- Antidepressant titration begins at 75 milligrams. Daily totals most often fall between 150 and 300 milligrams.
- Patients prone to fits should avoid it, since seizure risk rises.
- Central nervous system tumours are a reason to avoid it. Brain metastases fall within that group.
- It inhibits cytochrome P450 2D6 (CYP2D6), the enzyme that activates some drugs. CYP2D6 converts tamoxifen to endoxifen, so bupropion can reduce endoxifen levels.
- In cancer fatigue, one systematic review reported less fatigue in six of seven studies. The other view is that antidepressants are not recommended specifically for fatigue, since placebo performed as well in patients without depression.

Emetogenic risk
- Cisplatin carries more than 90 percent emesis risk without prophylaxis, at any dose.
- An anthracycline combined with cyclophosphamide is high risk.
- Cyclophosphamide is high risk above 1500 milligrams per square metre and moderate below that.
- Carboplatin is moderate risk. An area under the curve of 4 or more adds a neurokinin-1 antagonist to prophylaxis.
- Doxorubicin and epirubicin given alone are moderate risk. Neither book gives a dose threshold that moves them into the high group.
