Pediatric rheumatology beyond JIA: childhood lupus, juvenile dermatomyositis with calcinosis, MIS-C, chronic nonbacterial osteomyelitis, growth failure, transition.

Childhood-onset systemic lupus erythematosus, called cSLE and diagnosed before age 18 years, accounts for 15 percent to 20 percent of all SLE cases, exhibiting an aggressive disease phenotype with significantly higher rates of severe proliferative lupus nephritis in above 50 percent to 60 percent of patients, severe neuropsychiatric involvement with seizures and psychosis, and higher cumulative glucocorticoid toxicity with growth and bone damage. Adult classification criteria apply but treatment runs harder, with mycophenolate and cyclophosphamide induction for nephritis plus hydroxychloroquine for every child.

Juvenile dermatomyositis, called JDM, is the most common idiopathic inflammatory myopathy in childhood, characterized by pathognomonic heliotrope rash with a violaceous blush around the eyes, Gottron papules over the knuckles, symmetrical proximal muscle weakness with Gower sign on rising, and a distinctive prominent vasculopathy involving skin with nailfold dropout, muscles with perifascicular atrophy on biopsy, and the gastrointestinal tract with ulceration and perforation risk. Muscle enzymes with myositis antibodies and MRI guide therapy with glucocorticoids plus methotrexate, escalating to IVIG or rituximab for ulcerating or lung disease.

Dystrophic calcinosis, spanning calcinosis cutis lumps and rock-hard calcinosis universalis sheets, complicates up to 30 percent to 40 percent of JDM cases, particularly in patients with delayed diagnosis, prolonged active unremitting inflammation, or anti-NXP2 autoantibodies, causing painful subcutaneous nodules, skin ulceration with chalky discharge, and debilitating joint contractures. Early aggressive immunosuppression prevents it; established lumps take diltiazem, bisphosphonates, or surgical debulking.

Multisystem Inflammatory Syndrome in Children, called MIS-C, is a post-infectious hyperinflammatory syndrome developing 2 to 6 weeks following SARS-CoV-2 infection, characterized by persistent fever above 38 degrees, shock with vasopressor need, acute myocardial dysfunction with depressed ejection fraction, markedly elevated inflammatory markers including ferritin, CRP, D-dimer, troponin, and NT-proBNP, and features overlapping with Kawasaki disease such as rash, conjunctivitis, and coronary dilation. IVIG with glucocorticoids settles most cases, with anakinra or infliximab rescue.

Chronic nonbacterial osteomyelitis, called CNO and also known as chronic recurrent multifocal osteomyelitis or CRMO, is an autoinflammatory bone disorder of childhood presenting with recurrent episodes of sterile osteolytic bone pain and swelling, characteristically affecting the metaphyses of long bones, the clavicle, and the vertebrae, requiring whole-body MRI to map silent multifocal lesions. Cultures are sterile and antibiotics never help; biopsy excludes infection and malignancy in single-bone disease.

First-line therapy for CNO and CRMO relies on regular scheduled NSAID therapy (NSAIDs) at anti-inflammatory doses; patients with active spinal lesions or disease refractory to NSAIDs respond dramatically to intravenous pamidronate or zoledronic acid, bisphosphonates that resolve bone edema and prevent vertebral collapse, or to anti-TNF biologics such as etanercept and adalimumab. Spinal disease is never left on NSAIDs alone.

Growth failure in pediatric rheumatic diseases results from a combination of chronic active systemic inflammation, where IL-6 and TNF-alpha directly inhibit the growth hormone and IGF-1 axis with epiphyseal chondrocyte proliferation, and prolonged exposure to high-dose systemic glucocorticoids that flatten growth velocity. Steroid-sparing with methotrexate and biologics protects height, with growth charts at every visit.

Transition of adolescent patients from pediatric to adult rheumatology services requires a planned, structured multidisciplinary transition process initiated early by age 13 to 14 years, fostering patient self-advocacy, medication autonomy with adherence review, and preventing loss to follow-up during young adulthood. Readiness checklists with joint clinics bridge the gap.
