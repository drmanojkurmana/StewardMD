# Infectious diarrhoea and Clostridioides difficile
# Source: StewardMD gastroenterology knowledge base / Sleisenger 11e Ch. 109, 111

## Clostridioides difficile diagnostic criteria and therapy
Clostridioides difficile infection (CDI) is diagnosed by diarrhoea (>= 3 unformed stools in 24 hours) accompanied by positive stool toxin testing (EIA for toxins A/B or two-step algorithm combining GDH with toxin EIA/NAAT).
Oral fidaxomicin (200 mg twice daily for 10 days) is the preferred first-line bactericidal treatment for initial and recurrent CDI, associated with significantly lower recurrence rates compared to oral vancomycin (125 mg four times daily).
Fulminant CDI is defined by hypotension, shock, ileus, or toxic megacolon, treated urgently with high-dose oral vancomycin (500 mg four times daily) via NG tube, rectal vancomycin enemas, and intravenous metronidazole.

## Recurrence prevention and microbiome restoration
Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) or oral fecal microbiota products (fecal microbiota spores) achieve > 80% cure rates in patients with multiply recurrent C. difficile infection refractory to pulsed antibiotic regimens.
Bezlotoxumab is a fully human IgG1 monoclonal antibody with a half-life of 19 days that binds and neutralizes C. difficile toxin B, approved as adjunctive therapy in patients aged over 65 to prevent recurrent CDI in high-risk patients.

## Key parasitic and bacterial enteric pathogens
Amoebic colitis caused by Entamoeba histolytica characteristically produces discrete 'flask-shaped' mucosal ulcerations with intervening normal mucosa, diagnosed by stool antigen or multiplex PCR, treated with metronidazole followed by luminal paromomycin.
Cholera caused by Vibrio cholerae serogroups O1 or O139 produces massive secretory 'rice-water' diarrhoea driven by cholera toxin ADP-ribosylation of G-alpha-s, managed with aggressive isotonic rehydration and single-dose azithromycin or doxycycline.
Shiga toxin-producing E. coli (STEC/EHEC O157:H7) causes bloody diarrhoea where antibiotic therapy is strictly avoided due to increased release of Shiga toxins provoking hemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS).

## Additional clinical details from Sleisenger
Pseudomembranous colitis features raised yellow-white mucosal plaques composed of fibrin, mucin, and sloughed epithelial cells.
White blood cell count > 15,000 cells/uL or serum creatinine > 1.5 mg/dL defines severe CDI.
Loperamide and anti-motility agents are contraindicated in toxigenic infectious diarrhoea due to toxic megacolon risk.
