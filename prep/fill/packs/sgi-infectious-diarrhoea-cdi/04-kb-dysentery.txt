# Dysentery (Invasive Bacterial Diarrhea)
Dysentery (Invasive Bacterial Diarrhea), Gastrointestinal.
# Pathogens
The combination of fever, bloody stool, and tenesmus reflects mucosal invasion rather than toxin-mediated secretory diarrhea, pointing toward Shigella and other invasive enteric bacteria as the leading causes in this clinical pattern.
Very likely: Shigella species; Enteroinvasive/Enterohemorrhagic E. coli.
Likely: Campylobacter jejuni; Non-typhoidal Salmonella.
Possible: Entamoeba histolytica (if subacute, travel/endemic exposure).
# Investigations
Stool culture
Identifies the causative organism and susceptibility, the basis for de-escalation or confirmation of empiric choice.
Stool microscopy for ova and parasites, Excludes or confirms Entamoeba histolytica, which requires different therapy (metronidazole).
CBC, Assesses degree of inflammatory response and any hemolysis (relevant if EHEC/HUS is suspected).
Renal function tests, Screens for hemolytic uremic syndrome, particularly important if Shiga-toxin-producing E. coli is suspected.
Antibiotics recommended
# Reason
Bloody diarrhea with fever and tenesmus is consistent with invasive bacterial dysentery (e.g., Shigella, invasive E. coli, Campylobacter). Unlike watery gastroenteritis, this pattern justifies empiric antimicrobial therapy, particularly in moderate-to-severe disease, while awaiting stool culture.
# Pathophysiology
Dysentery results from injury to the colonic (and distal small-bowel) mucosa by two overlapping mechanisms. (1) Bacterial invasion: Shigella and enteroinvasive E. coli invade mucosal epithelial cells, multiply intracellularly and spread cell-to-cell, producing acute colitis mainly of the distal colon and rectum with edematous, hemorrhagic, ulcerated mucosa. (2) Cytotoxin production: organisms such as Shigella dysenteriae type 1, Shiga toxin-producing/enterohaemorrhagic E. coli and Vibrio parahaemolyticus elaborate cytotoxins that destroy mucosal cells; Shiga toxins inhibit the 60S ribosomal subunit, shut off protein synthesis, and after systemic translocation injure renal and cerebral microvascular endothelium. The resulting mucosal destruction yields small-volume bloody, mucopurulent stools containing inflammatory cells. Salmonella invades the bowel mucosa and causes inflammatory diarrhoea but generally does not destroy enterocytes or produce the full dysentery syndrome. Entamoeba histolytica trophozoites adhere via the Gal/GalNAc lectin, degrade mucus and extracellular matrix with proteases, and kill epithelial and inflammatory cells by contact-dependent (apoptosis, trogocytosis, pore-forming peptides) and contact-independent cytotoxicity, producing characteristic flask-shaped colonic ulcers and bloody diarrhoea, and can invade the portal venous system to seed the liver.
# Prognosis
Most invasive bacterial dysentery is self-limited: shigellosis usually resolves within about a week (faster with appropriate therapy), and STEC/EHEC illness typically lasts 5-10 days. Severe outcomes and death cluster in children under 5 (especially the malnourished) and the elderly. Adverse prognostic features in clinically severe shigellosis include nonbloody diarrhoea, moderate-to-severe dehydration, bacteraemia, absence of fever, abdominal tenderness, rectal prolapse, and altered consciousness. Haemolytic-uraemic syndrome occurs in roughly 15% of infected children under 10 with STEC/EHEC; with dialysis support its mortality is under 10%, but survivors often have persisting renal and neurological dysfunction; acute renal failure complicates 55-70% of HUS cases yet renal function recovers in most. Postinfectious sequelae include reactive arthritis (after Shigella flexneri, Salmonella, Campylobacter, Yersinia) and, after Campylobacter, Guillain-Barre syndrome; postinfectious irritable bowel syndrome may follow, particularly after Campylobacter.
# Severity classification
Inflammatory (dysenteric) disease is defined by grossly bloody/mucoid stool with fecal leukocytes/lactoferrin and a colonic locus. Indications that mark an episode as severe and warranting evaluation/empirical therapy include profuse diarrhoea with dehydration, overtly bloody stools, fever >=38.5C (>=101F), duration >48 h without improvement, severe abdominal pain in patients over 50, and elderly (>=70) or immunocompromised hosts. Dehydration is graded clinically as mild (thirst, dry mouth, decreased urine output), moderate (orthostatic hypotension, skin tenting, sunken eyes/fontanelle), and severe (lethargy, obtundation, feeble pulse, hypotension, shock). For shigellosis, severe disease is signalled by features such as bacteraemia, altered consciousness/seizures, toxic megacolon, perforation, severe dehydration, and occurrence in malnourished young children or the elderly.
# Clinical pearls
Dysentery is bloody diarrhoea arising from mucosal invasion or cytotoxin injury to the colon, not from secretory/enterotoxin mechanisms; the triad of high fever, abdominal pain and grossly bloody stool points to invasive disease rather than toxin-mediated watery diarrhoea.
The single most useful initial distinction is inflammatory vs noninflammatory diarrhoea: grossly bloody or mucoid stool, plus fecal leukocytes or a positive fecal lactoferrin/calprotectin, defines the inflammatory (dysenteric) category and a colonic/distal small-bowel locus.
Tenesmus (painful rectal spasm with a strong urge but little stool passage) signals proctitis and is characteristic of the distal-colonic involvement of shigellosis and amoebiasis.
Bloody diarrhoea with a low-grade fever or no fever is a clinical clue to Shiga toxin-producing/enterohaemorrhagic E. coli (EHEC, e.g. O157:H7), which can be mistaken for noninfectious disease such as ischaemic or inflammatory bowel disease.
Unlike most watery diarrhoeal syndromes, dysenteric illness rarely presents with major dehydration; in shigellosis the stools are small-volume and dehydration is uncommon.
Shigellosis classically evolves through four phases: incubation (usually 1-4 days), initial watery diarrhoea, then dysentery with frequent small bloody mucopurulent stools, tenesmus and cramps, followed by a postinfectious phase.
On stool microscopy, abundant PMNs in every field favour bacterial enteroinvasive disease (especially Shigella), whereas erythrophagocytic trophozoites with few PMNs favour Entamoeba histolytica.
Fewer than ~40% of patients with amoebic dysentery are febrile, in contrast to bacterial dysentery; nearly all have heme-positive stools, and amoebic colitis typically has a more gradual onset over 2-6 weeks.
EHEC/STEC stools are commonly grossly bloody with abdominal pain and fecal leukocytes in ~70% of cases, yet fever is characteristically absent.
An appendicitis-like presentation (right-sided pain, tenderness) should raise Yersinia enterocolitica, which invades the terminal ileum and proximal colon; Campylobacter and amoebic caecal involvement can similarly mimic appendicitis.
# Additional differentials
Shigella species (bacillary dysentery; prototypic invasive cause)
Shiga toxin-producing / enterohaemorrhagic E. coli (STEC/EHEC, e.g. O157:H7)
Enteroinvasive E. coli (EIEC; shigellosis-like dysentery, shares ancestry with Shigella)
Campylobacter jejuni (inflammatory diarrhoea, ~10% grossly bloody in adults)
Non-typhoidal Salmonella (inflammatory diarrhoea; full dysentery uncommon)
Yersinia enterocolitica (terminal ileitis/right-sided pain mimicking appendicitis)
Entamoeba histolytica (amoebic dysentery; subacute, travel/endemic exposure)
Vibrio parahaemolyticus (can cause dysentery; shellfish exposure)
Clostridioides difficile (cytotoxin-mediated colitis; antibiotic/healthcare exposure)
Klebsiella oxytoca (antibiotic-associated haemorrhagic colitis)
# Infection mimics
Clostridioides difficile colitis (consider with recent or current antibiotic use, hospitalisation/long-term care; can also be community-acquired without prior antibiotics)
Enteric fever (Salmonella Typhi, Yersinia enterocolitica) - penetrating organisms that can present with fever and abdominal pain; fecal mononuclear leukocytes rather than frank dysentery
Schistosomiasis and other invasive parasitic colitides in endemic exposure
Sexually transmitted proctocolitis (gonococcal, syphilitic, chlamydial/LGV) presenting with bloody mucoid rectal discharge and tenesmus
# Non infectious mimics
Inflammatory bowel disease - a first flare of ulcerative colitis or Crohn's disease can be clinically indistinguishable from invasive bacterial dysentery, particularly in industrialised settings; travel history and stool studies help discriminate, and IBD should not be diagnosed until invasive infection (e.g. Campylobacter, amoebiasis) is excluded
Ischaemic colitis - typically age >50, acute lower abdominal pain preceding watery then bloody diarrhoea, with inflammation of the sigmoid/left colon and rectal sparing
Colonic diverticulitis
Graft-versus-host disease of the gut
Intussusception (can mimic afebrile bloody diarrhoea, especially in children)
Drug-induced colitis (e.g. NSAIDs and other medication-related diarrhoea)
# Additional investigations
Fecal leukocyte smear (methylene blue wet mount) - suggests inflammatory disease, though predictive value is debated
Fecal lactoferrin (latex agglutination or ELISA) - more sensitive surrogate marker of fecal leukocytes than microscopy; fecal calprotectin similarly used
Specific/selective stool culture with isolate retention for Salmonella, Shigella and Campylobacter (MacConkey/Salmonella-Shigella/selenite media; Campylobacter on selective medium at 42C microaerophilic); sampling the bloody/mucopurulent portion or a rectal swab improves Shigella yield
Dedicated Shiga toxin / Shiga toxin-gene testing AND culture in ALL community-acquired diarrhoea (inflammatory or not), because bloody stool and fecal leukocytes are not reliably present with STEC/EHEC; O157 screened as sorbitol non-fermenters on sorbitol-MacConkey
Culture-independent multiplex nucleic acid amplification panels (e.g. detecting STEC, ETEC, EPEC, EAEC, EIEC) - faster and more sensitive than culture, but a positive result should be followed by attempted culture isolation for susceptibility testing and outbreak surveillance
Stool examination for ova and parasites with E. histolytica-specific rapid antigen detection (microscopy is less sensitive/specific); definitive amoebic diagnosis requires hematophagous trophozoites, as cysts cannot be distinguished from E. dispar/E. moshkovskii/E. bangladeshi; examine at least three fresh specimens
Amoebic serology (ELISA) - positive in >90% of amoebic colitis/ameboma/liver abscess and useful before starting glucocorticoids when IBD is considered
CBC and peripheral smear (microangiopathic hemolytic anaemia, schizocytes, thrombocytopenia signalling HUS), renal function/creatinine, and LDH when STEC/EHEC or S. dysenteriae 1 is suspected
Sigmoidoscopy/colonoscopy with biopsy - reserved for uncharacterised persistent diarrhoea or suspected IBD; endoscopic biopsy may not distinguish Campylobacter colitis from IBD, and is hazardous during fulminant amoebic colitis (perforation risk)
# Red flags
Grossly bloody diarrhoea with low-grade or absent fever - suspect STEC/EHEC and the risk of haemolytic-uraemic syndrome
Falling urine output, pallor, irritability, edema, or new neurological signs after several days of diarrhoea - developing HUS (microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia, thrombocytopenia, acute renal failure)
Abdominal distension and tenderness with loss of haustration / dilated transverse colon - toxic megacolon, a complication of severe Shigella (and severe amoebic) colitis
Signs of perforation or peritonitis (rebound, free air)
Altered consciousness, seizures or coma - seen especially in children under 5 with severe shigellosis and carries a poor prognosis
Severe dehydration (lethargy, feeble pulse, hypotension, shock)
High fever (>=38.5C), profuse diarrhoea, severe abdominal pain in patients over 50, or illness in the elderly (>=70) or immunocompromised - warrant prompt stool microbiological evaluation
Rectal prolapse in a child with dysentery (a recognised complication of severe shigellosis)
# Pitfalls
Do NOT give antibiotics for suspected enterohaemorrhagic/Shiga toxin-producing E. coli (grossly bloody diarrhoea, often afebrile) - antibiotics induce Shiga toxin-producing phage replication, can increase toxin production roughly 20-fold, and raise the risk of HUS; this caution applies especially to children.
Avoid antimotility/antiperistaltic agents (e.g. loperamide, diphenoxylate-atropine) in febrile or bloody dysentery - they may prolong fever and shedding in Shigella, increase the risk of toxic megacolon, and have been implicated in HUS in EHEC-infected children.
Do not diagnose inflammatory bowel disease before excluding invasive infection - Campylobacter and amoebic colitis can closely mimic and even be histologically confused with ulcerative colitis or Crohn's disease.
Exclude amoebiasis (stool studies plus serology) before giving glucocorticoids for presumed IBD - unsuspected amoebic colitis can become fulminant or develop toxic megacolon on steroids.
A negative fecal leukocyte/lactoferrin test or absence of fever does NOT rule out invasive disease - STEC/EHEC frequently lacks reliable fecal leukocytes and fever, which is why both Shiga toxin testing and culture are recommended in all community-acquired diarrhoea.
Shigella is fragile and is easily lost in transport (temperature/pH changes); inoculate promptly or use a holding medium (Cary-Blair) and sample the bloody/mucopurulent stool portion or use a rectal swab to maximise yield.
Microscopy cannot distinguish E. histolytica cysts from non-pathogenic E. dispar (and E. moshkovskii/E. bangladeshi); a microscopic species diagnosis of E. histolytica requires demonstrating trophozoites that have ingested erythrocytes (use antigen/molecular testing).
Empirical antimotility therapy should be avoided more broadly with febrile dysentery; bismuth subsalicylate should be avoided in immunocompromised or renally impaired patients (bismuth encephalopathy risk).
