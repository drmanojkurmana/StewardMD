# Clostridioides difficile Infection
Clostridioides difficile Infection, Gastrointestinal.
# Pathogens
C. difficile is essentially the sole cause of antibiotic-associated colitis with this clinical pattern; testing (toxin EIA, PCR, or a two-step algorithm) confirms the diagnosis since asymptomatic colonization is common and should not be treated.

# Reason
New diarrhea following recent antibiotic exposure (often within the preceding 8 weeks, though it can occur during therapy) raises suspicion for Clostridioides difficile infection. Note the specific terminology here — treatment is itself with a different class of "antibiotics" targeted at C. difficile (oral vancomycin or fidaxomicin), which is distinct from and the inverse of typical antibiotic stewardship: the priority is stopping the inciting antibiotic if still being given, plus starting C. difficile-directed therapy.
# Pathophysiology
Ingested spores of toxigenic C. difficile survive gastric acid, germinate in the small bowel, and colonise the lower gut once antibiotic exposure has disrupted the protective microbiota. There they elaborate two large clostridial toxins: toxin A (an enterotoxin and potent neutrophil chemoattractant) and toxin B (a cytotoxin). Both glucosylate Rho-family GTP-binding proteins that regulate the actin cytoskeleton, causing loss of cell shape, adherence and tight junctions, with epithelial barrier breakdown, fluid leakage, diarrhoea and pseudomembrane formation. Toxin B appears to be the dominant virulence factor: disease occurs with toxin A-negative but not toxin B-negative strains. A third (binary) toxin, CDT, is present in all NAP1/BI/027 epidemic isolates but its pathogenic role is undefined. Disease typically requires at least three events: antibiotic exposure establishing susceptibility, acquisition of a toxigenic strain, and an inadequate host (anamnestic antibody) immune response. Notably, patients colonised by (often nontoxigenic) strains who have not previously had CDI actually have a DECREASED, not increased, risk of subsequent CDI.
# Prognosis
Most patients recover, but recurrences are common (~15-30%). Attributable mortality, historically 0.6-3.5%, has reached ~6.9% in recent epidemic-strain outbreaks and rises progressively with increasing age.
Mild-to-moderate disease lacks these features.
# Clinical pearls
CDI is the most commonly diagnosed hospital-acquired diarrhoeal illness and the leading health care-associated infection; it follows ingestion of spores that germinate and produce toxins once the normal colonic microbiota has been disrupted.
Almost any antibiotic can precipitate CDI, including vancomycin itself; clindamycin, ampicillin, cephalosporins (especially 2nd/3rd generation), and fluoroquinolones carry the highest risk, whereas penicillin/beta-lactamase-inhibitor combinations carry notably less.
Proton pump inhibitors are at most a modest risk factor; the data are weak and PPIs have not been clearly implicated in patients not already on antibiotics.
Asymptomatic faecal carriage rises with length of hospital stay (often >=20% after 2 weeks inpatient) versus only 1-3% in community residents; neonates and infants are very frequently colonised yet almost never develop disease, attributed to absent mucosal toxin receptors early in life.
Stools in CDI are essentially never grossly bloody and have a characteristic odour, ranging from soft/unformed to watery or mucoid.
Recurrence after treatment occurs in roughly 15-30% of cases and may be either relapse with the same strain or reinfection with a new one, driven by ongoing disruption of the faecal microbiota by the treating antibiotic; a first recurrence raises the risk of a second.
Serum IgG antibody to toxins A and B from prior colonisation/childhood exposure helps determine who develops symptomatic disease versus remains an asymptomatic carrier and protects against recurrence.
Spores persist on environmental surfaces for months and on the hands of staff; alcohol hand gels are NOT sporicidal, so soap-and-water hand washing is required during outbreaks.
# Additional investigations
Only diarrhoeal (unformed) stool should be tested; testing of asymptomatic patients is not recommended except for epidemiologic study.
NAAT/PCR for the toxin gene is rapid and sensitive but may detect mere colonisation in a patient whose diarrhoea is due to another cause; toxin EIA is specific but insensitive.
Recommended multi-step algorithms: NAAT followed by toxin EIA on NAAT-positive results, or GDH (glutamate dehydrogenase common-antigen) plus toxin EIA with NAAT as arbiter when the two disagree; confirming actual toxin in stool is favoured to avoid over-diagnosing colonisation.
Empirical treatment is appropriate if stool testing is delayed.
Endoscopy (sigmoidoscopy/colonoscopy) is highly specific only if pseudomembranes are seen but is insensitive (pseudomembranes visible in only ~50% of culture/toxin-positive diarrhoea); a negative endoscopy does NOT exclude CDI. It is most useful as a rapid test in the seriously ill patient with an acute abdomen and no diarrhoea.
Abdominal CT in colitis shows bowel-wall thickening (significantly more prominent than in other colitides), wall oedema, mesenteric stranding and ascites.
# Red flags
Unexplained leukocytosis with WBC >=15,000/uL, especially when stool passage has ceased due to adynamic ileus (ileus seen on x-ray in ~20%) - a clue to unsuspected CDI and a marker of high risk for toxic megacolon and sepsis.
Marked leukocytosis >=20,000 WBCs/uL with an acute abdomen suggests fulminant CDI.
Fulminant disease may present WITHOUT diarrhoea, mimicking an acute surgical abdomen, with sepsis (hypotension, fever, tachycardia, leukocytosis), obstruction/ileus, colon-wall thickening and ascites - include CDI in the differential of acute abdomen, sepsis or toxic megacolon in anyone given antibiotics in the prior 2 months.
Toxic megacolon, colonic perforation, shock and the need for colectomy denote fulminant/complicated disease; a rising serum lactate (aim to operate before it reaches 5 mmol/L) signals the need for surgery.
Even a single recurrence carries an ~11% risk of serious complications (shock, megacolon, perforation, colectomy or death within 30 days).
# Pitfalls
Do not perform a 'test of cure' after treatment: >50% of patients keep harbouring the organism and its toxin after diarrhoea resolves, and results do not predict recurrence; such results must not be used to restrict long-term-care or nursing-home placement.
Distinguish colonisation from infection - a positive NAAT/PCR in a patient with diarrhoea from another cause may simply reflect colonisation; treating asymptomatic carriers is not indicated.
In adynamic ileus the diagnosis is frequently overlooked because stool passage stops - look for unexplained leukocytosis.
A negative endoscopy does not rule out CDI, and pseudomembranes are absent in roughly half of confirmed cases.
Antiperistaltic agents and opiates may mask symptoms and possibly worsen disease and should generally be avoided.
# Deescalation
Not typically applicable in the de-escalation sense since therapy is already targeted; reassess for fulminant disease if not improving, which would escalate rather than narrow therapy.
# Completeness
Either agent provides effective coverage of the causative organism when given correctly (oral route, adequate duration).

# Framework
Discontinue the inciting antibiotic if it is still being given and is not essential — this alone can resolve milder cases.
Avoid antimotility agents (e.g., loperamide), which can precipitate toxic megacolon.
For fulminant disease (ileus, megacolon, shock), oral vancomycin should be combined with IV metronidazole and surgical consultation — this is the one scenario where IV metronidazole plays a role alongside oral vancomycin.
This is a uniquely 'reverse stewardship' scenario: the treatment is itself an antibiotic, so the focus is on correct route/agent selection (oral, not IV vancomycin) rather than withholding therapy.
