# Cardiovascular Collapse, Cardiac Arrest, and Sudden Cardiac Death
Cardiovascular Collapse, Cardiac Arrest, and Sudden Cardiac Death
Aliases: Sudden cardiac death; SCD; Sudden cardiac arrest; SCA; Cardiovascular collapse.
# Pathophysiology
Most sudden cardiac death stems from a lethal ventricular tachyarrhythmia (polymorphic VT degenerating to ventricular fibrillation) arising on a vulnerable myocardial substrate, though bradyarrhythmia, asystole, and pulseless electrical activity are increasingly common terminal rhythms. Coronary artery disease is the predominant substrate, acting through acute ischemia/infarction or through reentry around scar from a healed infarct. Nonischemic processes - dilated, hypertrophic, infiltrative, and arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathies, valvular disease, congenital surgical scars, and myocarditis - produce replacement fibrosis, hypertrophy, ventricular stretch, electrical heterogeneity, altered ion-channel function, and abnormal calcium handling that lower the threshold for sustained arrhythmia. In structurally normal hearts, inherited channelopathies (long QT, Brugada, CPVT), pre-excited atrial fibrillation in Wolff-Parkinson-White, and QT-prolonging drug toxicity cause primary electrical arrest. Pulseless electrical activity reflects mechanical or metabolic catastrophe - massive MI, pulmonary embolism, exsanguination, tamponade, tension pneumothorax, profound hypoxia or acidosis. Heightened autonomic (sympathetic) tone is the common thread linking precipitants such as vigorous exertion, emotional and environmental stress to arrhythmogenesis. After return of circulation, global ischemia-reperfusion brain injury becomes the dominant determinant of outcome.
# Prognosis
Outcomes are dominated by time to resuscitation and the presenting rhythm. Survival to hospital discharge for out-of-hospital arrest has improved but remains low - the chapter states about 90% of patients do not survive to discharge, and roughly 20% of survivors are left with severe neurologic or physical disability. Shockable rhythms (VT/VF) carry the best prognosis, with survival approaching 30% in some regions, whereas PEA and asystole carry poor outcomes. Success of resuscitation declines sharply after 5 minutes from collapse. Ischemia-reperfusion brain injury accounts for over two-thirds of post-arrest deaths; reliable neurologic prognostication is generally deferred at least 72 hours after return to normothermia (typically 4-5 days post-arrest). Features predicting poor neurologic outcome include absent pupillary light reflex, status myoclonus, absent EEG reactivity, and persistent burst suppression.
# Severity classification
Acute risk and outcome stratify by presenting rhythm - shockable (VT/VF, better) versus non-shockable (PEA/asystole, worse). Chronic SCD risk in structural heart disease is graded chiefly by LVEF and presence of heart failure, with risk rising gradually as LVEF falls to 40% and exponentially below it.
# Clinical pearls
Sudden cardiac death accounts for about 15% of all deaths and roughly half of all cardiac deaths; in the United States the text cites an estimated 350,000 EMS-attended out-of-hospital arrests and 210,000 sudden cardiac deaths in adults yearly.
