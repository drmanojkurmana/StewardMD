# Electrical Storm and Incessant Ventricular Tachycardia
Electrical Storm and Incessant Ventricular Tachycardia, Cardiovascular.
Aliases: VT storm; Ventricular tachycardia storm; Incessant VT; Arrhythmic storm.
# Pathophysiology
Electrical storm reflects repeated, often rapidly recurring ventricular tachyarrhythmias arising from an unstable myocardial substrate combined with heightened sympathetic drive and acute triggering factors. Substrates vary: in the setting of acute myocardial ischemia/infarction, surviving Purkinje cells within infarcted myocardium can show triggered automaticity, generating recurrent polymorphic VT/VF. PVC-initiated polymorphic VT/VF can also occur without infarction or ischemia (idiopathic VF), where triggering premature ventricular contractions arise from fascicular tissue or papillary muscles, sometimes from scarred myocardium seen on cardiac MRI. Other substrates include acquired or congenital long QT (torsades des pointes from abnormal repolarization), Brugada syndrome (right ventricular outflow tract/epicardial substrate), and inflammatory cardiomyopathies. Incessant VT is usually monomorphic and may be driven or sustained by the proarrhythmic action of antiarrhythmic drugs.
# Prognosis
Spontaneous ventricular arrhythmias—particularly those requiring shock for termination—are associated with an increased subsequent risk of death and hospitalization in patients with depressed ventricular function. Untreated storm progresses rapidly and can be fatal, but catheter ablation, antiarrhythmic drug therapy, and adjunctive measures can be life-saving. An arrhythmic episode should prompt reevaluation for declining cardiac function, ischemia, or intercurrent illness.
# Severity classification
Defined event: electrical storm = >=3 episodes of VT or VF within 24 h each requiring intervention to terminate. VT is classified as monomorphic versus polymorphic, and as sustained versus incessant; substrate-based categories include ischemic, long QT, Brugada, PVC-initiated, and inflammatory.
# Clinical pearls
Electrical storm (VT/VF storm) is defined as three or more episodes of ventricular tachycardia or ventricular fibrillation within 24 hours, each requiring intervention to terminate.
It is uncommon in the general population but carries great concern because, untreated, it follows a rapid course that can lead to death; prompt recognition and intervention are essential.
Storm occurs in about 4% of patients with a primary-prevention ICD but in as many as 20% of patients with prior known VT or resuscitated sudden death.
VT is termed incessant when it recurs shortly after electrical, pharmacologic, or spontaneous conversion to sinus rhythm; it is typically monomorphic.
A slow incessant monomorphic VT may rarely escape ICD detection because its rate falls below the programmed detection threshold.
Antiarrhythmic drugs themselves can be proarrhythmic and render VT incessant (e.g., amiodarone or a sodium-channel blocker such as flecainide).
Multiple ICD shocks constitute a medical emergency warranting activation of emergency medical services; patients should never drive themselves to hospital after receiving a shock.
After any ICD shock, device interrogation is crucial to confirm therapy was delivered for a true ventricular arrhythmia rather than for lead malfunction or an atrial arrhythmia.
Antitachycardia pacing terminates over 70% of VT episodes—even rapid VT—so appropriate ICD programming markedly reduces shocks and improves quality of life.
Programming VF detection zones above 220 beats/min in primary-prevention ICDs and using longer detection times significantly reduce unnecessary and inappropriate shocks.
Determining whether the storming rhythm is monomorphic or polymorphic guides management, as does inspecting initiation/termination patterns on stored ICD electrograms for precipitating factors.
Reducing sympathetic drive is a core management principle: stellate ganglion block and upper thoracic epidural anesthesia can restore stability in some patients.
# Additional differentials
Acute coronary syndrome / myocardial ischemia precipitating polymorphic VT or VF
Acquired or congenital long QT syndrome with torsades des pointes
Brugada syndrome
Inflammatory cardiomyopathy (giant cell myocarditis, cardiac sarcoidosis, viral myocarditis)
Idiopathic PVC-initiated polymorphic VT/VF from fascicular tissue or papillary muscles
Proarrhythmia from antiarrhythmic therapy
Infection mimics: Viral myocarditis presenting with VT/VF storm.
# Non infectious mimics
Lead malfunction or atrial arrhythmia causing inappropriate ICD shocks rather than true ventricular arrhythmia
Electrolyte disturbance provoking ventricular arrhythmia
# Additional investigations
ICD interrogation to confirm appropriate ventricular detection/therapy and to review stored electrograms for arrhythmia onset and triggers
ECG monitoring to classify the rhythm as monomorphic versus polymorphic and to assess the QT interval and Brugada pattern (Rsr' with ST elevation in V1/V2)
Serum electrolyte assessment, especially potassium and magnesium
Evaluation for ischemia / acute coronary syndrome and cardiac function
Cardiac MRI to detect scarred myocardium when PVC-initiated polymorphic VT/VF is suspected
Endomyocardial biopsy when a new inflammatory cardiomyopathy is suspected in a patient without prior cardiac disease
# Red flags
Multiple ICD shocks (a medical emergency)
Polymorphic VT or VF, especially if accompanied by features of acute coronary syndrome
Hemodynamically unstable or recurrent ventricular arrhythmia requiring repeated cardioversion
Signs of gradual cardiac decompensation in a hemodynamically stable but incessant VT
New-onset frequent ventricular arrhythmia in a patient with no known prior cardiac disease (consider inflammatory myocarditis)
# Pitfalls
Assuming every ICD shock reflects a true ventricular arrhythmia—lead malfunction or an atrial arrhythmia can trigger inappropriate shocks, so interrogation is required.
Overlooking that interventions to control the arrhythmia may themselves worsen outcome—most antiarrhythmics can cause bradycardia requiring ICD pacing, which can itself impair ventricular function.
Failing to recognize antiarrhythmic proarrhythmia as a cause of incessant VT.
Missing a slow incessant monomorphic VT that falls outside programmed ICD detection parameters.
Not searching for a treatable substrate (ischemia, long QT, Brugada, inflammatory cardiomyopathy) that dictates specific therapy.
# Management
Immediate identification and correction of reversible triggers, including myocardial ischaemia, electrolyte disturbance with potassium and magnesium repletion, and withdrawal of proarrhythmic drugs
Intravenous antiarrhythmic therapy, chiefly amiodarone, combined with beta-blockade including non-selective agents to counter the heightened sympathetic drive
Sympathetic modulation with sedation, and for refractory storm deep sedation with intubation and stellate ganglion block or cardiac sympathetic denervation
ICD interrogation and reprogramming to confirm appropriate detection and therapy and to reduce ineffective or inappropriate shocks
Catheter ablation of the arrhythmogenic substrate or the triggering premature ventricular contractions in drug-refractory storm
Substrate-specific measures, including isoprenaline or quinidine for Brugada storm, magnesium and avoidance of QT-prolonging drugs in torsades de pointes, and revascularisation for an ischaemic substrate
Temporary mechanical circulatory support such as an intra-aortic balloon pump or ECMO as a bridge in haemodynamically unstable refractory storm
Escalation to a specialist electrophysiology and heart-failure centre, with consideration of transplantation in end-stage disease
