# Gonadotroph (clinically non-functioning) pituitary adenoma
Gonadotroph (clinically non-functioning) pituitary adenoma, Endocrine, Endocrinology.
Aliases: non-functioning pituitary adenoma; NFPA; gonadotroph adenoma; clinically silent gonadotroph tumour.
# Pathophysiology
This is a benign monoclonal tumour arising from pituitary gonadotroph cells. Although the cells often express FSH, LH or the glycoprotein alpha-subunit on immunostaining, they generally secrete these hormones inefficiently and without a recognisable clinical syndrome, hence the label clinically non-functioning. The clinical consequences instead arise from progressive tumour growth: compression of the optic chiasm causes visual field loss, distortion of the pituitary stalk interrupts dopaminergic inhibition and mildly raises prolactin, and destruction or compression of the normal anterior pituitary produces variable hypopituitarism. Sudden haemorrhagic or ischaemic change within the tumour causes pituitary apoplexy.
# Prognosis
Surgical decompression usually improves visual fields and stabilises the tumour, and many patients do well long term, though hypopituitarism often persists and requires hormone replacement. Residual or recurrent tumour is common enough to warrant lifelong imaging and endocrine surveillance, with radiotherapy considered for progressive remnants.
# Clinical pearls
The most common type of clinically non-functioning pituitary macroadenoma, usually of gonadotroph lineage, which typically presents through mass effect rather than a hormone excess syndrome
Although immunopositive for gonadotropins or their subunits, secretion is usually inefficient and clinically silent, so patients rarely have symptomatic FSH or LH excess
Presentation is often with headache, visual field defects (classically bitemporal hemianopia) and features of hypopituitarism from compression of the normal gland
Stalk compression can raise prolactin modestly, but this stalk-effect hyperprolactinaemia must be distinguished from a true prolactinoma
Many are found incidentally on imaging performed for unrelated reasons (pituitary incidentaloma)
Because they are not hormonally driven, medical therapy is of limited benefit and transsphenoidal surgery is the mainstay for symptomatic or growing tumours
Pituitary apoplexy from haemorrhage or infarction can be the dramatic first presentation of a previously unrecognised adenoma
# Additional differentials
Prolactinoma (true prolactin-secreting adenoma)
Craniopharyngioma
Meningioma of the sellar or suprasellar region
Rathke cleft cyst
Hypophysitis
Metastatic deposit or other sellar mass
Pituitary hyperplasia (e.g. from primary hypothyroidism)
# Additional investigations
Pituitary MRI to define tumour size, suprasellar extension and chiasmal or cavernous sinus involvement
Full anterior pituitary hormone profile to detect hypopituitarism
Serum prolactin, interpreted in the context of possible stalk effect
Measurement of FSH, LH and alpha-subunit to characterise gonadotroph output
Formal visual field testing (perimetry) when the tumour approaches the optic chiasm
Assessment of secondary adrenal and thyroid axes before any surgery
# Red flags
Progressive or acute visual field loss from chiasmal compression
Pituitary apoplexy with sudden severe headache, ophthalmoplegia and collapse
Secondary adrenal insufficiency requiring urgent glucocorticoid cover
Rapid tumour enlargement or invasion on serial imaging
# Pitfalls
Mistaking stalk-effect hyperprolactinaemia for a prolactinoma and treating with dopamine agonists instead of operating
Failing to diagnose and replace secondary adrenal insufficiency before surgery or stress
