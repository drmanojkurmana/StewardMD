# Urticaria, Angioedema & Allergic Rhinitis
Urticaria, Angioedema & Allergic Rhinitis, Immunology / Allergy.
Aliases: hereditary angioedema; allergic rhinitis.
# Pathophysiology
Atopy is a tendency to manifest asthma, rhinitis, urticaria, food allergy, and atopic dermatitis in association with allergen-specific IgE, though urticaria and anaphylaxis can also occur in non-atopic individuals. Mast cells are the key effector cells: IgE binds the high-affinity receptor FcepsilonRI (sensitization), and antigen-driven receptor aggregation triggers Lyn/Syk/Fyn tyrosine-kinase signaling, calcium influx, and degranulation. This releases preformed granule mediators (histamine, tryptase, chymase, carboxypeptidase A, proteoglycans), newly generated lipid mediators (PGD2 via cyclooxygenase; LTB4 and cysteinyl leukotrienes LTC4/D4/E4 via 5-lipoxygenase/FLAP; PAF), and cytokines/chemokines (TNF-alpha, IL-4, IL-5, IL-13, GM-CSF). Mast cells can also be activated IgE-independently via C5a/C3a anaphylatoxin receptors, TLRs, alarmin (TSLP, IL-33) receptors, and MRGPRX2 (a target of small-molecule drugs such as ciprofloxacin and rocuronium and of neuropeptides). Histamine and lipid mediators increase venular permeability producing dermal edema (urticaria) or deeper subcutaneous/dermal edema (angioedema). Cold urticaria provides the best evidence for IgE/mast-cell involvement, with documented mast cell degranulation and elevated lesional histamine. In contrast, bradykinin-mediated angioedema (HAE from C1INH deficiency, or ACE inhibitor-induced) results from excess bradykinin: C1INH normally inhibits activated factor XII, kallikrein, and C1r/C1s, and its deficiency leads to bradykinin accumulation with falling C4/C2. Allergic rhinitis arises from sensitized intraepithelial and perivenular nasal mast cells that, on allergen exposure, release histamine, PGD2, and leukotrienes, producing submucosal edema and eosinophilic infiltration.
# Prognosis
More than two-thirds of new-onset urticaria cases are ultimately acute and self-limited. Chronic spontaneous urticaria is most common in the third to fifth decades and the vast majority shows no laboratory abnormality. Dermatographism generally has a duration of <5 years. Allergic rhinitis prevalence in North America is 10-30% (peak >30% in the fifth decade). Allergen immunotherapy (SCIT/SLIT) for allergic rhinitis provides durable symptom benefit and can reduce asthma symptoms; guidelines recommend 3-5 years of SCIT with discontinuation after minimal symptoms over two consecutive allergen seasons.
# Severity classification
Urticaria/angioedema classified by duration: acute = episodes lasting <6 weeks; chronic = attacks persisting >6 weeks (Table 363-1 further classifies by precipitating factor into acute vs chronic categories, including drug, food, infection, physical/inducible, autoimmune, vasculitic, mastocytosis, and hereditary forms). Hereditary angioedema typed as: type 1 (low C1INH antigen, ~85%), type 2 (dysfunctional C1INH protein), and type 3 (normal C1INH/complement, often identifiable gene mutation e.g. factor XII). Allergic rhinitis classified as seasonal vs perennial, and managed by severity (mild vs moderate-to-severe).
# Clinical pearls
Urticaria (hives) are well-circumscribed wheals (dilation of superficial dermal vessels) that are intensely pruritic, often migrate, individually last <24 h, and resolve without bruising or scarring; angioedema arises from deeper dermis/subcutaneous tissue with more pain than pruritus and minimal erythema, taking hours to days to resolve.
Urticaria and angioedema reflect similar pathophysiology at different skin levels and often co-occur, with roughly 40% of patients reporting both; together they affect >20% of the population at some point in life.
Acute urticaria/angioedema are episodes lasting <6 weeks (usually food, environmental, or drug allergen, or viral infection); attacks persisting >6 weeks are termed chronic and are often spontaneous/idiopathic with no identifiable cause. More than two-thirds of new-onset cases are ultimately diagnosed as acute.
Inducible (physical) urticarias are identified by the precipitating stimulus: dermatographism (linear wheal after brisk stroke, affects 2-5%), cholinergic urticaria (small 1-2 mm pruritic wheals with large surrounding erythema triggered by a rise in core body temperature such as fever, hot bath, or exercise), cold, solar, pressure, vibratory, heat, aquagenic, and contact urticaria; most inducible-urticaria lesions individually last <2 h.
Hereditary angioedema (HAE) is a fully penetrant autosomal dominant disorder from SERPING1 mutation causing C1 inhibitor (C1INH) deficiency (type 1, ~85%) or dysfunctional protein (type 2); presentation in childhood/adolescence and a family history of angioedema are distinguishing features.
HAE diagnosis is suggested by family history, early age of onset, lack of pruritus and urticarial lesions, recurrent gastrointestinal colic, episodes of laryngeal edema, and failure to respond to H1 antihistamines; laboratory confirmation shows low C1INH antigen (type 1) or nonfunctional protein (type 2), with chronically depleted C4 and C2 that fall further during attacks.
Cold urticaria can be confirmed by ice-cube placement on the volar forearm provoking a wheal within minutes; cryoglobulins or cold agglutinins are present in up to 5% of these patients, and immersion in cold water (swimming) can progress to vascular collapse.
Up to 45% of chronic urticaria has an autoimmune basis, including autoantibodies to IgE or to the alpha chain of FcepsilonRI, and may be associated with antithyroid antibodies (antimicrosomal peroxidase/thyroglobulin) in both Hashimoto's thyroiditis and a euthyroid state.
Allergic rhinitis is characterized by sneezing, rhinorrhea, nasal obstruction, lacrimation, and itching of the conjunctiva/nasal mucosa/oropharynx; the nasal mucosa is classically pale and boggy. It requires a temporal relationship to allergen exposure plus documented IgE sensitization.
