# Acute glomerulonephritis
Acute glomerulonephritis, Nephrology.
Red flags: Rapidly progressive GN needs urgent nephrology.
# Investigations
Urinalysis (dysmorphic RBCs, casts)
Renal function, complement
ASO / autoimmune serology
# Pathophysiology
GN reflects glomerular inflammation producing hypertension, volume expansion (edema, sometimes ascites), and an abnormal (nephritic) urinalysis; intense cellular proliferation reduces filtering surface area, causing oliguria/anuria, sodium and water retention, and concentrated low-sodium urine. Three mechanistic categories underlie RPGN: (1) ANCA-associated pauci-immune vasculitis (anti-proteinase-3/cANCA, anti-myeloperoxidase/pANCA) where autoantibodies recruit complement and drive inflammatory vasculitis of kidney and peripheral vessels; (2) anti-GBM disease, where circulating IgG directed at an epitope in the alpha-3 chain of the non-collagenous domain of type IV collagen binds in situ with C3; and (3) immune-complex disease with low circulating C3 from subendothelial deposits (post-infectious/PIGN, endocarditis, SLE, cryoglobulinemia, hepatitis B/C, syphilis). In PIGN, antibody-bacterial antigen complexes form ~10 days-3 weeks after pharyngeal or skin (impetigo) infection by nephritogenic group A strep, activate complement causing hypocomplementemia and subendothelial deposits, with later subepithelial 'humps'. Pathologically, RPGN shows parietal epithelial and inflammatory cell proliferation forming cellular crescents that compress capillaries; crescents progress to fibrosis and global glomerulosclerosis. IgA nephropathy results from mesangial deposition of C3 binding galactose-deficient IgA-1.
# Prognosis
Outcome depends on acuity vs chronicity: early reversible inflammatory/proliferative/necrotizing lesions can respond to prompt treatment, whereas sclerotic and atrophic biopsy findings indicate chronicity. Untreated RPGN capillaritis leads to complete, irreversible loss of kidney function; crescents that become fibrotic and atrophic cause global glomerulosclerosis, and when >50% of glomeruli are involved (diffuse) the process usually progresses to global glomerulosclerosis. Anti-GBM disease is usually a single acute fulminant event, while ANCA vasculitides may relapse (even subclinically) over time. IgA nephropathy can show rapid GFR decline to end-stage disease, especially in those of African ancestry with high-risk APOL1 alleles. Early diagnosis is critical.
# Severity classification
RPGN crescent burden on biopsy stratifies severity: focal and segmental necrotizing GN when <50% of glomeruli are acutely and only segmentally involved (better preserved), versus diffuse involvement when >50% of glomeruli are affected, which usually progresses to global glomerulosclerosis. Healed focal segmental lesions yield secondary FSGS, distinct from primary podocytopathic FSGS. RPGN is mechanistically triaged into three serologic categories: ANCA-associated (pauci-immune), anti-GBM, and low-C3 immune-complex disease.
# Clinical pearls
The nephritic urinalysis is the foundation of diagnosis: dysmorphic RBCs, RBC casts, pyuria, and proteinuria; always examine a freshly spun sediment to confirm active GN.
Dysmorphic ('acanthocyte') RBCs on phase-contrast microscopy point to a glomerular bleeding source; RBC casts are highly specific but very insensitive for GN, so their absence does not exclude glomerular disease.
The triad of dysmorphic RBCs, RBC casts, and proteinuria >500 mg/day is essentially diagnostic of glomerulonephritis and warrants serologic workup plus biopsy even when creatinine is still normal.
RPGN is a clinical diagnosis: any nephritic picture with rapidly rising creatinine should be treated as crescentic GN until proven otherwise.
At least three immunologic studies (ANCA, anti-GBM, complement) should be sent early to triage the three RPGN categories before immunosuppression.
Gross/cola-coloured glomerular hematuria with concurrent pharyngitis (synpharyngitic, day 1-3) suggests IgA nephropathy rather than post-streptococcal GN, which classically follows infection by 10 days to 3 weeks.
Both PIGN and IgA nephropathy deposit C3 in the glomerulus, but only PIGN lowers serum complement; a normal serum C3 with mesangial IgA favours IgA nephropathy/IgA vasculitis.
Infection-associated GN occurring during an ongoing infection (e.g., MRSA abscess, osteomyelitis, empyema) is characteristically associated with glomerular IgA deposition and differs from classic post-infectious GN.
# Additional differentials
ANCA-associated (pauci-immune) vasculitis — microscopic polyangiitis, granulomatosis with polyangiitis (formerly Wegener), eosinophilic GPA/Churg-Strauss
Anti-GBM disease / Goodpasture disease (anti-GBM with pulmonary hemorrhage)
Post-infectious / post-streptococcal GN (low C3)
IgA nephropathy and IgA vasculitis / Henoch-Schonlein purpura (normal C3)
Lupus nephritis (SLE)
Cryoglobulinemic GN (low C4, high rheumatoid factor; hepatitis C or paraproteinemia)
Infection-associated GN of endocarditis, hepatitis B/C, syphilis
Hereditary nephritis and thin basement membrane disease (isolated glomerular hematuria)
# Infection mimics
Subacute/acute bacterial endocarditis with immune-complex GN and low C3
Hepatitis B and C-associated GN
Syphilis-associated GN, usually with vasculitis
Staphylococcal (often MRSA) deep infections — abscess, osteomyelitis, empyema — causing infection-associated IgA-dominant GN
Schistosomiasis-associated glomerular disease
# Non infectious mimics
Renal microvasculature disease — hemolytic-uremic syndrome and thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (MAHA with schistocytes, high LDH, low haptoglobin, thrombocytopenia, end-organ injury)
Malignant hypertension
Renal vein thrombosis (heavy proteinuria and hematuria) and renal artery thrombosis
Isolated hematuria from stones, neoplasm, tuberculosis, trauma, prostatitis (postrenal/non-glomerular)
# Additional investigations
Phase-contrast microscopy of spun sediment for dysmorphic RBCs/acanthocytes and RBC casts
Serum complement C3 (and C4 for cryoglobulinemia) to separate low-complement immune-complex GN from normal-complement causes
ANCA panel — anti-proteinase-3 (cANCA) and anti-myeloperoxidase (pANCA)
Anti-GBM antibody
ASO / streptozyme / anti-DNase B titres for recent streptococcal infection
Blood cultures (rule out endocarditis), hepatitis B/C serology, HIV, VDRL, cryoglobulins
Quantify proteinuria — spot albumin-to-creatinine or protein-to-creatinine ratio, or 24-h collection
Renal biopsy — indicated when RBC casts present; defines pattern, prognosis, and treatment
# Red flags
Rapidly rising creatinine with a nephritic sediment = crescentic RPGN, a nephrology emergency requiring urgent immunology and biopsy
Hemoptysis, pulmonary infiltrates, or alveolar hemorrhage with renal failure = pulmonary-renal syndrome (anti-GBM/Goodpasture, ANCA vasculitis, SLE, cryoglobulinemia) — potentially fatal pulmonary hemorrhage
Upper-airway symptoms (sinusitis, nasal congestion, epistaxis) with GN suggest ANCA-associated vasculitis
Anuria in acute GN indicates severe RPGN among the catastrophic anuric causes
Anti-GBM disease typically presents as a single fulminant episode demanding rapid diagnosis and prompt plasma exchange to preserve kidney function
# Pitfalls
Relying on dipstick alone: it detects only albumin and can be falsely positive at pH >7 or with concentrated/blood-contaminated urine, and falsely negative in dilute urine or with non-albumin (light-chain) proteinuria.
False-positive dipstick hematuria when myoglobinuria/rhabdomyolysis is present with no RBCs on microscopy.
Assuming absence of RBC casts excludes GN — casts are highly specific but very insensitive.
Mislabeling synpharyngitic gross hematuria as post-streptococcal GN; it is usually IgA nephropathy and PIGN instead follows infection by 10 days-3 weeks.
Expecting low serum complement in IgA nephropathy/IgA vasculitis — serum C3 is normal despite glomerular C3 deposition.
Withholding biopsy in azotemia-free patients with the diagnostic nephritic triad — they still warrant serologic workup and biopsy.
Both anti-GBM and ANCA can be co-positive with pauci-immune pathology; such cases are managed as ANCA disease.
# Acute glomerulonephritis: treatment
Haematuria (dysmorphic RBCs/casts), proteinuria, hypertension ± AKI. Rapidly progressive GN is an emergency, Admit; nephrology.
RPGN — high-dose corticosteroid + cytotoxic/biologic induction (specialist-directed)
# Steps
Assess for rapidly progressive GN (rising creatinine) — nephrology emergency; urgent biopsy.
Send immunology (ANCA, anti-GBM, complement, ANA) before immunosuppression.
RPGN: high-dose corticosteroids + cyclophosphamide/rituximab ± plasma exchange (anti-GBM/severe).
Supportive: BP control (ACEi/ARB), manage fluid/electrolytes, treat any infection trigger.
Treat the specific cause; monitor renal function closely.
