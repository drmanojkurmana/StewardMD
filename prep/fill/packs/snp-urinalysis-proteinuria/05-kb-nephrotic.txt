# Nephrotic syndrome
Nephrotic syndrome, Nephrology.
# Red flags
Thrombosis & infection risk; consider underlying cause
# Investigations
Urine protein:creatinine (heavy)
Serum albumin, lipids
Renal biopsy if indicated
# Pathophysiology
Nephrotic syndrome reflects increased glomerular permeability to plasma proteins. Minimal change disease causes diffuse fusion (effacement) of podocyte foot processes producing predominantly 'selective' albumin loss, whereas immune-complex disruption of the basement membrane and slit diaphragms yields 'nonselective' loss of albumin and larger proteins. Hypoalbuminaemia results from urinary loss plus increased proximal-tubule catabolism of filtered albumin, exceeding hepatic synthesis (a hypercatabolic, negative-nitrogen-balance state). Oedema arises from renal sodium retention and reduced plasma oncotic pressure; perceived reduction in effective intravascular volume activates the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, AVP and the sympathetic nervous system, and filtered proteases can directly activate the epithelial Na channel (ENaC) in principal cells, promoting further salt/water retention. Hyperlipidaemia/hypercholesterolaemia reflect increased hepatic lipoprotein synthesis. Notably, hypertension is uncommon in primary kidney diseases causing nephrotic syndrome. Hereditary nephrotic syndrome can result from podocyte slit-diaphragm gene mutations (nephrin NPHS1, podocin NPHS2); APOL1 risk variants predispose to FSGS, with disease expression triggered by a 'second hit' of infection/inflammation damaging podocytes and glomerular endothelium.
# Prognosis
Glomerular proteinuria in diabetes typically appears after years of small-vessel disease (hence frequent coexistent retinopathy) and predicts progression to nephrotic syndrome and renal failure; the rate of progression is faster with hypertension, obesity and poor glucose control. Diffuse glomerular involvement (most glomeruli) makes nephrotic syndrome likely, whereas focal disease (<50% of glomeruli) tends to give subnephrotic albuminuria. In membranous nephropathy, ~70% of primary cases are anti-PLA2R associated. Collapsing glomerulopathy (HIVAN) is a severe, treatment-resistant FSGS variant. Quartan malarial nephropathy responds poorly to therapy.
# Severity classification
Albuminuria categories (Ch.55 Fig.55-3): moderately increased 30-300 mg/d or mg/g (formerly microalbuminuria); severely increased 300-3500 mg/d or mg/g (formerly macroalbuminuria); nephrotic range >3500 mg/d or mg/g. Full nephrotic syndrome defined by the triad of oedema, hypoalbuminaemia (<3.5 g/dL) and proteinuria >3.5 g/d, usually with lipid abnormalities (high LDL, low HDL, lipiduria).
# Clinical pearls
Heavy proteinuria (>3.5 g/d) can occur WITHOUT the full nephrotic picture (no hypoalbuminaemia/oedema/hyperlipidaemia) in several renal diseases, notably diabetes; proteinuria is not synonymous with nephrotic syndrome.
Periorbital, facial and eyelid swelling (worst on waking) helps distinguish nephrotic oedema from the dependent oedema/orthopnoea of heart failure; penile and scrotal oedema may be severe enough to obstruct urinary flow in men.
Severe oedema can produce skin bullae that rupture and predispose to ulceration and cellulitis; vocal cord oedema may cause hoarseness.
Urine microscopy may show oval fat bodies (lipid-laden tubular epithelial cells), cholesterol monohydrate Maltese-cross crystals under polarised light, and fatty casts.
Rapid-onset hypoalbuminaemia may produce horizontal white nail-bed lines (Muehrcke's lines).
Abrupt adult-onset nephrotic syndrome with markedly elevated cholesterol suggests a podocytopathy such as minimal change disease, which is often preceded by upper respiratory infection, allergy or immunisation.
Dipstick detects predominantly albumin and the most acidic proteins; it misses kappa/lambda light chains (myeloma), so a normal dipstick does not exclude overflow proteinuria — send urine protein electrophoresis/immunoelectrophoresis.
Nephrotic-range proteinuria without oedema, with rapid renal function loss, raises HIV-associated nephropathy (collapsing glomerulopathy, a severe treatment-resistant FSGS variant), more common in patients of African ancestry.
# Additional differentials
Minimal change disease (idiopathic; secondary to Hodgkin's lymphoma or other lymphoproliferative disorders) — predominant cause in children <6 years
Primary FSGS / podocytopathy versus secondary FSGS (remnant kidney after partial nephrectomy, adaptive injury, chronic hypoxaemia, sickle cell disease, obesity, heroin, congenital urogenital anomalies)
Membranous nephropathy — ~70% of primary cases associated with anti-PLA2R antibodies; secondary forms with malignancy (GI, prostate, breast, lung, bladder), SLE, drugs (gold, penicillamine, NSAIDs), infections
Membranoproliferative / C3 glomerulopathy and dense-deposit disease (hypocomplementaemia); secondary to SLE, cryoglobulins, SBE
Diabetic glomerulosclerosis (subnephrotic albuminuria progressing to nephrotic range)
Amyloidosis (AL light-chain, AA/SAA from chronic inflammation)
Multiple myeloma / plasma cell dyscrasia with light-chain (overflow) proteinuria not detected by dipstick
# Infection mimics
HIV-associated nephropathy (collapsing FSGS)
Postinfectious glomerulonephritis and COVID-19-associated glomerular injury
Subacute bacterial endocarditis (immune-complex glomerulonephritis)
Hepatitis C-associated cryoglobulinaemic disease
Schistosomiasis
Quartan malarial nephropathy — chronic Plasmodium malariae immune-complex injury causing focal/segmental glomerulonephritis with capillary basement-membrane splitting, mainly in children, responds poorly to therapy (p.~1670 malaria chapter)
# Non infectious mimics
Heart failure oedema — dependent with orthopnoea, contrasts with periorbital/facial nephrotic oedema
Hepatic cirrhosis with hypoalbuminaemia and oedema/ascites
Severe malnutrition and protein-losing enteropathy as alternative hypoalbuminaemic states
# Additional investigations
Spot morning urine albumin-to-creatinine or protein-to-creatinine ratio (normalised to creatinine to discount urine dilution) or 24-h urine protein/albumin — preferred over dipstick for quantification
Serum and urine protein immunoelectrophoresis / UPEP to detect light chains when myeloma is possible (dipstick misses light chains)
Urine microscopy for oval fat bodies, Maltese-cross lipid crystals and fatty casts
Anti-PLA2R antibody testing in suspected primary membranous nephropathy
Complement levels (membranoproliferative/C3 glomerulopathy is hypocomplementaemic)
Imaging/angiography for suspected renal vein thrombosis presenting with heavy proteinuria and haematuria
# Red flags
Heavy proteinuria with new haematuria suggesting renal vein thrombosis
Rapidly declining renal function with very heavy proteinuria and no oedema suggesting collapsing glomerulopathy/HIVAN
Cellulitis or ulceration from ruptured oedema bullae
Hoarseness from vocal cord oedema or scrotal/penile oedema obstructing urinary flow
# Pitfalls
Assuming any nephrotic-range proteinuria equals classic nephrotic syndrome — heavy proteinuria can occur without hypoalbuminaemia/oedema.
Relying on dipstick alone — it misses light chains, so myeloma-associated overflow proteinuria can be overlooked; total protein:creatinine ratio is not specific for albumin.
Equating subnephrotic albuminuria with absence of significant glomerular disease — focal disease causes lower albuminuria yet can be significant.
Misreading biopsy-proven FSGS as a single entity — primary podocytopathic FSGS, secondary/adaptive FSGS, and post-GN healing scars are distinct.
# Confirm
Heavy proteinuria (>3.5 g/day), hypoalbuminaemia, oedema ± hyperlipidaemia. Identify primary vs secondary cause; biopsy in adults.
Nephrology referral.
RAAS blockade + diuresis + statin (with cause-specific immunosuppression)
# Steps
Quantify proteinuria; renal biopsy in adults to define the lesion (guides specific therapy).
Reduce proteinuria with ACEi/ARB; manage oedema with salt restriction + loop diuretics (cautious).
Statin for hyperlipidaemia; consider VTE prophylaxis (high thrombotic risk, esp. albumin <20-25 g/L).
Cause-specific immunosuppression (e.g. steroids for minimal change) per nephrology.
Treat secondary causes (diabetes, amyloid, drugs, infection); vaccination.
Thromboprophylaxis + cause-specific immunosuppression
ACEi/ARB + diuretics + statin; nephrology for biopsy-directed therapy
