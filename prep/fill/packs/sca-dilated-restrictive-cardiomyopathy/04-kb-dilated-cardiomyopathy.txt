# Dilated Cardiomyopathy
Dilated Cardiomyopathy, Cardiology.
Aliases: DCM.
# Pathophysiology
DCM is characterized by decreased LV systolic function, typically with LV chamber dilation (LVEF <=0.50, usually <=0.40 at presentation). Multiple causes (idiopathic, familial/genetic, prior myocarditis, ischaemic, toxic/alcohol, chemotherapy, peripartum, tachycardia-mediated, metabolic/endocrine) converge on a common phenotype resembling post-infarct injury: early myocyte death, later apoptosis, and compensatory hypertrophy of remaining myocytes in response to increased wall stress. Local and circulating neurohormonal factors drive deleterious secondary remodeling; dynamic remodeling of the interstitial scaffold affects diastolic function and the degree of dilation. As the ventricle dilates, the mitral valve apparatus is distorted, producing functional mitral regurgitation. RV dilation/dysfunction develops directly from injury or, more often, secondarily from pulmonary hypertension and ventricular interaction. Chronic inflammation contributes to progression even when not initially implicated, and many 'acute' presentations have progressed silently over months to years.
# Prognosis
DCM secondary responses are often modifiable or reversible. Almost a third of patients with recent-onset cardiomyopathy without coronary artery disease show substantial spontaneous recovery to normal ejection fraction, and partial recovery to LVEF >0.40 ('heart failure with improved ejection fraction') is common with guideline-directed neurohormonal therapy, CRT for LBBB, and diuretics. Alcoholic cardiomyopathy can improve markedly within 3-6 months of abstinence but has a grim prognosis if heavy drinking continues. In peripartum cardiomyopathy, LVEF improves to >=0.50 in 50-80% of cases (often within 6 months); recovery is less likely with LVEF <0.30, LV end-diastolic dimension >=0.60, black race, and presentation >6 weeks postpartum; U.S. 1-year mortality ranged 4-11% and up to twofold higher in Africa. Takotsubo syndrome has a generally good long-term prognosis (lowest mortality with emotional rather than physical triggers) but in-hospital complications/mortality resemble acute MI, with ~10% recurrence.
# Severity classification
Classification of cardiomyopathies: DCM phenotype - Early: LVEF <=0.50 and/or LVEDV >112% normal for age/sex or LVEDD >95% predicted for sex/height; LVEF <=0.40 is the threshold for traditional heart-failure therapies for low LVEF; persistent LVEF <=0.30-0.35 is the threshold for primary-prevention ICD. Mid-range LVEF 0.40-0.50 is often a transitional state (deterioration from early DCM or improvement into DCM remission). DCM is distinguished from HCM (increased wall thickness, septal >=15 mm men / >=13 mm women, LVEF >=normal usually >0.60), RCM (functional diagnosis from diastolic dysfunction/elevated filling pressures, often marked atrial enlargement), and arrhythmogenic CM (ACM-LV/ACM-RV/ARVC, ventricular arrhythmias dominating)..
# Clinical pearls
DCM is defined by decreased left ventricular systolic function, typically with increased LV dimensions, although dilation may be minimal in some cases.
Quantitatively, DCM is defined when LVEF is <=0.50, but most clinical presentations occur with LVEF <=0.40; persistent LVEF <=0.30-0.35 is the threshold for primary-prevention ICD.
Despite multiple etiologies and variable initial presentations, DCM often progresses into a convergent clinical phenotype similar to that following acute myocardial infarction, with myocyte death, apoptosis, compensatory hypertrophy, and neurohormonal activation.
Mitral regurgitation commonly develops as the ventricle dilates and the valvular apparatus is distorted, and is commonly moderate to severe by the time heart failure is advanced.
Right ventricular dilation and dysfunction may result directly from the initial injury but more often develop later from elevated afterload due to secondary pulmonary hypertension and mechanical interaction with the failing LV.
Alcohol is the most common toxin implicated in chronic DCM, but alcoholic cardiomyopathy is relatively rare and remains a diagnosis of exclusion; atrial fibrillation occurs commonly both early ('holiday heart') and in advanced stages.
Truncating variants in TTN and other DCM disease genes can be found in about 10% of patients with presumed alcohol cardiomyopathy and about 15% of peripartum cardiomyopathy, indicating that environmental triggers can unmask genetic predisposition.
Chemotherapy agents are the most common drugs implicated in toxic cardiomyopathy; doxorubicin cardiotoxicity generally does NOT cause marked LV dilation, so stroke volume and perfusion can be low with only a modest fall in ejection fraction.
Tests of thyroid function are part of the routine evaluation of cardiomyopathy because clinical signs of thyroid disease may be masked; hyperthyroidism should always be considered with new-onset atrial fibrillation or ventricular tachycardia.
Heritability is present in at least 30% of DCM without other clear etiology, and expanded genetic testing reveals pathogenic variants sufficient to cause cardiomyopathy in 20-40% of DCM patients.
# Additional differentials
Ischaemic cardiomyopathy / coronary artery disease or prior infarction
Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM), including the ~5% that evolve to reduced EF with restrictive physiology (HCM-rEF)
Restrictive cardiomyopathy (RCM) - functional diagnosis based on diastolic dysfunction/elevated filling pressures; some sarcomeric variants and storage diseases overlap
Arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy (ACM-LV / ACM-RV/ARVC) where ventricular arrhythmias dominate before severely reduced LVEF
Primary valvular disease
Takotsubo (stress-induced) cardiomyopathy - distinct acute presentation with apical ballooning, often resolving
Heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) - overlaps with proposed obesity and diabetic cardiomyopathy
# Infection mimics
Viral myocarditis (coxsackie, adenovirus, COVID-19, influenza, HIV) progressing to dilated cardiomyopathy
Chagas' disease (Trypanosoma cruzi) and other parasitic infection (toxoplasmosis, trypanosomiasis)
Bacterial (diphtheria), spirochetal (Lyme disease - Borrelia burgdorferi), rickettsial (Q fever), and fungal myocarditis
# Non infectious mimics
Granulomatous inflammatory disease - sarcoidosis (often regional wall-motion abnormality, DCM phenotype, or RV involvement) and giant cell myocarditis
Eosinophilic myocarditis / Loeffler endocarditis (can progress to restrictive physiology)
Collagen vascular / connective tissue disease, polymyositis, dermatomyositis, systemic lupus erythematosus
Immune checkpoint-inhibitor myocarditis (most dramatic noninfectious inflammatory myocarditis)
Hemochromatosis / iron overload (variably classified as metabolic or restrictive but often presents as DCM)
Endocrinopathy - thyroid disease, pheochromocytoma; very-high-renin states (renal artery stenosis)
Nutritional deficiency - thiamine (beriberi), selenium (Keshan's disease), carnitine, calcium, phosphate
# Additional investigations
Echocardiography (initial imaging modality to define morphology and function)
Cardiac MRI for myocardial tissue characterization, late gadolinium enhancement patterns of fibrosis, and T1/T2 mapping for focal/diffuse inflammation (note: avoid gadolinium in pregnancy)
Electrocardiogram and chest radiograph
Chemistry including sodium, potassium, calcium, magnesium, glucose/glycohemoglobin, renal and liver function, lipids, thyroid-stimulating hormone, serum iron and transferrin saturation, urinalysis, creatine kinase isoforms, cardiac troponin
Hematology including CBC with differential, eosinophil count, ESR
Genetic counseling and multigene cardiomyopathy panel
Targeted infectious work-up when suspected (HIV, Chagas', Lyme, toxoplasmosis, trichinosis); rheumatologic serologies
Endomyocardial biopsy (including electron microscopy) when a specific diagnosis with therapeutic implications is suspected
Coronary angiography in patients with evidence of ischemia/infarction who are candidates for intervention
# Red flags
Sudden cardiac death risk: ventricular arrhythmias may be presenting events; persistent LVEF <=0.30-0.35 is the threshold for primary-prevention ICD
Embolic events from atrial fibrillation or apical LV thrombi can be the first presentation
Peripartum cardiomyopathy with LVEF <0.35 carries higher risk of life-threatening arrhythmias at presentation and early after discharge, and marked dilation/LVEF <0.35 raises LV thrombus and embolic risk
Acute cardiac presentation (elevated troponin, conduction blocks, bizarre arrhythmias) in a patient on immune checkpoint inhibitors warrants immediate coronary-care-unit admission and high-dose glucocorticoids without delay for biopsy
Hyperthyroidism combined with heart failure is a dangerous combination that may decompensate precipitously and fatally during titration of antithyroid therapy
# Pitfalls
'Congestive heart failure' should NOT be considered a diagnosis or an etiology of cardiomyopathy - it is a nonspecific syndrome of congestion common to many cardiac diagnoses and requires thorough etiologic evaluation.
Phenotypes overlap and evolve over time (DCM can reverse-remodel to higher EF; HCM can evolve to reduced EF), so reliance on a single morphologic phenotype is diminishing.
Alcoholic cardiomyopathy is a diagnosis of exclusion, and many patients are fully functional without apparent stigmata of alcoholism.
Anthracycline cardiotoxicity can produce low stroke volume/perfusion with only modestly reduced EF because marked LV dilation is usually absent.
In peripartum cardiomyopathy, edema and dyspnea may be mistakenly attributed to pregnancy itself; renin-angiotensin system inhibitors should NOT be given before delivery due to adverse fetal effects, and gadolinium should NOT be used on MRI during pregnancy.
Detection of anticardiac antibodies and viral genome fragments in chronic cardiomyopathy is of uncertain significance (possible cause vs neutral bystander) and is not in routine clinical use.
Immunosuppression for nonspecific chronic lymphocytic myocarditis has not demonstrated convincing benefit.
# Management
Guideline-directed medical therapy for heart failure with a beta-blocker, an angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitor or ACE inhibitor, a mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist and an SGLT2 inhibitor
Identify and reverse modifiable causes, including alcohol cessation, rate control of tachycardia-mediated disease, treatment of thyroid or metabolic disorders and withdrawal of cardiotoxic agents
Diuretics for congestion and management of the functional mitral regurgitation that accompanies ventricular dilation
Device therapy with an implantable cardioverter defibrillator for primary prevention when the ejection fraction remains severely reduced, and cardiac resynchronisation for a broad QRS with dyssynchrony
Anticoagulation for atrial fibrillation or demonstrated intracardiac thrombus
Advanced therapies for refractory disease, including inotropes, mechanical circulatory support such as a left ventricular assist device and cardiac transplantation
Genetic counselling with cascade family screening and a multigene cardiomyopathy panel in familial disease
Serial echocardiographic monitoring to track remodelling and possible recovery of function
