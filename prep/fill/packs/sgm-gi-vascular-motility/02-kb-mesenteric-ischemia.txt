# Acute mesenteric ischemia
Acute mesenteric ischemia, Vascular / GI.
# Red flags
Pain out of proportion to exam — time-critical
# Investigations
CT angiography (mesenteric)
Lactate
Urgent surgical/vascular review
# Pathophysiology
Intestinal ischaemia occurs when splanchnic perfusion fails to meet the bowel's metabolic demand, producing ischaemic tissue injury. The gut is supplied by the celiac artery, SMA and IMA with extensive collateralisation between major trunks and mesenteric arcades; protective responses include this collateral network, autoregulation of flow and the ability to increase oxygen extraction. Watershed colonic zones with inherently lower flow are Griffiths' point (splenic flexure) and Sudeck's point (descending/sigmoid colon), the commonest sites of colonic ischaemia. The splanchnic bed can receive up to 30% of cardiac output. Occlusive ischaemia results from an embolus or progressive thrombosis in a major mesenteric artery (over 75% of emboli are cardiac in origin and lodge in the SMA distal to the middle colic artery). Chronic intestinal angina generally requires progressive stenosis of two of the three major vessels. Non-occlusive ischaemia is driven by disproportionate mesenteric arteriolar vasospasm in response to severe physiological stress such as shock; untreated, early mucosal stress ulceration progresses to full-thickness injury. Mesenteric venous thrombosis is usually precipitated by a hypercoagulable state.
# Prognosis
Acute mesenteric ischaemia carries a high mortality, roughly 50-80% at presentation (with the acute arterial form quoted at over 50%); it is one of the most challenging diagnoses, and the single most important determinant of survival is the timeliness of diagnosis and treatment. Endovascular stenting for chronic mesenteric ischaemia has about an 80% long-term success rate.
# Severity classification
Acute mesenteric vascular insufficiency is subdivided into (1) arterio-occlusive mesenteric ischaemia — further split into arterial embolus (~50%) and arterial thrombosis (~25-30%, often acute-on-chronic), (2) non-occlusive/vasospastic mesenteric ischaemia (~20%, vasospasm or hypoperfusion), and (3) mesenteric venous thrombosis (<10%). Ischaemic colitis mucosal injury is graded as mild (minimal erythema), moderate (pale ulceration extending into muscularis), or severe (black/green discoloration with full-thickness necrosis).
# Clinical pearls
Severe, acute, non-remitting abdominal pain that is strikingly out of proportion to physical findings is the most common complaint in arterial acute mesenteric ischaemia, present in about 95% of cases; pain precedes signs because ischaemia begins in the mucosa and progresses outward to the serosa.
About half of acute mesenteric ischaemia cases are embolic, lodging in the mid-to-distal SMA just past the middle colic artery origin; emboli commonly arise from atrial fibrillation, recent MI, soft atherosclerotic plaque, infective endocarditis, valvular heart disease, or recent cardiac/vascular catheterisation.
Acute-on-chronic thrombotic ischaemia (about 25-30% of cases) occurs at sites of severe atherosclerotic narrowing of the SMA or celiac artery, typically in patients with pre-existing mesenteric atherosclerosis who may have had prior chronic mesenteric ischaemia.
Embolic disease is suggested by older patients with a predisposing cardiac source (AF, prior embolic event, recent infective endocarditis), whereas thrombotic disease suggests underlying atherosclerosis with possible prior intestinal angina.
Chronic mesenteric ischaemia classically presents as recurrent dull, crampy, postprandial epigastric pain ('intestinal angina') with fear of eating and weight loss; chronic diarrhoea may occur and symptoms typically last 6-12 months before diagnosis.
Mesenteric venous thrombosis tends to present more vaguely and subacutely with non-specific abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, distension, mild-to-moderate tenderness and dehydration, making it easy to under-recognise.
The decision to operate in suspected acute mesenteric ischaemia is often based on a high index of suspicion from history and examination despite normal laboratory results.
# Additional differentials
Chronic mesenteric ischaemia / intestinal angina (postprandial pain, weight loss, fear of eating)
Ischaemic colitis (notably after elective or emergent aortic repair)
Gastrointestinal malignancy (must be excluded in chronic presentations)
Superior mesenteric artery syndrome (duodenal compression after weight loss/immobilisation)
Median arcuate ligament syndrome (celiac artery compression)
# Non infectious mimics
Acute intestinal obstruction (mechanical or strangulated bowel)
Other noninflammatory causes of abdominal pain such as biliary colic and neoplasia
Inflammatory abdominal conditions: pancreatitis, cholecystitis, appendicitis, diverticulitis, peptic ulcer, inflammatory bowel disease
# Additional investigations
Thin-cut (1 mm or thinner) CT angiography is the gold standard for detecting mesenteric arterial occlusive (embolic/thrombotic) disease and for assessing bowel; venous-phase imaging detects mesenteric venous thrombosis with diffuse bowel-wall thickening and splanchnic thrombus.
Mesenteric duplex ultrasound is a sensitive/specific screening tool; a high SMA peak flow velocity carries ~80% positive predictive value, and a negative duplex virtually excludes mesenteric ischaemia (test must be done fasting).
Leukocytosis, metabolic acidosis and lactic acidosis support advanced ischaemia but may be absent in reversible ischaemia and cannot reliably distinguish reversible ischaemia from frank necrosis.
ECG and echocardiogram may help identify an embolic cardiac source but must not delay surgical therapy.
Hypercoagulability workup in suspected mesenteric venous thrombosis (e.g. factor V Leiden, prothrombin mutation, protein C/S or antithrombin deficiency, antiphospholipid syndrome).
Colonoscopy if ischaemic colitis is suspected, to grade mucosal injury.
# Red flags
Pain out of proportion to examination with a high-risk cardiac source (AF, recent MI, endocarditis) — embolic acute mesenteric ischaemia until proven otherwise
Later signs of peritonitis and cardiovascular collapse indicate established transmural infarction
Blood per rectum, marked distension or obtundation in a critically ill/shocked patient (suggests non-occlusive ischaemia)
Rising lactate / metabolic acidosis is a late marker and its absence does not exclude ischaemia
# Pitfalls
Treating pain out of proportion to examination as benign — early ischaemia produces severe pain with deceptively unremarkable abdominal signs because injury starts in the mucosa.
Waiting for abnormal labs before acting — lactate and acidosis are late and may be normal in reversible ischaemia or even necrosis; the operative decision is often made on clinical suspicion despite normal results.
Overlooking non-occlusive ischaemia in obtunded/critically ill patients where examination findings are absent or obscured by the underlying illness.
Missing the vaguer, subacute presentation of mesenteric venous thrombosis (non-specific pain, nausea, distension).
Failing to perform mesenteric duplex while fasting — bowel gas obscures flow and the post-prandial vasodilatory response.
# Confirm
Severe abdominal pain OUT OF PROPORTION to exam, ± AF/vascular disease, rising lactate. CT angiography is key.
Emergency surgery + ICU.
Resuscitation + broad-spectrum antibiotics + anticoagulation (with urgent revascularisation)
# Steps
EMERGENCY — urgent CT angiography; do not delay for equivocal exam.
Resuscitate (fluids), broad-spectrum antibiotics, correct acidosis; NG decompression.
Urgent surgical/IR intervention — revascularisation (embolectomy/stent) and resect non-viable bowel.
Therapeutic anticoagulation for embolic/thrombotic arterial or venous occlusion.
ICU; high mortality — speed is critical.
Therapeutic anticoagulation
Emergency CT angiography, resuscitate, revascularise
