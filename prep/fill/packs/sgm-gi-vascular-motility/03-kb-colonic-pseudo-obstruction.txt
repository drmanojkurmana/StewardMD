# Acute colonic pseudo-obstruction
Acute colonic pseudo-obstruction, GI / Hepatology, Gastroenterology.
Aliases: Ogilvie; Ogilvie syndrome; ACPO; acute colonic pseudo-obstruction.
# Pathophysiology
Acute colonic pseudo-obstruction reflects a disturbance of the autonomic regulation of colonic motility, with a relative imbalance favouring reduced parasympathetic (excitatory) drive or excess sympathetic (inhibitory) tone in the distal colon. This produces a functional adynamic segment and proximal dilatation without any mechanical block. Precipitants include major surgery, severe systemic illness, sepsis, metabolic and electrolyte derangements, and drugs that impair gut motility. As the caecum distends, its wall tension rises (in keeping with the law of Laplace, tension increases with radius), compromising perfusion and risking ischaemic necrosis and perforation.
# Prognosis
Most patients recover with correction of precipitating factors and conservative or pharmacological decompression. Prognosis worsens substantially if ischaemia or perforation develops, which carries high mortality, so early recognition and monitoring of caecal size are critical.
# Clinical pearls
Massive dilatation of the colon, especially the caecum and right colon, in the absence of any mechanical obstructing lesion
Typically occurs in hospitalised or debilitated patients after surgery, severe illness, trauma, infection, or in the setting of electrolyte disturbance and certain medications
Presents with progressive abdominal distension, often with relatively little pain, and variable nausea, vomiting, and altered bowel output
A mechanical obstruction and toxic megacolon must be excluded, usually with abdominal imaging and often a water-soluble contrast study or CT
The main danger is progressive caecal dilatation leading to ischaemia and perforation, with risk rising sharply as caecal diameter increases and as distension persists
Initial management is conservative: correct electrolyte abnormalities, stop contributing drugs (especially opioids and anticholinergics), decompress with a nasogastric tube, and mobilise the patient
If conservative measures fail and there is no perforation, pharmacological decompression with a parasympathomimetic agent is effective, given under cardiac monitoring
Colonoscopic decompression or, as a last resort, surgery is reserved for refractory cases or when ischaemia or perforation is suspected
Additional differentials: Mechanical large-bowel obstruction (tumour, stricture, volvulus); Toxic megacolon from severe colitis; Sigmoid or caecal volvulus; Generalised paralytic ileus; Faecal impaction with distension; Chronic intestinal pseudo-obstruction.
# Additional investigations
Abdominal radiograph to document colonic and caecal diameter
CT of the abdomen or water-soluble contrast enema to exclude a mechanical cause and assess for ischaemia or perforation
Electrolyte panel including potassium, magnesium, calcium, and phosphate
Review of the medication chart for motility-impairing drugs
Serial clinical and radiological monitoring of caecal size and abdominal signs
# Red flags
Progressive caecal dilatation, especially beyond a critical diameter, indicating high risk of ischaemia and perforation
