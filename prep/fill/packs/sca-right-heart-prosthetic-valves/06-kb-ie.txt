# Infective Endocarditis
Infective Endocarditis, Cardiovascular.
# Pathogens
Pathogen likelihood depends heavily on the clinical context: native valve subacute disease points toward viridans streptococci, acute presentations and IV drug use point toward S. aureus, and prosthetic valve infection (especially early) points toward coagulase-negative staphylococci — empiric choice should be tailored to this context pending blood culture results.
# Very likely
Viridans group streptococci (native valve, subacute presentation)
Staphylococcus aureus (acute presentation, IV drug use)
# Likely
Enterococcus species (especially post-genitourinary procedures, elderly)
Coagulase-negative staphylococci (prosthetic valve, particularly early post-operative)
# Possible
HACEK organisms and other fastidious organisms (culture-negative endocarditis)
# Investigations
Blood cultures (≥3 sets from separate sites, before antibiotics)
The single most important diagnostic step — identifies the organism and is a major Duke criterion; false-negative cultures from premature antibiotics significantly complicate diagnosis and management.
Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE), followed by transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) if TTE inconclusive or high suspicion persists
Identifies vegetations, abscess, or valve destruction — a major Duke criterion and essential for surgical planning.
ECG
Screens for new conduction abnormalities (e.g., new heart block), which can indicate perivalvular abscess extension.
CBC, ESR/CRP, renal function, Supports overall assessment of inflammatory burden and any renal involvement (e.g., immune complex glomerulonephritis).
Antibiotics recommended

# Pathophysiology
The prototypic lesion is the vegetation - a mass of platelets, fibrin, microorganisms and scant inflammatory cells - usually on heart valves but also on the low-pressure side of a ventricular septal defect, on mural endocardium injured by aberrant blood jets or foreign bodies, or on intracardiac devices. Intact endothelium resists most bacteria; endothelial injury (e.g., at sites of high-velocity jet impact or on the low-pressure side of a structural lesion) permits direct infection by virulent organisms or formation of a sterile platelet-fibrin thrombus (nonbacterial thrombotic endocarditis, NBTE) that becomes a nidus for bacterial seeding during transient bacteremia. NBTE is most often associated with mitral regurgitation, aortic stenosis, aortic regurgitation, ventricular septal defects and complex congenital heart disease, and also arises from hypercoagulable states (marantic endocarditis of malignancy/chronic disease and the bland vegetations of SLE and antiphospholipid syndrome). Circulating organisms adhere via surface adhesins (MSCRAMMs) - fibronectin-binding proteins, S. aureus clumping factor, enterococcal Fss2/Ace/Ebp pili, streptococcal glucans/FimA; S. aureus can even invade intact endothelium via fibronectin-binding proteins and von Willebrand factor, allowing infection of previously normal valves. Adherent organisms proliferate into microcolonies, induce platelet deposition and a local procoagulant (tissue-factor) state, and become buried in fibrin; deep organisms are metabolically inactive and relatively antibiotic-resistant while surface organisms are continuously shed into the blood. Clinical manifestations arise from intracardiac structural damage, embolization of vegetation fragments causing remote infarction/infection, hematogenous seeding during bacteremia, and immune-complex deposition; constitutional symptoms reflect cytokine release.
# Prognosis
Overall survival in developed countries is about 80-85%, but varies markedly by subgroup. About 85-90% survive NVE caused by viridans streptococci, HACEK organisms, or synergy-susceptible enterococci. Non-injecting patients with S. aureus NVE have 55-70% survival; injecting patients have 85-90%, but 1-year mortality rises to 20-30% if substance-use disorder is not addressed. Prosthetic-valve IE arising within 2 months of surgery carries 40-50% mortality versus only 10-20% for late-onset PVE; elderly TAVR-PVE in-hospital mortality is 35-50%, rising to 60-75% at 1 year. Crude survival after successful treatment is roughly 80-90% at 1 year and 70-80% at 2 years. Death often reflects comorbidities and end-organ complications rather than antibiotic failure. Adverse outcomes are linked to older age, severe comorbidity/diabetes, delayed diagnosis, prosthetic or aortic valve involvement, virulent or resistant pathogens (S. aureus, P. aeruginosa, yeast), intracardiac and major neurologic complications, and health care-associated infection.
# Severity classification
Diagnosis uses the 2023 Duke-International Society for Cardiovascular Infectious Diseases (Duke-ISCVID) update of the modified Duke criteria (sensitivity 80-93%). MAJOR criteria: (1) Microbiologic - a typical IE organism in 2 or more separate blood culture sets (S. aureus, S. lugdunensis, E. faecalis, all streptococci except S. pneumoniae and S. pyogenes, Granulicatella/Abiotrophia/Gemella, HACEK group; and, when intracardiac prosthetic material is present, CoNS, C. striatum, C. jeikeium, S. marcescens, P. aeruginosa, C. acnes, nontuberculous mycobacteria and Candida), OR a nontypical organism in 3 or more sets; positive PCR/single culture/serology for C. burnetii, Bartonella spp. or T. whipplei. (2) Imaging - echocardiography or cardiac CT showing vegetation, perforation/leaflet aneurysm, abscess, pseudoaneurysm or fistula, OR new valvular regurgitation, OR new prosthetic dehiscence; OR abnormal FDG-PET/CT activity on a valve/graft/lead/prosthetic material. (3) Surgical - IE seen at direct surgical inspection. MINOR criteria: predisposing condition (prior IE, injection drug use, prosthetic valve, prior valve repair, congenital heart disease such as bicuspid aortic valve, CIED, more-than-mild regurgitation/stenosis, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy); fever over 38.0 C; vascular phenomena (arterial emboli, septic pulmonary infarcts, mycotic aneurysm, intracranial/conjunctival hemorrhage, Janeway lesions); immunologic phenomena (glomerulonephritis, Osler nodes, Roth spots, positive rheumatoid factor); microbiologic evidence not meeting a major criterion; and PET/CT activity within 3 months of prosthetic implantation. CLASSIFICATION: DEFINITE IE = pathologic confirmation OR 2 major, or 1 major + 3 minor, or 5 minor criteria. POSSIBLE IE = 1 major + 1 minor, or 3 minor. REJECTED = firm alternative diagnosis, or resolution with under 4 days of antibiotics, or no histologic evidence at surgery/autopsy after under 4 days of therapy. IE is also classified by tempo (acute vs subacute), site (native vs prosthetic valve, right-sided/tricuspid), cause, and predisposition (injection drug use, health care-associated).
# Clinical pearls
IE spans a clinical continuum from acute to subacute, and the causative organism largely dictates the tempo: beta-hemolytic streptococci, S. aureus and pneumococci tend to produce an acute, rapidly destructive course, whereas viridans streptococci, enterococci, CoNS and HACEK organisms usually cause subacute disease (Bartonella, T. whipplei, C. burnetii and M. chimaera are exceptionally indolent).
Fever is present in 80-90% of cases and a heart murmur in 80-85%, but in subacute IE fever is typically low-grade (rarely above 39.4 C) and may be blunted altogether in elderly, severely debilitated, or renal-failure patients.
A new or worsening regurgitant murmur is heard in only 20-50% of cases; in acute IE on a previously normal valve a murmur may be absent at first but is ultimately detected in about 85%.
Classic peripheral stigmata (Janeway lesions, Osler nodes, Roth spots, subungual/splinter hemorrhages) appear in only about 2-15% of cases and have become infrequent with earlier diagnosis; their absence does not exclude IE.
Janeway lesions are nonsuppurative painless hemorrhagic macules of the palms/soles classically linked to prolonged subacute infection, whereas septic emboli (subungual hemorrhages, Osler nodes) mimicking these lesions are common in acute S. aureus IE.
Three independent predictors of embolization are S. aureus etiology, mobile vegetations larger than 10 mm, and involvement of the anterior mitral leaflet.
In people who inject drugs, 35-60% of IE is confined to the tricuspid valve and presents with fever but faint or absent murmur and no peripheral stigmata, often with septic pulmonary emboli (cough, pleuritic pain, nodular infiltrates).
S. aureus bacteremia carries an 8-14% prevalence of IE and high mortality, so echocardiography (high-quality TTE, preferably TEE) is recommended routinely; intracardiac device, emboli, 2 or more positive cultures, community acquisition, vertebral osteomyelitis or persistent bacteremia each raise IE risk and argue for early TEE.
About 12-17% of patients with E. faecalis bacteremia and about 7% with non-beta-hemolytic streptococcal bacteremia have IE, justifying echocardiographic screening when predictive features are present.
Up to half of arterial emboli precede the diagnosis of IE; embolic risk falls rapidly once effective antibiotics begin, so emboli occurring late during or after therapy do not by themselves indicate treatment failure.
