# Tricuspid Valve Disease
Tricuspid Valve Disease, Cardiovascular.
Aliases: Tricuspid stenosis; Tricuspid regurgitation; TS; TR.
# Pathophysiology
Tricuspid stenosis is defined by a diastolic pressure gradient between the right atrium and right ventricle that increases with inspiration (greater transvalvular flow) and falls with expiration. A mean diastolic gradient of about 4 mmHg is usually enough to raise mean right atrial pressure to levels causing systemic venous congestion - hepatomegaly, ascites, and edema. Resting cardiac output is typically depressed and fails to rise with exercise. In tricuspid regurgitation, the incompetent valve allows backward flow from RV to RA, with the regurgitant volume governed by RV systolic driving pressure and the size of the regurgitant orifice; severity is modulated by pulmonary artery systolic pressure, annular dimensions, respiratory changes in RV preload, and RA compliance. Significant TR enlarges the right atrium, raises RA and jugular venous pressures with prominent c-v waves, can 'ventricularize' the RA waveform, and leads to RV volume overload, dilation, and eventual systolic dysfunction - accelerated by any added pressure load from pulmonary hypertension or prior myocardial fibrosis.
# Prognosis
Mild-to-moderate tricuspid regurgitation is generally well tolerated when there is no other hemodynamic disturbance. Because TR commonly coexists with left-sided valve lesions, LV dysfunction, or pulmonary hypertension, those conditions often dominate the clinical course. As isolated severe TR progresses, reduced forward cardiac output causes fatigue and exertional dyspnea, and declining RV function leads to cervical pulsations, abdominal fullness, anorexia, muscle wasting, weight gain, and painful lower-extremity swelling. Severe TR is associated with markedly reduced cardiac output and elevated mean RA and RV end-diastolic pressures.
# Severity classification
Severe tricuspid stenosis is defined echocardiographically by a tricuspid valve area of 1 cm2 or less, or a pressure half-time of 190 ms or more; a mean diastolic gradient around 4 mmHg is hemodynamically significant. For tricuspid regurgitation, severity is staged (progressive Stage B, asymptomatic severe Stage C, symptomatic severe Stage D); annular dilation is defined as >40 mm (>21 mm/m2) on transthoracic echocardiography or >70 mm on direct intraoperative measurement, and severe TR is accompanied by hepatic vein systolic flow reversal.
# Clinical pearls
Tricuspid stenosis is far less common than mitral stenosis in North America and Western Europe, is usually rheumatic in origin, occurs more often in women, and essentially never appears as an isolated lesion (it is typically combined with mitral stenosis).
Hemodynamically significant tricuspid stenosis is seen in roughly 5-10% of patients with severe mitral stenosis, and rheumatic tricuspid stenosis is commonly accompanied by some tricuspid regurgitation; nonrheumatic causes of stenosis are rare.
More than 85% of tricuspid regurgitation encountered clinically is secondary (functional), arising from tricuspid annular dilation and leaflet tethering in the setting of RV remodeling from pressure/volume overload, MI, or trauma rather than from intrinsic valve disease.
New-onset atrial fibrillation, especially in older patients, can produce 'atrial functional' tricuspid regurgitation through atrial and annular remodeling.
Patients with severe tricuspid stenosis characteristically complain of relatively little dyspnea given the marked hepatomegaly, ascites, and edema present, because the right-sided obstruction limits pulmonary congestion.
Tricuspid regurgitation is more often detected by inspecting the neck veins (prominent c-v waves with rapid y descent) than by cardiac auscultation.
Carvallo's sign - inspiratory accentuation of the holosystolic murmur at the lower left sternal border - helps distinguish the murmur of tricuspid regurgitation from that of mitral regurgitation; it diminishes on expiration or during the Valsalva strain phase.
The murmur of tricuspid stenosis resembles that of mitral stenosis but is best heard at the lower left sternal border/xiphoid, is most prominent in presystole during sinus rhythm, and increases with inspiration while decreasing during expiration and Valsalva strain.
In tricuspid stenosis with sinus rhythm, the right atrial a wave can be enormous (approaching RV systolic pressure) and the y descent is prolonged; presystolic pulsations of an enlarged liver may also be palpable.
On ECG, absence of right ventricular hypertrophy in a patient with right-sided heart failure who is presumed to have mitral stenosis should raise suspicion for coexisting tricuspid valve disease; right atrial enlargement gives tall peaked P waves in lead II and prominent upright P waves in V1.
A distinct category of tricuspid regurgitation is caused by cardiac implantable electronic device (CIED) leads crossing the valve, producing leaflet entrapment or perforation; right ventricular apical pacing can additionally cause dyssynchronous contraction and functional TR.
Coexisting tricuspid stenosis can mask the hemodynamic and clinical features of associated mitral stenosis by lowering cardiac output and keeping left atrial, pulmonary artery, and RV systolic pressures only normal or mildly elevated.
# Additional differentials
Mitral stenosis (frequently coexists and dominates the picture)
Mitral regurgitation (murmur can be confused with TR without attention to respiratory variation)
Constrictive pericarditis / restrictive cardiomyopathy as a cause of right-sided venous congestion
Other causes of right heart failure with hepatic congestion and ascites
# Infection mimics
Infective endocarditis of the tricuspid valve causing primary regurgitation
# Non infectious mimics
Carcinoid heart disease causing tricuspid leaflet retraction and regurgitation
Ebstein's malformation and other congenital tricuspid deformities
Myxomatous tricuspid valve prolapse
Radiation-induced valve disease
Endomyocardial fibrosis
# Additional investigations
Transthoracic echocardiography (Doppler) - assesses leaflet thickening/doming, transvalvular gradient, RA and IVC size, associated TR severity, mitral and LV/RV function, and PA pressure
Continuous-wave Doppler of the TR jet velocity to estimate PA systolic pressure (may be blunted when TR is very severe)
ECG for right atrial enlargement, RVH, prior inferior/RV MI pattern, atrial fibrillation, or preexcitation in Ebstein's anomaly
Chest x-ray - RA and superior vena cava prominence with azygos engorgement in combined TS/MS; RA/RV enlargement in TR
Real-time three-dimensional echocardiography and cardiac MR for difficult assessment of TR severity, PA pressures, and RV size/function
Exercise testing to assess functional capacity in asymptomatic severe TR
Cardiac catheterization - not routinely needed for TS assessment
# Red flags
Severe hepatic congestion progressing to cirrhosis, jaundice, serious malnutrition, anasarca, and ascites in severe tricuspid valve disease
Persistent right-sided failure after an adequate mitral commissurotomy, suggesting unrecognized tricuspid stenosis
Progressive RV dilation and declining systolic function in severe TR
Marked reduction in cardiac output with prominent c-v wave and rapid y descent and loss of the x descent in severe TR
# Pitfalls
Tricuspid stenosis is easily overlooked because it almost always occurs alongside obvious mitral disease and its diastolic murmur resembles that of mitral stenosis - the diagnosis must be deliberately considered.
The TR murmur may be mistaken for mitral regurgitation unless its inspiratory accentuation and the degree of RV enlargement are appreciated.
Accurate echocardiographic quantification of TR severity, PA pressure, and RV size/function is genuinely difficult in many patients.
Continuous-wave Doppler estimation of PA systolic pressure can be falsely low when TR is very severe because the jet velocity is blunted by rapidly rising RA pressure.
# Management
For tricuspid stenosis, relieve systemic venous congestion with loop diuretics and sodium restriction and treat the underlying rheumatic disease that usually coexists with mitral stenosis
Correct significant tricuspid stenosis with balloon valvuloplasty or surgical repair or replacement, typically undertaken at the time of mitral valve surgery
For functional regurgitation, address the driving pulmonary hypertension and left-sided heart disease and relieve congestion with diuretics
Provide rate or rhythm control and anticoagulation for atrial fibrillation, which can itself generate atrial functional regurgitation through annular remodelling
Offer surgical annuloplasty, repair or valve replacement for severe disease, with a bioprosthesis often preferred in the tricuspid position, and consider transcatheter intervention in selected high-risk patients
Give secondary antibiotic prophylaxis with penicillin for rheumatic disease and address endocarditis prophylaxis considerations
Undertake serial echocardiographic surveillance of valve gradient, regurgitation severity, right ventricular function and exercise tolerance, managing decisions through a heart valve team
