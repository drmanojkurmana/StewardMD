# Carcinoid heart disease
Carcinoid heart disease, Cardiovascular / Neuroendocrine, Cardiology.
Aliases: Hedinger syndrome; carcinoid cardiac disease; carcinoid cardiac involvement; carcinoid heart; carcinoid syndrome heart disease; carcinoid valvular disease; carcinoid valvular heart disease.
# Pathophysiology
Carcinoid heart disease is driven by chronic exposure of the endocardium to circulating serotonin and other vasoactive mediators released by a metastatic neuroendocrine tumour. Serotonin stimulates fibroblast proliferation and deposition of fibrous tissue (carcinoid plaque) on the valve leaflets, subvalvular apparatus and endocardium, causing leaflet thickening, retraction and immobility. Because the pulmonary vascular bed inactivates these mediators, disease is characteristically right-sided; left-sided disease implies mediators reaching the left heart via a bronchial primary or an intracardiac shunt. The result is tricuspid regurgitation and pulmonary valve stenosis or regurgitation, leading to volume and pressure overload of the right heart and progressive right ventricular failure.
# Prognosis
Carcinoid heart disease is a leading cause of death in carcinoid syndrome and markedly worsens prognosis once right heart failure develops; timely valve replacement in selected patients can relieve symptoms and improve survival, although established fibrosis is irreversible.
# Clinical pearls
Fibrotic endocardial and valvular disease complicating carcinoid syndrome, historically termed Hedinger syndrome, affecting a large proportion of patients with the syndrome
Predominantly right-sided: plaque-like deposits on the tricuspid and pulmonary valves and right heart endocardium cause tricuspid regurgitation and pulmonary stenosis or regurgitation
Left-sided involvement is uncommon and suggests a bronchial primary, a very high mediator load, or a right-to-left shunt through a patent foramen ovale, since the lungs otherwise inactivate mediators
Presents with right heart failure, including a raised JVP with prominent v waves, hepatomegaly, ascites and peripheral oedema, often out of proportion to left-sided symptoms
A raised NT-proBNP and markedly elevated urinary 5-HIAA correlate with the presence and severity of cardiac involvement and are used for screening
Echocardiography is the key diagnostic tool, showing thickened, retracted and immobile valve leaflets; cardiac MRI can further characterise disease and right ventricular function
Controlling the tumour and serotonin secretion with somatostatin analogues and other antitumour therapies is central but does not reverse established valvular fibrosis
Valve replacement surgery in appropriately selected patients is the only effective treatment for severe valvular disease and can improve symptoms and prognosis
# Additional differentials
Other causes of tricuspid regurgitation (functional, rheumatic, infective endocarditis, prior device leads)
Right heart failure from pulmonary hypertension or left heart disease
Constrictive pericarditis or restrictive cardiomyopathy
Ebstein anomaly or other congenital tricuspid or pulmonary valve disease
Rheumatic multivalvular disease
Endomyocardial fibrosis
# Additional investigations
Transthoracic echocardiography for valve morphology, regurgitation or stenosis and right heart function
NT-proBNP as a screening and severity marker
24-hour urinary 5-HIAA and chromogranin A
Cardiac MRI for right ventricular volumes and valve assessment
Somatostatin receptor PET/CT and cross-sectional imaging to stage the tumour
Right heart catheterisation in selected cases before intervention
Transoesophageal echocardiography with bubble study to assess for a patent foramen ovale or left-sided disease
# Red flags
Progressive right heart failure with ascites, oedema and hepatic congestion
Severe tricuspid regurgitation or pulmonary stenosis on echocardiography
Syncope or low cardiac output
Rapidly rising NT-proBNP or urinary 5-HIAA
Need for non-cardiac surgery where carcinoid crisis risk compounds cardiac risk
# Pitfalls
Not screening for cardiac involvement with echocardiography and NT-proBNP in patients with carcinoid syndrome
Attributing oedema and ascites to the tumour or liver metastases rather than to right heart failure
Delaying valve surgery until right ventricular function is irreversibly impaired
Assuming medical control of the tumour will reverse established valvular disease
Failing to plan perioperative carcinoid crisis prophylaxis for cardiac or non-cardiac surgery
# Management
Somatostatin analogues to suppress vasoactive mediator release and control the carcinoid syndrome
Tumour-directed therapy, including hepatic-directed treatment, to lower the serotonin burden driving valvular fibrosis
Diuretic therapy for congestive right heart failure with ascites, hepatomegaly and peripheral oedema
Surgical valve replacement for severe symptomatic tricuspid and pulmonary valve disease before right ventricular function deteriorates
Screening and monitoring with NT-proBNP and serial echocardiography, using cardiac MRI where further characterisation is required
Somatostatin analogue prophylaxis to prevent intraoperative carcinoid crisis
Coordinated care through a neuroendocrine tumour multidisciplinary team including cardiology
