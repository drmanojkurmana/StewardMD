Percutaneous nephrolithotomy, the creation of a working track from the skin of the flank directly into the collecting system of the kidney, is the preferred first line operation for bulky renal stone disease. Campbell-Walsh-Wein Urology sets the threshold clearly: renal calculi larger than 20 mm, staghorn calculi that branch through the pelvis into several calyces, and lower pole calculi over 15 mm belong to PCNL, while smaller stones remain the territory of shock wave lithotripsy and flexible ureteroscopy.

Safe access begins with anatomy. The kidney is entered through a posterior calyx, and the needle travels in the plane of Brodel, the relatively avascular corridor lying at the junction of the anterior two thirds and the posterior third of the renal parenchyma. This corridor carries few segmental arteries, so a puncture held to it bleeds far less than one driven through the vascular anterior divisions, and it delivers the sheath coaxially into the collecting system for efficient stone work.

Upper pole stones resist access from below, and here the supracostal puncture earns its place. Passing above the 12th rib, and at times above the 11th rib, lines the sheath up with the upper calyx and shortens operating time. The cost is thoracic: the pleura and lung sit in the needle path, and hydrothorax or pneumothorax complicates 5% to 15% of supracostal cases, which is why a plain chest radiograph after the procedure is standard practice.

Tract calibre has shrunk with instrumentation. Standard PCNL works through 24F to 30F sheaths that evacuate fragments rapidly but divide more parenchyma and lose more blood. Mini-PCNL works through 14F to 20F sheaths, so parenchymal trauma and measured blood loss drop markedly, while holmium laser dusting keeps stone clearance competitive for moderate burdens. Massive staghorn volumes still justify the standard wide tract.

Complexity grading guides consent. The Guy stone score assigns Grade I to a solitary simple stone and climbs to Grade IV for staghorn and complex branched patterns, with rising grades forecasting falling stone free rates. The CROES nephrolithometric nomogram widens the lens, combining stone burden with calyceal location and patient factors into a single validated estimate of clearance probability.

Bleeding demands a ladder. Diffuse venous oozing from the fresh track usually yields to balloon tamponade, the inflation of a low pressure balloon inside the sheath tract against the raw parenchyma. Brisk arterial haemorrhage that persists after tamponade is instead an indication for selective transcatheter renal artery angioembolization, with coils or particles delivered superselectively to the injured segmental branch.

For stones that branch beyond the reach of one instrument, endoscopic combined intrarenal surgery runs two routes together: flexible ureterorenoscopy advances retrograde through the ureter while the nephroscope works antegrade down the PCNL tract, and the pair clears parallel calyces in a single sitting. When the case finishes cleanly, with no residual fragments and only trivial bleeding, the surgeon exits tubeless, omitting the nephrostomy tube and leaving a ureteral stent or simple catheter, which trims pain and shortens the hospital stay.
