# Hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state
Hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state, Endocrine.
# Red flags
Profound dehydration — careful fluid/insulin; precipitant often infection
# Investigations
Glucose, osmolality, electrolytes
Venous gas (minimal ketosis)
Search for precipitant
# Pathophysiology
HHS arises from RELATIVE insulin deficiency combined with inadequate fluid intake. Insulin deficiency drives hepatic glucose overproduction (glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis) and impairs glucose uptake by skeletal muscle. The resulting hyperglycaemia causes an osmotic diuresis with intravascular volume depletion that is worsened by inadequate fluid replacement. The reason ketosis is absent is not fully understood; the insulin deficiency is presumed to be only relative and less severe than in DKA, and some studies show lower counterregulatory hormone and free-fatty-acid levels in HHS than in DKA, with possibly reduced hepatic ketone synthesis or an insulin/glucagon ratio that does not favour ketogenesis. Inflammatory markers (cytokines, C-reactive protein) are elevated in both HHS and DKA.
# Prognosis
Even with appropriate treatment, HHS has a substantially higher mortality rate than DKA, reported up to about 15% in some clinical series. The worse outlook reflects that HHS patients are usually older, have more profound fluid losses and dehydration (developed over days to weeks), are more likely to have mental status changes, and more often have a life-threatening precipitating event with accompanying comorbidities.
# Severity classification
HHS is instead defined by marked hyperglycaemia, hyperosmolality (>300 mOsm/L) and the relative absence of ketosis/acidosis, on a continuum with DKA.
# Clinical pearls
Typical patient is an older adult with type 2 diabetes presenting after a several-week prodrome of polyuria, weight loss and falling oral intake, progressing to confusion, lethargy or coma.
Examination reflects profound dehydration and hyperosmolality: hypotension, tachycardia and altered mental status.
Features classically ABSENT in HHS that are seen in DKA: nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain and Kussmaul respirations.
HHS and DKA lie on a continuum of hyperglycaemia; up to one-third of patients have overlapping features of both.
Plasma glucose in HHS is usually markedly higher than in DKA and may exceed 55.5 mmol/L (1000 mg/dL).
A debilitating condition (e.g. prior stroke, dementia) or a social situation that limits access to water frequently contributes to HHS by impairing water intake.
Worldwide reported cases of both HHS and DKA are rising for reasons that are not clear.
# Additional differentials
Diabetic ketoacidosis (and the DKA/HHS overlap presentation).
Other causes of an altered/hyperosmolar sensorium - the differential for hyperosmolar coma includes uremia, nonketotic hyperosmolar states and other metabolic derangements.
# Additional investigations
Plasma glucose - typically markedly elevated, may be >55.5 mmol/L (1000 mg/dL).
Serum osmolality - characteristically >300 mOsm/L.
Corrected serum sodium - measured Na may be normal or low, but corrected Na (add ~1.6 meq/L for each 5.6 mmol/L [100 mg/dL] rise in glucose) is usually elevated.
Renal function (BUN/creatinine) - prerenal azotaemia from volume depletion; creatinine typically moderately raised.
Ketones/beta-hydroxybutyrate - absent or only mild; serum beta-hydroxybutyrate <1.0 mmol/L; any moderate ketonuria is usually due to starvation.
Acid-base status - acidosis absent or mild; a small anion-gap metabolic acidosis may reflect raised lactic acid.
# Red flags
HHS carries a substantially higher mortality than DKA (up to ~15% in some series), reflecting older patients, more pronounced dehydration and serious precipitating events with comorbidities.
Hyperosmolar state must be reversed gradually - too-rapid correction risks worsening neurologic function.
Precipitant is often a serious concurrent illness - myocardial infarction, stroke, sepsis, pneumonia or other severe infection - which must be actively sought.
# Pitfalls
A normal or low measured serum sodium can be falsely reassuring - it must be corrected for hyperglycaemia, after which it is usually elevated and reveals a profound water deficit.
Mild ketonuria may be present but is typically from starvation, not from ketoacidosis - it should not redirect the diagnosis away from HHS.
Fever may be absent even when infection is the precipitant; signs of infection should be sought regardless.
# Confirm
Marked hyperglycaemia with high osmolality and minimal ketosis/acidosis, profound dehydration ± reduced consciousness (typically older T2DM).
Admit HDU/ICU.
IV saline first, low-rate insulin, K+ replacement, VTE prophylaxis
# Steps
Aggressive IV 0.9% saline — fluid replacement is the priority (large deficits, replace gradually).
Low-rate IV insulin, started after fluids (or if ketonaemia/glucose not falling with fluids alone).
Replace potassium guided by levels; monitor osmolality fall (aim gradual to avoid cerebral oedema).
VTE prophylaxis (high thrombotic risk); find and treat the precipitant.
Monitor glucose, Na, K and neuro status closely.
Aggressive IV saline first, then low-rate insulin
