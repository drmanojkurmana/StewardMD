# Diabetic ketoacidosis
Diabetic ketoacidosis, Endocrine.
Red flags: DKA protocol; look for precipitating infection.
# Investigations
Venous gas
Blood & urine ketones
Glucose, electrolytes
Search for precipitant (infection)
# Pathophysiology
DKA results from increased fatty acid metabolism with accumulation of the ketoacids acetoacetate and beta-hydroxybutyrate, producing hyperglycaemia, ketonaemia and a high-anion-gap metabolic acidosis. It typically occurs in insulin-dependent diabetes when insulin is stopped or when an intercurrent illness (e.g. infection, gastroenteritis, pancreatitis, myocardial infarction) acutely raises insulin requirements. In euglycaemic DKA caused by SGLT2 inhibitors, glycosuria drives an osmotic diuresis and volume depletion while plasma glucose stays in the normal range despite the ketoacidosis.
# Clinical pearls
An acetone-like fruity odour on the breath is a classic bedside clue to ketoacidosis.
Urine dipstick (nitroprusside reaction) detects acetoacetate but not beta-hydroxybutyrate, so it can underestimate the true degree of ketosis; measure plasma beta-hydroxybutyrate directly.
As the circulation is restored with fluids, beta-hydroxybutyrate is converted to acetoacetate, so the nitroprusside ketone reading may paradoxically become more positive during treatment rather than reflecting worsening disease.
Glucose may be normal or only mildly raised in euglycaemic DKA (SGLT2-inhibitor associated) and in starvation ketoacidosis, so a normal glucose does not exclude ketoacidosis.
Excretion of ketoacids obligates loss of cations (sodium, potassium), worsening volume depletion and promoting chloride retention.
# Additional differentials
Alcoholic ketoacidosis — high-anion-gap acidosis (predominantly beta-hydroxybutyrate) without hyperglycaemia, usually with preserved renal function, in chronic alcohol use with vomiting and poor intake.
Starvation ketoacidosis — ketoacidosis with normal or low glucose.
Other high-anion-gap acidoses: lactic acidosis, toxic ingestions (e.g. salicylates), and renal failure/uraemia.
# Non infectious mimics
Acute pancreatitis can mimic DKA (and vice versa), with overlapping abdominal pain; DKA may itself be accompanied by amylase elevation, complicating the distinction.
# Additional investigations
Plasma beta-hydroxybutyrate measured directly (more reliable than urine nitroprusside ketones).
Anion gap calculation, including assessment for a mixed non-anion-gap / high-anion-gap acidosis when the change in bicarbonate exceeds the change in anion gap.
# Red flags
Mucormycosis risk: DKA patients are at high risk of rhino-orbital-cerebral mucormycosis; acidosis frees iron from carrier proteins and beta-hydroxybutyrate upregulates fungal adherence/penetration receptors — consider in a DKA patient with sinus/orbital/facial signs.
Severe acidaemia (pH <7.00) or shock identifies the small subset where bicarbonate may be considered; cerebral oedema risk limits its use.
Hyperchloraemic (non-anion-gap) acidosis and volume overload can be precipitated by aggressive isotonic saline during treatment.
# Pitfalls
Relying on urine ketone dipstick, which misses beta-hydroxybutyrate and underestimates ketosis.
Interpreting a rising nitroprusside ketone result during fluid resuscitation as deterioration, when it reflects the beta-hydroxybutyrate-to-acetoacetate shift.
Assuming a normal/near-normal glucose excludes DKA, missing euglycaemic DKA on SGLT2 inhibitors.
Over-resuscitation with isotonic saline causing volume overload and/or hyperchloraemic acidosis.
# Diabetic ketoacidosis: treatment
Hyperglycaemia + ketonaemia/ketonuria + high-anion-gap metabolic acidosis. Often a precipitant (infection, missed insulin, new T1DM).
Admit (HDU/ICU if severe).
Fluids + fixed-rate insulin + potassium replacement
# Steps
IV 0.9% saline resuscitation first (fluid deficit is large); careful in young/cardiac/renal.
Fixed-rate IV insulin infusion (~0.1 U/kg/h); continue basal insulin if on it.
Replace potassium once K <5.5 and urine output adequate (insulin drives K intracellularly).
Add dextrose when glucose <14 mmol/L; monitor ketones, glucose, K, pH hourly.
Find and treat the precipitant; do not stop insulin infusion until ketones cleared and eating.
IV fluids + fixed-rate insulin infusion
