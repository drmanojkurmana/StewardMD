# Diabetic ketoacidosis (adult)
Adults
Emergency department, HDU, ICU, ward
Confirm DKA by glucose or known diabetes, ketones and acidosis; give crystalloid, check potassium before insulin, start fixed-rate IV insulin 0.1 units/kg/h, add dextrose when glucose falls below 250 mg/dL (13.9 mmol/L) and continue insulin until ketoacidosis resolves.
Aliases: DKA; euglycaemic DKA; SGLT2 inhibitor ketoacidosis; FRIII; fixed rate insulin infusion; hyperglycaemic crisis.
# Diagnose and grade
Diagnosis needs all three (2024 consensus): glucose 200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L) or more OR known diabetes; blood beta-hydroxybutyrate (BOHB) 3.0 mmol/L or more OR urine ketones 2+ or more; venous pH below 7.3 and/or bicarbonate below 18 mmol/L.
Mild: pH above 7.25 to 7.30 or bicarbonate 15 to 18 mmol/L, alert. Moderate: pH 7.0 to 7.25 or bicarbonate 10 to below 15, alert or drowsy. Severe: pH below 7.0, bicarbonate below 10, BOHB above 6.0 mmol/L, stupor or coma.
Euglycaemic DKA (about 10%): glucose below 200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L) with ketoacidosis. Causes: SGLT2 inhibitors (most cases), pregnancy, poor intake, alcohol, liver failure. Check ketones in any unwell patient on an SGLT2 inhibitor even if glucose is normal.
Features: polyuria, polydipsia, weight loss, vomiting, abdominal pain, dehydration, Kussmaul breathing, ketotic breath. Abdominal pain that persists after acidosis corrects needs a surgical cause excluded.
Precipitants: infection, missed or rationed insulin (often cost or access), new type 1 diabetes, MI, stroke, pancreatitis, glucocorticoids, antipsychotics, immune checkpoint inhibitors, SGLT2 inhibitors. About one third have a mixed DKA/HHS picture.
Differentials for raised ketones: starvation ketosis, alcoholic ketoacidosis (glucose often normal or low), ketosis of pregnancy and hyperemesis.
# Investigations
Capillary glucose, venous blood gas (pH, bicarbonate, potassium), blood BOHB (preferred to urine ketones), sodium, urea, creatinine, phosphate, CBC, calculated osmolality.
Urine nitroprusside ketones miss BOHB and lag: they underestimate severity early and overestimate it during recovery. Anion gap above 12 mmol/L supports DKA where blood ketones are unavailable.
ECG (hyperkalaemia or hypokalaemia changes, ischaemia). Look for the precipitant: blood and urine cultures, chest X-ray, lipase, troponin as indicated, pregnancy test in women of childbearing age.
# Fluids, potassium and insulin
Fluids: 0.9% saline or balanced crystalloid (Ringer lactate) at 500 to 1000 mL/h for the first 2 to 4 hours (about 1 L/h if severely hypovolaemic). Aim to replace about half the deficit in 8 to 12 hours and the rest over 24 to 48 hours. Balanced crystalloids may resolve DKA faster.
Check potassium BEFORE insulin. K below 3.5 mmol/L: hold insulin and give KCl 10 to 20 mmol/h IV until K is above 3.5 (faster rates need central access and cardiac monitoring).
K 3.5 to 5.0 mmol/L: add KCl 20 to 30 mmol to each litre of fluid to keep K 4 to 5 mmol/L. K above 5.0: give no potassium, start insulin, recheck K in 2 hours. Never give potassium as an IV push.
Moderate or severe DKA: fixed-rate IV infusion of regular (soluble) insulin 0.1 units/kg/h (50 units in 50 mL 0.9% saline = 1 unit/mL). If IV access or the infusion is delayed, give 0.1 units/kg IV or IM once.
Continue the patient's usual SC basal insulin during the infusion; in others, a low dose of basal analogue (0.15 to 0.3 units/kg SC) can be started alongside to shorten infusion time and prevent rebound.
When glucose falls below 250 mg/dL (13.9 mmol/L): add 5% or 10% dextrose alongside the saline and reduce insulin to 0.05 units/kg/h. Keep glucose 150 to 200 mg/dL (8.3 to 11.1 mmol/L) and continue insulin until DKA resolves.
Euglycaemic DKA (glucose below 200 mg/dL): start 5% or 10% dextrose with the saline at the same time as the insulin infusion.
Stop SGLT2 inhibitors on admission. Identify and treat the precipitant (for example antibiotics for infection).
# Mild DKA and electrolyte adjuncts
Uncomplicated mild or moderate DKA can be treated with SC rapid-acting analogue (lispro, aspart, glulisine) in ED or step-down care: 0.1 units/kg SC bolus, then 0.1 units/kg every hour or 0.2 units/kg every 2 hours. Not for severe DKA, HHS or shock.
SC regimen when glucose falls below 250 mg/dL: reduce to 0.05 units/kg every hour or 0.1 units/kg every 2 hours, with dextrose-containing fluid.
Bicarbonate only if pH is below 7.0: 100 mmol sodium bicarbonate (100 mL of 8.4%) in 400 mL sterile water, repeated every 2 hours until pH is above 7.0. Watch potassium closely.
Phosphate only if below 1.0 mmol/L with muscle weakness or respiratory or cardiac compromise: add 20 to 30 mmol potassium phosphate to replacement fluids. Rapid replacement can cause hypocalcaemia.
Give prophylactic LMWH unless contraindicated or thrombosis is suspected.
# Monitoring and resolution
Capillary glucose every 1 to 2 hours. Potassium 2 hours after starting insulin, then every 4 hours. Venous pH, bicarbonate, BOHB, electrolytes, phosphate and creatinine every 2 to 4 hours until resolution.
Expected response (JBDS): ketones fall at least 0.5 mmol/L/h, bicarbonate rises 3 mmol/L/h, glucose falls about 54 mg/dL/h (3 mmol/L/h). If not, check the infusion and line, then increase the insulin rate.
Resolution: plasma ketones below 0.6 mmol/L AND venous pH 7.3 or above OR bicarbonate 18 mmol/L or above; ideally glucose below 200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L).
Do not use anion gap or urine ketones to judge resolution: saline-induced hyperchloraemic normal anion gap acidosis can mimic persistent DKA.
Hourly fluid balance; aim for urine output about 0.5 mL/kg/h. Monitor mental state; hypoglycaemia and hypokalaemia are the commonest treatment complications.
# Transition to subcutaneous insulin
Switch when DKA has resolved and the patient can eat and drink. Give SC basal insulin 1 to 2 hours BEFORE stopping the IV insulin infusion to prevent rebound ketoacidosis.
Known insulin users: restart the preadmission dose; if control was poor (high HbA1c) or a drug contributed, change the regimen before discharge.
Insulin-naive: total daily dose 0.5 to 0.6 units/kg/day (about 0.3 units/kg/day if frail, older or CKD). Give 40 to 60% as basal and split the rest into three pre-meal rapid-acting doses; if nil by mouth, basal plus correction every 4 to 6 hours.
Do not start or restart SGLT2 inhibitors in hospital; after DKA in type 2 diabetes they are not routinely recommended. SGLT2 inhibitors are not recommended in type 1 diabetes.
Before discharge: sick-day rules, never stop basal insulin, ketone testing, adequate insulin supply, and follow-up within 2 to 4 weeks.
# Escalate to HDU or ICU
Severe DKA (pH below 7.0, bicarbonate below 10 mmol/L, BOHB above 6.0 mmol/L) or altered consciousness, or a critical precipitant (sepsis, MI, GI bleed): manage in ICU.
JBDS markers for HDU referral: K below 3.5 mmol/L on admission, GCS below 12, SpO2 below 92% on air, systolic BP below 90 mmHg, pulse above 100 or below 60 per minute.
Cerebral oedema is rare in adults but carries high mortality: falling GCS or new headache during treatment needs urgent imaging; treat with mannitol and ventilatory support.
# Special groups
Older adults, heart failure, ESKD on dialysis: give 250 mL fluid boluses with frequent haemodynamic reassessment. ESKD patients have higher glucose and potassium with lower ketones; replace potassium cautiously.
Pregnancy: DKA may be euglycaemic; involve senior obstetric and medical teams immediately and manage in an HDU or delivery suite with fetal monitoring.
Children and adolescents: use a paediatric DKA protocol (slower fluids, higher cerebral oedema risk); this adult protocol does not apply.
# Pitfalls
Starting insulin when potassium is below 3.5 mmol/L (risk of arrhythmia and respiratory muscle weakness).
Stopping the insulin infusion when glucose normalises while ketones persist: add dextrose and continue insulin instead.
Stopping IV insulin without overlapping SC basal insulin, causing rebound DKA.
Missing euglycaemic DKA in patients on SGLT2 inhibitors, in pregnancy or with poor intake because glucose is near normal.
