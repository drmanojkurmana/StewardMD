Venous access, pumps and leaks: working notes

Central access
- A central line ends in a large vein near the heart. Long-term central access is common for people receiving cancer care.
- Blood draws through a multi-lumen central catheter spare repeated needle sticks, which matters during long stretches of myelosuppression (low blood cell output from treatment).

Implanted port (mediport)
- A surgically placed reservoir sits under the skin and connects to a central vein. Often called a mediport.
- When continuous central access is needed during an infection, a non-surgical line such as a peripherally inserted central catheter (PICC) is the reasonable first choice.
- A new surgically implanted port is reasonable only after blood cultures show clear clearance of the infection.

Catheter-related bloodstream infection (CRBSI)
- Germs travel into the blood along an indwelling line.
- Prevention starts at insertion with aseptic technique, applied through a bundle or checklist of steps that have evidence behind them.
- Each time staff touch the catheter, it is a chance for contamination.
- These infections triple the risk of death during a hospital stay.
- Daily full-body cleansing with 2% chlorhexidine gluconate (2 percent) is effective. It is standard care in intensive care units (ICUs) for patients with central lines.

Infusion pumps
- A pump delivers drug at a programmed rate across a fixed period.
- Continuous infusion rates are written as an amount per hour. Midazolam, a sedative, is commonly given at 1 to 5 milligrams per hour (1-5 mg/h).
- Programmable pumps may give a patient-controlled bolus with a lockout interval (the minimum wait between doses), plus a continuous background rate.
- Smart pumps are set for each medication and dose. They warn the nurse before a dose that is too high is delivered.
- Smart pumps are one of the medication-safety technologies in use.

Extravasation basics
- Extravasation means a drug leaks from the vein into surrounding tissue.
- First step: stop the infusion. Then try to aspirate residual drug from the site.
- The next measure depends on the drug class involved.
- Vesicant means a drug that injures tissue when it leaks.

Anthracyclines
- Doxorubicin is a potent vesicant. Severe skin necrosis (tissue death) can follow a leak and may need surgical debridement (removal of dead tissue) and skin grafts.
- Necrosis can become visible 4 to 7 days after the leak.
- Doxorubicin should run through a central venous catheter or a rapidly flowing intravenous line.
- Dactinomycin can also cause severe tissue necrosis after extravasation.
- Dexrazoxane is an intracellular chelating agent. It can mitigate doxorubicin extravasation.
- Where dexrazoxane is unavailable or contraindicated, topical dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) is the recommended alternative for anthracycline leaks (ONS/ASCO 2025 guideline).
- Cold compresses are recommended for anthracycline leaks (ONS/ASCO 2025 guideline).
Vinca alkaloids
- Vinca alkaloids are potent vesicants.
- Stop treatment and aspirate any residual drug.
- For extravasation generally, the device is left in place at first, and as much drug as possible is withdrawn by syringe (ONS/ASCO 2025 guideline).
- Flush the vein after treatment to lower the risk of phlebitis (vein inflammation).
- Use heat, not ice, for 1 hour, four times daily, for 3 to 5 days, to limit tissue damage.
- Hyaluronidase is an enzyme that loosens tissue. Give 150 to 1500 units under the skin.
- Use about six injections in a clockwise circle around the site, with a 25-gauge needle changed for each injection.
- Hyaluronidase may reduce discomfort and latent cellulitis (hidden infection of the tissue).
- A surgical consultation to consider early debridement is also recommended.
