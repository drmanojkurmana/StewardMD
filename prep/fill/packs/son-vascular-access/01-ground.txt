Venous access, pumps and leaks: working notes

Central access options
- A multi-lumen central catheter lets staff draw blood without repeated needle sticks. This helps during long spells of low blood cell output caused by treatment.
- An implanted port is a reservoir set under the skin and linked to a central vein. Surgeons place it, and clinicians often call it a mediport.
- The chapters allow a fresh surgical port only once blood cultures confirm the bloodstream infection has cleared.
- While a patient still needs continuous central access during an infection, a non-surgical catheter such as a PICC is the sensible option.
- Two 2025 port-versus-PICC analyses disagree on infection, so the lesson makes no infection claim for ports.

Catheter-related bloodstream infection
- Germs can travel along an indwelling line into the bloodstream.
- Central catheters are a main focus of infection prevention.
- Protection starts at insertion, using a checklist built on evidence. Every later handling step also needs a sterile approach.
- Each staff interaction with the line creates a chance for contamination.
- Such infections triple the odds of dying during a hospital admission.
- Full-body washing with 2% chlorhexidine gluconate every day is effective and is routine in intensive care units (ICUs).
- Hub care uses alcohol. Site preparation at insertion uses chlorhexidine with alcohol.

Infusion pumps
- A pump releases medicine at a fixed setting, frequently as a continuous drip.
- A 2021 review describes battery-operated programmable pumps as accurate to within 2.5 to 6%.
- Continuous rates are quoted per hour; midazolam (a sedative) can be given at 1 to 5 milligrams per hour.
- Programmable pumps can offer a patient-triggered dose. A lockout interval is the fixed minimum wait between those doses.
- Smart pumps hold limits for each drug and dose, and they alert staff before an excessive dose is delivered.

Vesicants and extravasation
- A vesicant damages tissue when it escapes from a vein. Escape of that kind is extravasation.
- Doxorubicin is a potent vesicant from the anthracycline family. Skin death can follow a leak and may require surgical removal of tissue plus grafts.
- Doxorubicin damage can show 4 to 7 days after the leak.
- Doxorubicin is preferably given through a central venous line.
- Dactinomycin can also cause severe tissue death after a leak.
- Vincristine is a potent vesicant. Local heat and hyaluronidase infiltration are used.

Dexrazoxane
- Dexrazoxane is a chelating agent that works inside cells by binding metal ions.
- It can reduce the harm from a doxorubicin leak.
- The EU product summary asks for the first infusion as early as possible, inside 6 hours of the leak.
- The schedule is once daily for 3 days: 1000 mg/m2, then 1000 mg/m2, then 500 mg/m2.
- Every infusion is given across 1 to 2 hours through a big vein distant from the leak.

Cold compresses and DMSO for anthracyclines
- A cold compress constricts local vessels and restrains spread of an anthracycline.
- The cold pack is removed 15 minutes before dexrazoxane begins.
- When dexrazoxane is unavailable or contraindicated, dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) is the substitute, applied every 8 hours for 7 days.
- DMSO is never used alongside dexrazoxane.

Vinca alkaloids
- Vinca alkaloids are potent vesicants.
- For a suspected leak, halt treatment and attempt to withdraw residual drug.
- Heat rather than ice helps limit tissue injury. Apply it for 1 hour at a time, four times daily, across 3 to 5 days.
- Flushing the vein after treatment reduces phlebitis risk.
- Hyaluronidase breaks down connective tissue. Give 150 to 1500 units beneath the skin.
- Place six injections in a clockwise ring around the site, using a 25-gauge needle replaced for each one.
- The injections may ease discomfort and hidden tissue infection.
- An early surgical opinion about debridement is also advised.
