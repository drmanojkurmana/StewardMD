Nasoorbitoethmoid fractures shatter the central midface where the nose, orbits, and ethmoid sinuses meet. The critical structure is the central tendon-bearing bone fragment that carries the medial canthal tendon, and the whole repair strategy depends on whether that fragment and its tendon insertion survive intact.

Markowitz and Manson classify NOE fractures by the fate of this central fragment. Type I is a single large central fragment with the tendon still attached. Type II shows a comminuted central fragment, yet the medial canthal tendon insertion remains intact on one piece. Type III means severe comminution with frank avulsion of the medial canthal tendon from the bone.

Clinical hallmarks declare an NOE fracture at the bedside. Traumatic telecanthus widens the intercanthal distance beyond 35 mm because the canthal-bearing fragments splay apart. The nasal dorsum collapses into a saddle nose deformity, and dural tears at the cribriform plate leak cerebrospinal fluid as CSF rhinorrhoea.

Orbital blowout fractures follow a different mechanism, where a blunt globe impact raises intraorbital pressure until the thinnest wall gives way. The break most commonly involves the thin posteromedial orbital floor just behind the infraorbital rim, often extending into the lamina papyracea of the ethmoid bone medially.

Entrapped orbital contents produce the classic functional signs. The inferior rectus or inferior oblique muscle catches in the floor defect, causing vertical diplopia that worsens on upward gaze. The forced duction test turns positive because the globe cannot move freely, and periorbital ecchymosis with infraorbital paraesthesia completes the picture.

The white-eyed blowout fracture in children is a trap for the unwary, presenting with a deceptively quiet eye but an entrapped muscle. The trapped tissue triggers the oculocardiac reflex through vagal stimulation, causing bradycardia with nausea, vomiting, and syncope that can prove life-threatening. This injury is a surgical emergency demanding release within hours.

Repair thresholds decide which blowouts need the operating theatre within 2 weeks. Persistent diplopia with a positive forced duction test, enophthalmos exceeding 2 mm from enlarged orbital volume, or a floor defect exceeding 50% of the orbital floor each mandate surgery. Smaller defects without entrapment settle with observation and steroids.

Reconstruction rebuilds the floor with autologous bone or cartilage grafts, or with porous polyethylene Medpor sheets and titanium mesh for larger defects. The transconjunctival approach hides the scar inside the eyelid, while the subciliary approach runs just below the lashes for wider exposure.
