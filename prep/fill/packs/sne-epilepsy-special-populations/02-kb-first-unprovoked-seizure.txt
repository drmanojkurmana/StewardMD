# First unprovoked seizure (adult)
Adults
Emergency department, first seizure clinic, outpatient
Confirm an epileptic seizure, exclude provoking causes (glucose, sodium, calcium, alcohol, drugs, infection), do an ECG and neuroimaging, arrange EEG and specialist review within 2 weeks, give driving and safety advice, and start antiseizure medication only when recurrence risk is high or the patient chooses.
Aliases: first seizure; first fit; new onset seizure; single seizure; unprovoked seizure; new-onset epilepsy; blackout with jerking.
# Was it an epileptic seizure?
Take a detailed eyewitness history and use video if available: warning, stiffening then jerking, duration, lateral tongue bite, incontinence, prolonged post-ictal confusion.
Distinguish from syncope (triggers such as standing or pain, brief jerks, rapid recovery), cardiac arrhythmia, functional seizures, TIA, hypoglycaemia and intoxication.
Provoked (acute symptomatic) seizure: within about 7 days of stroke, head injury or brain surgery, during CNS infection, metabolic derangement, or alcohol or drug toxicity or withdrawal. Treat the cause; this is not epilepsy.
Ask about earlier unrecognised events (morning myoclonic jerks, absences, focal aware seizures, nocturnal events): the patient may already have epilepsy.
One unprovoked seizure with a recurrence risk of 60% or more (for example a remote structural lesion with epileptiform EEG) can be diagnosed as epilepsy (ILAE practical definition).
# In the emergency department
Seizure lasting 5 minutes or more, or recurrent without recovery: treat as status epilepticus (see that protocol).
Check capillary glucose. If below 60 mg/dL (3.3 mmol/L): thiamine 100 mg IV then 50 mL of 50% dextrose IV (thiamine first in alcohol use or malnutrition).
Observe until back to baseline; look for injuries (shoulder dislocation, head injury, aspiration).
Refer urgently to a first seizure or neurology clinic, to be seen within 2 weeks (NICE). Starting long-term antiseizure medication is usually a specialist decision.
# Investigations
Bloods: glucose, sodium, calcium, magnesium, renal and liver function, CBC; pregnancy test in women who could be pregnant; alcohol and toxicology when relevant.
12-lead ECG in everyone to look for long QT, Brugada pattern, pre-excitation, heart block or cardiomyopathy.
Urgent CT in the ED if focal deficit, not returning to baseline, head injury, anticoagulant use, fever, cancer or immunosuppression. MRI (epilepsy protocol) is the preferred imaging for adults with an unprovoked seizure.
EEG: routine awake EEG as soon as possible, ideally within 72 hours; sleep-deprived EEG if normal. A normal EEG does not exclude epilepsy.
LP only if CNS infection or SAH is suspected, after imaging if there are focal signs or reduced consciousness.
India: contrast CT or MRI to look for neurocysticercosis (single small enhancing lesion) and tuberculoma, common causes of new seizures.
# Whether to start antiseizure medication
Recurrence risk is highest in the first 2 years (21 to 45%) and is higher with a prior brain insult, epileptiform EEG, significant imaging abnormality or a seizure during sleep (AAN/AES 2015).
Immediate treatment reduces recurrence over the next 2 years (by about 35% in absolute terms) but does not improve long-term remission or quality of life; adverse effects occur in 7 to 31%, mostly mild and reversible.
Consider starting after a first seizure if there is a neurological deficit, unequivocal epileptiform EEG, a structural brain abnormality, or the patient finds the recurrence risk unacceptable (NICE 2022).
Choice: levetiracetam or lamotrigine for focal seizures; lamotrigine, levetiracetam or valproate for generalised tonic-clonic seizures. Valproate in women and girls only under the pregnancy prevention programme.
Levetiracetam: start 250 to 500 mg orally twice daily, increase every 2 weeks as needed to max 1500 mg twice daily; reduce in renal impairment; watch mood and behaviour.
Lamotrigine: 25 mg once daily for 14 days, then 50 mg once daily for 14 days, then increase by up to 100 mg every 1 to 2 weeks; usual maintenance 100 to 200 mg daily. Stop if rash; slower titration with valproate.
# Advice and follow-up
Safety: no swimming or bathing alone (shower instead), avoid heights, open fires and dangerous machinery until reviewed; regular sleep; limit alcohol.
Driving: stop driving now and follow local law; document the advice. UK DVLA car licence: 6 months off driving after a first unprovoked seizure, or 12 months if there is an underlying causative factor; bus and lorry licence 5 years.
Women: discuss contraception and pregnancy plans; enzyme-inducing drugs reduce hormonal contraceptive efficacy, and valproate and topiramate need the pregnancy prevention programme.
Give first aid information, ask the family to video any further event, and explain who to contact if another seizure occurs before the clinic appointment.
