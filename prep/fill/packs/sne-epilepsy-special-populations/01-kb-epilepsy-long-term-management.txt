# Epilepsy: long-term management (adult)
Adults
Outpatient, epilepsy clinic, primary care
Classify the seizure type, use one antiseizure medication chosen for that type (lamotrigine or levetiracetam first for most adults), do not start valproate under 55 unless two specialists agree, counsel on SUDEP, driving, contraception and pregnancy, and refer early if two drugs fail.
Aliases: antiseizure medication; ASM; AED; antiepileptic drug; seizure disorder; valproate pregnancy prevention; SUDEP; drug-resistant epilepsy; juvenile myoclonic epilepsy; JME; epilepsy and pregnancy.
# Confirm and classify before treating
Classify the seizure type (focal, generalised tonic-clonic, absence, myoclonic, tonic or atonic) and the epilepsy syndrome where possible (ILAE); drug choice depends on it, and some drugs worsen generalised epilepsies.
Epilepsy: 2 unprovoked seizures more than 24 hours apart, 1 unprovoked seizure with a recurrence risk of 60% or more, or an epilepsy syndrome (ILAE). For a single seizure see First unprovoked seizure (adult).
Start antiseizure medication once the diagnosis is confirmed (NICE NG217). If seizures continue despite an optimal dose of a first-line drug, review the diagnosis: syncope, arrhythmia, functional seizures and non-adherence.
Drug-resistant epilepsy (ILAE): failure of adequate trials of 2 tolerated, appropriately chosen and used antiseizure medication schedules to achieve sustained seizure freedom. Refer to a tertiary epilepsy service.
# Choosing the drug (NICE NG217)
Use a single drug whenever possible. Start low and titrate; in older adults use lower doses and slower titration and check interactions.
Focal seizures (with or without bilateral tonic-clonic spread): lamotrigine or levetiracetam first line; then carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine or zonisamide; then lacosamide.
Generalised tonic-clonic seizures and idiopathic generalised epilepsy: lamotrigine, levetiracetam or sodium valproate. Valproate is the most effective, but follow the MHRA restrictions below.
Absence seizures alone: ethosuximide first, then lamotrigine, levetiracetam or valproate; absence with other seizure types: lamotrigine, levetiracetam or valproate. Myoclonic: levetiracetam or valproate. Tonic or atonic: lamotrigine or valproate, with neurologist input.
Absence, myoclonic, tonic or atonic seizures (including JME): carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine, phenytoin, gabapentin, pregabalin, tiagabine and vigabatrin may worsen seizures; lamotrigine can worsen myoclonus.
Changing drugs: build up the second drug while keeping the first; if it works, taper the first slowly. Try add-on therapy only after monotherapy fails, titrating carefully and watching for sedation.
Carbamazepine (and possibly oxcarbazepine and eslicarbazepine) and phenytoin carry a higher risk of serious skin reactions in some ancestries; screen Han Chinese and Thai patients for HLA-B*1502 before carbamazepine. Stop and review at any rash.
# Valproate, contraception and pregnancy
Valproate (MHRA, NICE 2025): do not start it for the first time in anyone, male or female, under 55 unless 2 specialists independently document that no other effective and tolerated treatment exists or that reproductive risks do not apply.
Women and girls who could become pregnant may take valproate only within the Pregnancy Prevention Programme. Exposed babies: up to about 1 in 10 have major malformations and up to 4 in 10 have developmental problems.
Men taking valproate: advise condoms plus contraception by a female partner during treatment and for 3 months after stopping; refer men planning a family within a year for a medication review.
Topiramate: do not use in women who could become pregnant unless Pregnancy Prevention Programme conditions are met. Carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine, phenytoin and topiramate reduce hormonal contraceptive effect; oestrogens lower lamotrigine levels.
Planning pregnancy or pregnant: refer to the epilepsy specialist team and never stop antiseizure drugs abruptly. Consider a pre-conception level and monitoring of levels (for example lamotrigine, levetiracetam) in pregnancy; plan to return to pre-pregnancy doses soon after birth.
Start folic acid before conception in women taking antiseizure drugs who could become pregnant; 5 mg daily has been standard UK advice but the optimal dose is debated, so follow local specialist guidance. Encourage breastfeeding.
India: neurocysticercosis and tuberculoma are common causes of focal epilepsy; phenytoin and phenobarbital are widely used because of cost, but NICE prefers lamotrigine or levetiracetam where affordable.
# Monitoring and review
Review at least yearly, and more often with drug-resistant epilepsy, learning disability, high SUDEP risk, serious comorbidity, or valproate or topiramate in a woman who could become pregnant.
Drug levels are not routine. Check them for uncontrolled seizures, suspected toxicity or non-adherence, pregnancy and renal failure. Carbamazepine usually needs about 4 to 12 mg/L.
Long-term carbamazepine, phenytoin, primidone and valproate reduce bone density: consider vitamin D and calcium in those at risk.
Keep carbamazepine, phenytoin, phenobarbital and primidone on the same manufacturer's product (MHRA category 1).
Ask about mood, anxiety and suicidal thoughts at each review; levetiracetam, topiramate and perampanel can cause irritability, depression or aggression.
# SUDEP, safety and driving
Discuss epilepsy-related death, including SUDEP, from diagnosis. Modifiable risks: non-adherence, alcohol or drug misuse, generalised or focal to bilateral tonic-clonic seizures, uncontrolled seizures, living alone and sleeping alone without supervision.
For people with nocturnal seizures at higher risk, discuss night-time supervision or a monitoring device.
Safety: shower rather than bath, never swim alone, avoid heights, open fires and unguarded machinery; keep regular sleep and limit alcohol.
Driving: follow the local licensing authority. Example (UK DVLA, car licence): 12 months seizure-free; 6 months after a first unprovoked seizure without high-risk features; no driving during drug withdrawal and for 6 months after.
Give a written emergency plan: a seizure lasting 5 minutes or more, or clusters, need a rescue benzodiazepine (buccal midazolam 10 mg in adults) and emergency care; see Convulsive status epilepticus.
# Specialist referral and stopping treatment
Refer to a tertiary epilepsy service (NICE: seen within 4 weeks) for diagnostic uncertainty, drug resistance, intolerable side effects, or when video-EEG, epilepsy surgery, vagus nerve stimulation or ketogenic diet is being considered.
Seek specialist advice for tonic or atonic seizures, a suspected severe syndrome (for example Lennox-Gastaut), pregnancy planning and any plan to start valproate.
Stopping: after 2 years seizure-free, make an individual recurrence-risk assessment. Taper one drug at a time, usually over at least 3 months (longer for benzodiazepines and barbiturates). If seizures recur, reverse the last reduction and seek advice.
# Pitfalls
Giving carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine or phenytoin for absence or myoclonic seizures (for example unrecognised JME) and worsening them.
Titrating lamotrigine too fast, especially with valproate: serious rash risk. Restart from the titration schedule after a gap of more than 5 half-lives.
Starting valproate in a young woman without the Pregnancy Prevention Programme, or stopping it abruptly on discovering pregnancy instead of arranging urgent specialist review.
Missing interactions: enzyme inducers with contraceptives, warfarin and many other drugs; valproate roughly doubling lamotrigine levels.
# Drugs
Lamotrigine
# Dose
Monotherapy: 25 mg once daily for 2 weeks, then 50 mg once daily for 2 weeks, then increase by up to 50 to 100 mg every 1 to 2 weeks; usual 100 to 200 mg daily (some need up to 500 mg)
With valproate: 25 mg on alternate days for 2 weeks, 25 mg daily for 2 weeks, then by 25 to 50 mg every 1 to 2 weeks to 100 to 200 mg daily. With enzyme inducers and no valproate: start 50 mg daily. Stop at any rash.
Levetiracetam
# Dose
500 mg twice daily (or 250 mg twice daily for 2 weeks first); adjust by 250 to 500 mg twice daily every 2 to 4 weeks; max 1500 mg twice daily
CrCl 50 to 79: 500 to 1000 mg twice daily; 30 to 49: 250 to 750 mg twice daily; below 30: 250 to 500 mg twice daily. Irritability, low mood.
Carbamazepine
# Dose
100 to 200 mg once or twice daily, increased slowly; usual 800 to 1200 mg daily in divided doses (up to 2000 mg in some)
Enzyme inducer; hyponatraemia; rash and SJS (HLA-B*1502). Target level about 4 to 12 mg/L.
Sodium valproate
# Dose
600 mg daily, increased by 200 mg every 3 days; usual 1000 to 2000 mg daily (20 to 30 mg/kg/day); max 2500 mg daily
Do not start under 55 unless 2 specialists agree (MHRA). Pregnancy Prevention Programme. Hepatotoxicity, pancreatitis, weight gain, tremor; raises lamotrigine levels.
Midazolam (buccal), 10 mg buccally for a seizure lasting 5 minutes or more (adult), Rescue medicine within an individual emergency plan; call emergency services if the seizure continues.
