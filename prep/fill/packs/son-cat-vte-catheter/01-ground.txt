Cancer-associated clotting: grounding notes

Scope. Cancer makes venous thromboembolism more likely, and the notes below cover four linked questions: outpatient risk scoring, prophylaxis, treatment and catheter problems. Every figure here was taken from a standard reference or a guideline, then restated in plain words.

Risk scoring. The Khorana score adds points for the primary tumour site and for four measurements taken before chemotherapy starts. A stomach or pancreatic primary adds 2 points. Lung, lymphoma, gynaecologic and genitourinary primaries, other than prostate, add 1 point each. Four further measures each count one point when outside the cut-off: platelets at or above 350,000 per microlitre, a haemoglobin under 10 g/dL or use of red cell growth factors, a leukocyte count above 11,000 per microlitre, and a body mass index of 35 kg/m2 or more.

Risk groups. A total of 0 points is low risk, with symptomatic clotting in about 0.3 to 0.8 percent. A total of 1 or 2 points is intermediate, at 1.8 to 2.0 percent. A total of 3 or more points is high risk, at 6.7 to 7.1 percent.

Prophylaxis. Routine prevention during chemotherapy is controversial. In trials of high-risk patients, rivaroxaban and apixaban cut clotting by about 50 percent, with bleeding near 5 percent. Standard references note that ASCO does not routinely recommend prophylaxis for outpatients in general. The 2023 ASCO update narrows its advice to a Khorana total of 2 or more before a new systemic regimen, where apixaban, rivaroxaban or LMWH can be offered if bleeding risk is not significant and interacting drugs are absent.

Platelets. Medically ill patients with cancer should not receive prophylaxis if their platelet count falls below 50,000 per microlitre. Bleeding attributable to a low count by itself is uncommon when the count stays over 50,000, and it tends to appear only below 10,000 to 20,000. A 2022 review summarises 2018 guidance from the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis, which backs full-dose anticoagulation when the count exceeds 50,000, a reduced dose from 25,000 to 50,000, and holding anticoagulation below 25,000.

Treatment. Cancer-associated clots are treated with LMWH, an injected heparin fragment, or with a DOAC such as apixaban or edoxaban, a fixed-dose tablet. Standard references prefer LMWH alone when the cancer is gastrointestinal. Apixaban is given as 10 mg twice daily for 7 days, then 5 mg twice daily, and it was not inferior to dalteparin. Treatment continues until the patient is declared cancer-free.

Pulmonary embolism. Massive PE involves hypotension and extensive thrombus covering at least half of the pulmonary vasculature, in roughly 5 to 10 percent of patients. Fibrinolysis with alteplase is standard for massive PE. Submassive PE has normal pressure with right ventricular dysfunction, makes up about 20 to 25 percent of cases, and fibrinolysis is controversial there.

Oxygen. Most minor PE patients are not hypoxaemic. Under the 2017 British Thoracic Society oxygen guidance, PE patients without a hypercapnic risk should aim for 94 to 98 percent saturation. Those carrying that risk, where carbon dioxide accumulates, fall into an 88 to 92 percent band.

Catheters. A long-term central venous catheter can clot. Standard references advise removal with anticoagulation to prevent embolization. Early superior vena cava syndrome may be cleared by fibrinolysis without losing the catheter. Pleural catheters sometimes need thrombolysis to stay open.

Occlusion. A fibrin sheath forms on most central lines within two weeks but usually does not block flow. A clot inside the lumen can block the line entirely, and such clots make up 5 to 25 percent of occlusions. In a 2012 review, alteplase cleared 52 percent of blocked catheters within 30 minutes and 86 percent overall.
