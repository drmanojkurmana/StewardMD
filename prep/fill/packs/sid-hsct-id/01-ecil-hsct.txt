ECIL and IDSA transplant infection guidance: infection risk after haematopoietic stem cell transplantation follows immune recovery phases. The pre-engraftment phase covers days 0 to 30 after transplant. Profound neutropenia and mucosal injury dominate, and bacteria such as coagulase-negative staphylococci and viridans streptococci, plus candida species and herpes simplex virus, are the leading threats. Catheter-associated bloodstream infection is common in this interval. The post-engraftment phase covers days 30 to 100. T-cell and natural killer recovery lags, and CMV, HHV-6, toxoplasma and invasive Aspergillus infections emerge. Herpes simplex virus persists as a risk where acyclovir is omitted. The late phase begins after day 100. Humoral recovery is incomplete, and encapsulated organisms such as Streptococcus pneumoniae and Haemophilus influenzae, late CMV, varicella zoster virus and invasive molds remain important. Graft-versus-host disease and its steroid therapy prolong every risk. Allogeneic recipients carry broader and longer risk than autologous recipients. Chimeric antigen receptor T-cell therapy causes prolonged B-cell aplasia with hypogammaglobulinaemia and recurrent sinopulmonary infection. Cord blood grafts engraft later and infect more. Donor and recipient CMV serostatus, prior CMV reactivation, T-cell depletion, myeloablative conditioning and graft-versus-host disease all raise late risk.

Surveillance centres on weekly CMV PCR from engraftment through day 100 in high-risk pairs, continued beyond day 100 when graft-versus-host disease persists. A rising viral load triggers pre-emptive therapy before organ disease appears. Febrile neutropenia needs blood cultures and empiric cefepime plus vancomycin, with the first dose given within 60 minutes. Pneumonia workup pairs CT with bronchoalveolar lavage PCR and galactomannan. Colitis needs endoscopy with biopsy. Encephalitis after engraftment points to HHV-6 PCR in blood and cerebrospinal fluid. Adenovirus in children is tracked by PCR viral load in blood, urine and stool with hemorrhagic cystitis and pneumonia. Serial immunoglobulin levels and CD4 counts track humoral and cellular recovery and time revaccination.

CMV prevention in seropositive recipients uses letermovir 480 mg once daily to day 100, reduced to 240 mg daily when given with cyclosporine. Letermovir prevents but does not treat established CMV disease. Treatment of active CMV uses valganciclovir 900 mg twice daily or ganciclovir 5 mg per kg twice daily, switched to foscarnet 60 mg per kg three times daily for resistance or marrow toxicity, with hydration and electrolyte monitoring for renal injury. Refractory or resistant CMV is salvaged with maribavir. Herpesvirus prophylaxis uses acyclovir 800 mg twice daily for 1 year. Pneumocystis prophylaxis uses cotrimoxazole 960 mg three times weekly with TMP-SMX double strength, continued until CD4 recovery. Mold prophylaxis uses fluconazole 400 mg daily, or posaconazole 300 mg daily, or voriconazole when mold risk is high. Azole antifungals raise tacrolimus and cyclosporine levels. Adenovirus disease is treated with cidofovir given with probenecid. Hypogammaglobulinaemia is corrected with IVIG when immunoglobulin falls and sinopulmonary infections recur. Live vaccines stay deferred until immune recovery allows, and revaccination with inactivated vaccines is planned after 6 months. Ganciclovir myelosuppression can deepen cytopenias. Relapse of the underlying malignancy and second malignancy follow prolonged suppression. Non-relapse mortality concentrates in mismatched grafts, CMV mismatched pairs and graft-versus-host disease. Durable control tracks CD4 recovery above 200 cells per microlitre with immunoglobulin normalisation. Cerebrospinal fluid examination and lung biopsy resolve ambiguous cases.
