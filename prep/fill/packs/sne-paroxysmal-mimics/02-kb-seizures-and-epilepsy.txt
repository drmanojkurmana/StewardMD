# Seizures and Epilepsy
Seizures and Epilepsy, Nervous system.
Aliases: Epilepsy; Seizure disorder; Convulsive disorder.
# Pathophysiology
Seizures arise from a shift in the normal balance between neuronal excitation and inhibition in the CNS. Focal seizure activity begins in a discrete cortical region and can invade surrounding cortex; the hallmark is the electrographic spike produced by near-simultaneous bursting of many excitatory neurons (hypersynchronization). At the cellular level, a prolonged paroxysmal depolarization shift driven by calcium influx opens voltage-dependent sodium channels and generates repetitive action potentials, followed by a GABA- or potassium-channel-mediated hyperpolarizing afterpotential. Spread is normally limited by intact hyperpolarization and feedforward surround inhibition; recruitment of neighboring neurons occurs through rising extracellular potassium, presynaptic calcium accumulation enhancing transmitter release, NMDA-receptor activation, and ephaptic effects, allowing propagation via local and commissural (corpus callosum) pathways. Epilepsy results from a dynamic interplay of endogenous (often genetic, maturational) factors, epileptogenic factors (e.g., severe head trauma, stroke, infection, neurodegeneration, developmental malformations), and precipitating factors (sleep deprivation, stress, hormonal change, toxins, photic stimulation). Many gene mutations affecting ion channels (e.g., SCN1A, KCNQ2), neurotransmitter receptors, and metabolic transporters (e.g., SLC2A1/GLUT-1) underlie specific syndromes.
# Prognosis
Outcome varies widely by syndrome and etiology. Typical absence epilepsy and juvenile myoclonic epilepsy generally respond well to medication (though JME remission is uncommon), whereas mesial temporal lobe epilepsy, Lennox-Gastaut syndrome, and epilepsies tied to structural lesions or developmental delay are often refractory. About half of patients do not respond to the first medication, and roughly 30% have ongoing seizures despite optimal drug therapy. Favorable predictors for successfully discontinuing therapy include 1-5 years of complete control, a single seizure type (generalized carrying a better prognosis than focal), normal neurologic examination and intelligence, no family history of epilepsy, and a normal EEG; a normal EEG generally implies a better prognosis. People with epilepsy have a death rate roughly 2-3 times that of matched controls, mostly attributable to the underlying cause but also to accidents, status epilepticus, and SUDEP.
