# Autoimmune Polyendocrine Syndromes
Autoimmune Polyendocrine Syndromes, Endocrine.
Aliases: APS; APECED; autoimmune polyglandular syndrome; polyglandular deficiency syndrome.
# Pathophysiology
APS-1 is a monogenic, classically autosomal recessive disorder caused by loss-of-function mutations in the AIRE (autoimmune regulator) gene on chromosome 21. AIRE is most highly expressed in thymic medullary epithelial cells, where it promotes expression of tissue-specific self-antigens; its loss reduces this expression and is thought to allow autoreactive T cells to escape central (thymic) deletion. Over 100 mutations are described, with higher frequency in certain ethnic groups (Iranian Jews, Sardinians, Finns, Norwegians, Irish). Newly recognized autosomal dominant mutations cluster in the PHD1 domain and tend to cause later-onset, less aggressive disease. Patients develop anticytokine autoantibodies (anti-IL-17A, IL-17F, IL-22, and type 1 interferons). In contrast, APS-2 is polygenic, with the dominant risk in the HLA complex on chromosome 6: Addison's disease in APS-2 is strongly linked to HLA-DR3 and HLA-DR4, with additional contributions from HLA-B8, DQ2, DQ8, and DRB1 subtypes. Non-HLA immune genes including MIC-A (the 5.1 allele strongly associated with Addison's), PTPN22, CTLA4, and IL-2R-alpha variants further modulate susceptibility. Organ damage in both syndromes is mediated by organ-specific autoantibodies and autoreactive T cells against targets such as 21-hydroxylase, GAD65, thyroid peroxidase, transglutaminase, and intrinsic factor.
# Prognosis
These are chronic, progressive disorders in which individual component diseases continue to emerge over years to decades, so cumulative prevalence rises with age and lifelong surveillance is required. APS-1 typically presents earliest (often infancy) and its chronic candidiasis is nearly always present and poorly responsive to treatment. In APS-2, autoantibodies can precede clinical disease by many years, allowing early detection; about half of patients with Addison's disease develop an additional autoimmune disorder over their lifetime, and individuals with two or more islet autoantibodies have a near-100% lifetime risk of clinical type 1 diabetes. With timely hormone replacement and monitoring, prognosis for individual endocrinopathies is generally favorable, but unrecognized adrenal insufficiency or precipitated adrenal crisis is potentially fatal.
# Severity classification
No formal severity grading is defined; classification is by type (APS-1 vs APS-2, with proposed APS-3/APS-4 subtypes) based on the constellation of component autoimmune disorders. For type 1 diabetes risk within APS-2, presence of two or more islet autoantibodies with normal glucose tolerance is staged as stage 1 disease.
# Clinical pearls
Autoimmune polyendocrine syndromes (APS) are divided into two principal categories: APS type 1 (APS-1) and APS type 2 (APS-2); some authors further split APS-2 into APS-3 and APS-4, but this added subdivision adds little to understanding pathogenesis or guiding management.
Although the underlying autoimmunity predominantly targets endocrine glands, many nonendocrine tissues can be affected, so a range of nonendocrine disorders is recognized as part of these syndromes.
APS-1 (APECED) is defined by a triad of chronic mucocutaneous candidiasis, hypoparathyroidism, and Addison's disease; the diagnosis is usually made clinically when two of these three major components are present.
APS-1 is rare (<500 reported cases) and classically is autosomal recessive, caused by mutations in the AIRE gene on chromosome 21; AIRE is most highly expressed in thymic medullary epithelial cells where it drives expression of tissue-specific self-antigens such as insulin.
Anticytokine autoantibodies are characteristic of APS-1: anti-IL-17A, anti-IL-17F, and anti-IL-22 underlie the mucocutaneous candidiasis, while detection of anti-interferon alpha and anti-interferon omega antibodies identifies nearly 100% of APS-1 cases and is not seen in other autoimmune disorders.
