# Juvenile Sjogren syndrome
Juvenile Sjogren syndrome, Rheumatology / Paediatrics, Paediatric Rheumatology.
Aliases: childhood Sjogren syndrome; paediatric Sjogren syndrome; juvenile Sjögren syndrome; juvenile sicca syndrome.
# Pathophysiology
Juvenile Sjogren syndrome is characterised by lymphocytic infiltration of exocrine glands, chiefly the salivary and lacrimal glands, driven by autoreactive T and B cells. Glandular epithelial activation, type I interferon signalling and B-cell hyperactivity lead to progressive glandular dysfunction and the production of autoantibodies such as anti-Ro/SSA and anti-La/SSB. Chronic B-cell stimulation underlies the hypergammaglobulinaemia and the long-term predisposition to lymphoma, while immune complex and interferon-driven inflammation account for extraglandular features affecting joints, kidneys, blood and the nervous system.
# Prognosis
Childhood disease is often dominated by recurrent parotitis and may follow a relatively indolent glandular course, but systemic involvement and the lifelong, if low, lymphoma risk require ongoing surveillance. Sicca symptoms tend to persist and can affect dental and ocular health over time.
# Clinical pearls
A chronic autoimmune exocrinopathy targeting salivary and lacrimal glands, but children often present differently from adults
Recurrent bilateral parotid gland swelling (parotitis) is a common and sometimes presenting feature in children, more so than the classic sicca symptoms
Dry eyes and dry mouth (sicca) may be underreported by children, so a low threshold for objective testing is needed
Extraglandular manifestations include arthralgia, arthritis, renal tubular acidosis, cytopenias and neurological involvement
