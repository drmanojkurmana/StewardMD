# Ventricular Septal Defect
Ventricular Septal Defect, Paediatric cardiology, Paediatrics.
Aliases: VSD; ventricular septal defects.
# Pathophysiology
A communication in the ventricular septum allows blood to shunt according to the pressure and resistance gradient between the ventricles. After birth, as pulmonary vascular resistance declines, left-to-right shunting increases, raising pulmonary blood flow and volume-loading the left atrium and left ventricle. A large, sustained shunt increases pulmonary artery pressure and flow, and over time medial hypertrophy and intimal changes in the pulmonary vasculature raise resistance irreversibly; when pulmonary resistance exceeds systemic, the shunt reverses and produces cyanosis (Eisenmenger syndrome).
# Prognosis
Small defects have an excellent outlook and often close spontaneously. Large defects, if repaired before irreversible pulmonary vascular disease develops, generally have a good outcome; delayed closure risks fixed pulmonary hypertension and a markedly worse prognosis.
# Clinical pearls
The most common congenital heart malformation, an opening in the interventricular septum that permits shunting between the ventricles; most are membranous or muscular in location.
The direction and size of the shunt depend on the defect size and the relative resistances of the pulmonary and systemic circulations, so many defects become clinically apparent only as pulmonary resistance falls in the first weeks of life.
Small defects produce a loud, often harsh holosystolic murmur at the lower left sternal border but little haemodynamic burden, and many close spontaneously.
