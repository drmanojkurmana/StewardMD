Extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy, abbreviated ESWL, fragments urinary stones with focused acoustic shock waves that travel through the skin while the patient lies coupled to a lithotripter. The shock wave is the active principle, and lithotripsy is the stone breaking that follows. Ideal renal stones lie outside the lower pole, measure less than 20 mm in largest diameter, and show attenuation under 1000 Hounsfield units on noncontrast computed tomography. Softer stones under 1000 Hounsfield units and smaller burdens under 20 mm clear best, while hard brushite or cystine stones resist fragmentation.

ESWL is absolutely contraindicated in pregnancy, because shock waves and fluoroscopy endanger the foetus, and in uncorrected bleeding diathesis, because perirenal haematoma follows easily. Active untreated urinary tract infection must be sterilised first, since fragment passage can precipitate urosepsis. Severe skeletal malformations that prevent positioning or targeting also forbid treatment, as does an uncorrected coagulopathy.

Steinstrasse, German for stone street, describes a column of unpassable stone fragments that stack in the ureter after ESWL and block urine flow. Most episodes pass with conservative care and analgesia, but persistent obstruction, fever, or a solitary kidney demands a JJ stent (a double J internal ureteral splint) or ureteroscopy, abbreviated URS, to restore drainage.

The Holmium:YAG laser is the workhorse lithotripter of modern ureteroscopy. It emits at 2100 nm, acts by a photothermal mechanism that vaporises water trapped in stone pores, and penetrates only 0.4 mm into surrounding fluid, so the urothelium stays safe. It fragments every stone composition, including hard calcium oxalate monohydrate and cystine.

The thulium fiber laser, abbreviated TFL, emits at 1940 nm, nearer the peak of water absorption, so it dusts stones with less retropulsion (less backward stone propulsion) and makes finer dust. Its fibres are slim, commonly 150 to 200 micrometres in diameter, which preserves deflection of the flexible ureteroscope in the lower pole and improves irrigation.

Flexible ureterorenoscopy passes a deflectable endoscope through the urethra, bladder, and ureter into the renal pelvis. A ureteral access sheath, a hollow conduit placed over a guidewire in 10/12F or 12/14F calibres, keeps intrarenal pelvic pressure below 30 cmH2O during prolonged lasing. Low pressure prevents pyelovenous backflow, the reflux of irrigant into veins, and lowers postoperative sepsis.

Lower pole caliceal anatomy governs fragment clearance after ESWL because chips must float uphill against gravity. An infundibular angle below 30 degrees, an infundibular length above 30 mm, and an infundibular width under 5 mm each trap fragments and favour ureteroscopy over shock waves for lower pole stones.

A ureteral stent left after uncomplicated ureteroscopy is discretionary when the stone was small and the ureter was already pre-stented, but stenting is strongly advised after ureteral dilatation, after access sheath usage, or after any mucosal injury, to guard against obstruction from oedema or clots.
