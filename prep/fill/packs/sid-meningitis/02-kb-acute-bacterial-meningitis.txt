# Acute bacterial meningitis (adult)
Adults
Emergency department, ward, ICU
Blood cultures, then IV ceftriaxone plus vancomycin with dexamethasone within 1 hour of arrival; add amoxicillin when Listeria is possible (age over 50, pregnancy, immunocompromise). Do the LP at once unless CT criteria are met, and never let imaging delay antibiotics.
Aliases: ABM; bacterial meningitis; pyogenic meningitis; meningococcal meningitis; pneumococcal meningitis; Listeria meningitis; CT before LP; meningitis dexamethasone; meningococcal prophylaxis.
# Recognise
The classic triad (fever, neck stiffness, altered mental status) is present in under half of adults, but almost all have at least two of headache, fever, neck stiffness and altered consciousness.
A petechial or purpuric rash suggests meningococcal disease, which can progress to septic shock within hours.
Listeria risk: age over 50, pregnancy, immunocompromise (steroids, transplant, cancer, HIV), diabetes, alcohol misuse, chronic liver or kidney disease.
Indian differentials to keep in mind: tuberculous meningitis (subacute, cranial nerve palsies), cryptococcal meningitis (HIV), scrub typhus, leptospirosis, cerebral malaria, and viral encephalitis (HSV, Japanese encephalitis).
# First hour
Take 2 sets of blood cultures, then give IV antibiotics and dexamethasone within 1 hour of arrival. Do the LP first only if it can be done immediately; never delay the first dose for LP or imaging.
CT before LP only if (ESCMID): focal neurological deficit (excluding cranial nerve palsies), new-onset seizures, GCS below 10, or severe immunocompromise (HIV, transplant, immunosuppressive therapy).
If CT is needed: cultures, dexamethasone and antibiotics first, then CT, then LP if safe.
Also defer LP with signs of impending herniation, shock, significant coagulopathy or thrombocytopenia, extensive purpura, uncontrolled seizures, or skin infection at the LP site.
Resuscitate as for sepsis if shocked; secure the airway if GCS is 8 or less; treat seizures promptly. Use droplet precautions until 24 hours of effective antibiotics if meningococcal disease is possible.
# Investigations
CSF: opening pressure, cell count and differential, protein, glucose with paired plasma glucose, Gram stain, culture, and PCR or a multiplex panel where available. Save an extra tube for TB, cryptococcal antigen or viral PCR.
Typical bacterial CSF: neutrophilic pleocytosis (often above 1000 cells/mm3), CSF to blood glucose ratio below 0.4, raised protein. A negative Gram stain does not exclude bacterial meningitis.
Blood: cultures x 2, CBC, CRP or procalcitonin, renal and liver function, sodium, glucose, coagulation, lactate; blood meningococcal PCR if rash or sepsis. HIV test; malaria test and scrub typhus serology where endemic.
Repeat LP only if there is no clinical improvement at 48 hours, or for pneumococcal meningitis with reduced cephalosporin susceptibility treated with dexamethasone (ICMR).
# Empirical antibiotics
Age 16 to 50 without Listeria risk: ceftriaxone 2 g IV every 12 hours PLUS vancomycin (ICMR 2022). Indian surveillance shows substantial penicillin and some cephalosporin resistance in pneumococci.
Age over 50, pregnancy, immunocompromise or other Listeria risk: add amoxicillin or ampicillin 2 g IV every 4 hours to ceftriaxone plus vancomycin. Ceftriaxone does not cover Listeria.
Beta-lactam allergy without anaphylaxis: meropenem 2 g IV every 8 hours plus vancomycin (ICMR alternative). Anaphylaxis: get urgent microbiology advice; options include chloramphenicol, with co-trimoxazole for Listeria cover.
Healthcare-associated meningitis (neurosurgery, shunt, external ventricular drain, penetrating trauma) needs vancomycin plus an anti-pseudomonal agent such as cefepime or meropenem by local antibiogram; manage with neurosurgery.
Narrow once the organism is known: stop vancomycin if the pneumococcus is cephalosporin-susceptible; amoxicillin for Listeria; ceftriaxone or benzylpenicillin for susceptible meningococcus.
# Dexamethasone and duration
Dexamethasone 10 mg IV every 6 hours for 4 days (0.15 mg/kg), first dose just before or with the first antibiotic dose.
If antibiotics have already started, give dexamethasone as soon as possible, ideally within 4 hours of the first antibiotic dose; NICE advises not starting it more than 12 hours after.
Continue for 4 days in pneumococcal or H. influenzae meningitis or if the cause is unknown. Stop if a non-bacterial cause is confirmed; ESCMID advises stopping in Listeria meningitis. Do not use steroids routinely for meningococcal septicaemia.
Duration (ESCMID): N. meningitidis 7 days (NICE 2024 allows stopping at 5 days if recovered); S. pneumoniae 10 to 14 days; H. influenzae 7 to 10 days; L. monocytogenes at least 21 days; gram-negative bacilli 21 days (ICMR).
Culture-negative meningitis with a bacterial CSF profile: usually 10 to 14 days (ICMR). There is no need to repeat LP before stopping in a patient who is improving.
# Monitoring
Neurological observations (GCS, pupils) hourly at first; watch for seizures, hyponatraemia, hydrocephalus, cerebral oedema, venous sinus thrombosis and subdural empyema.
Keep normoglycaemia and normonatraemia; avoid hypotonic fluids. Do not restrict fluids in a hypovolaemic patient.
Worsening or no improvement by 48 hours: repeat imaging (MRI preferred) and LP, and review the diagnosis and regimen.
Arrange an audiology assessment before or soon after discharge; early referral matters because post-meningitic cochlear ossification can prevent implantation.
# Public health and contacts
Notify suspected meningococcal and H. influenzae disease to the district surveillance unit (IDSP).
Close contacts of meningococcal disease (household and intimate contacts in the 7 days before onset), ideally within 24 hours: ciprofloxacin 500 mg orally once, or rifampicin 600 mg orally every 12 hours for 2 days, or ceftriaxone 250 mg IM once (preferred in pregnancy).
Healthcare workers need prophylaxis only after direct unprotected exposure to respiratory secretions (for example intubation or suction without a mask).
The index patient needs carriage eradication if treated with an agent other than ceftriaxone or cefotaxime.
# Pitfalls
Delaying antibiotics for CT or LP: outcome worsens with each hour of delay.
Omitting Listeria cover in older, pregnant or immunosuppressed adults.
Stopping antibiotics because the CSF culture is negative after prior antibiotics; use the cell profile, Gram stain and PCR.
Missing tuberculous or cryptococcal meningitis in a subacute illness, lymphocytic CSF or HIV infection.
