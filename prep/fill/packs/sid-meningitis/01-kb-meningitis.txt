# Acute Bacterial Meningitis
Acute Bacterial Meningitis, Central Nervous System.
# Pathogens
S. pneumoniae and N. meningitidis are the leading causes of community-acquired bacterial meningitis across most age groups. Listeria is specifically added in neonates, those over 50, pregnant patients, and the immunocompromised, since standard empiric regimens do not cover it.
Very likely: Streptococcus pneumoniae; Neisseria meningitidis.
Likely: Listeria monocytogenes (neonates, elderly, immunocompromised, pregnant).
Possible: Haemophilus influenzae (unvaccinated populations); Gram-negative bacilli (post-neurosurgery, immunocompromised).
Red flags: Immediate empiric antibiotics required.

# Pathophysiology
Bacterial meningitis is an acute purulent infection of the subarachnoid space accompanied by a CNS inflammatory reaction that can cause decreased consciousness, seizures, raised intracranial pressure and stroke; meninges, subarachnoid space and brain parenchyma are often jointly involved (meningoencephalitis). The leading organisms (S. pneumoniae, N. meningitidis) first colonize the nasopharynx, cross or breach the epithelium into the bloodstream, evade neutrophil phagocytosis and complement killing via their polysaccharide capsule, and reach the CSF through the choroid plexus or cerebral capillary endothelium. They multiply readily in CSF because normal CSF has few leukocytes and little complement/immunoglobulin, so opsonization and phagocytosis are poor. Much of the neurologic injury comes from the host inflammatory response rather than direct bacterial damage, so injury can progress even after antibiotics sterilize the CSF. Lysis of bacteria releases cell-wall components (gram-negative LPS; pneumococcal teichoic acid and peptidoglycan) that drive production of TNF-alpha, IL-1beta, chemokines and other mediators by microglia, astrocytes, monocytes, endothelium and CSF leukocytes. These cytokines increase blood-brain barrier permeability (vasogenic edema and protein leakage), the proteinaceous/leukocytic exudate obstructs CSF flow and resorption (obstructive and communicating hydrocephalus, interstitial edema), and neutrophil degranulation plus reactive oxygen/nitrogen species and excitatory amino acids produce cytotoxic edema and cell death (notably in the hippocampal dentate gyrus). Cerebral blood flow rises early then falls with loss of autoregulation; vasculitis and thrombosis of arteries, cortical veins and dural sinuses cause ischemia/infarction. The combined interstitial, vasogenic and cytotoxic edema raises ICP and can lead to coma and herniation.
# Prognosis
Case-fatality varies by organism: roughly 3-7% for H. influenzae, N. meningitidis or group B streptococcal meningitis, about 15% for Listeria, and around 20% for pneumococcal meningitis. Risk of death rises with decreased consciousness on admission, seizures within 24 h, signs of raised ICP, age (infancy or >50 years), comorbidities such as shock or need for mechanical ventilation, and delay in starting treatment. A markedly low CSF glucose (<40 mg/dL) and very high CSF protein (>300 mg/dL) predict worse outcomes in some series. About 25% of survivors have moderate-to-severe sequelae, commonly decreased intellectual function, memory impairment, seizures, sensorineural hearing loss with dizziness, and gait disturbance.
# Severity classification
For distinguishing bacterial from aseptic meningitis in children, the prospectively validated Bacterial Meningitis Score is cited: very low probability (NPV ~99.7%) when all of negative CSF Gram stain, CSF neutrophils <1000/uL, CSF protein <80 mg/dL, peripheral ANC <10,000/uL, and no seizures are met.
# Clinical pearls
The classic triad of fever, headache and nuchal rigidity is useful but each component occurs in over 80% of adult cases while the full triad is frequently incomplete, so its absence does not exclude bacterial meningitis.
A reduced level of consciousness, ranging from lethargy to coma, is present in more than three-quarters of patients; nausea, vomiting and photophobia are also common.
Nuchal rigidity is the characteristic sign of meningeal irritation (neck resists passive flexion), but Kernig's and Brudzinski's signs have uncertain sensitivity/specificity and may be absent in the very young, the elderly, the immunocompromised or those with depressed mental status.
Coexisting cervical spine disease in older patients can produce false-positive neck stiffness.
Whenever bacterial meningitis is suspected, draw blood cultures immediately and start empirical antibiotics plus adjunctive dexamethasone without delay; if neuroimaging is needed before LP, give antibiotics after blood cultures rather than waiting.
Antibiotics given a few hours before LP do not meaningfully change CSF WBC count or glucose and are unlikely to prevent Gram-stain visualization or PCR detection of bacterial nucleic acid, so a delayed LP after starting therapy still has diagnostic value.
Dexamethasone should ideally be given about 20 minutes before, or at the latest with, the first antibiotic dose, because steroids blunt macrophage/microglial TNF-alpha production only if given before those cells are activated by endotoxin; benefit is unlikely if started more than 6 hours after antibiotics.
Petechial or purpuric skin lesions are an important clue to meningococcal infection and, if present, should be biopsied (Gram stain of the lesion may reveal the organism).
Profound depression of consciousness (stupor/coma), seizures or focal neurologic signs point away from uncomplicated viral meningitis and suggest encephalitis or bacterial/parameningeal disease.
# Additional differentials
Herpes simplex virus encephalitis (headache, fever, altered consciousness, focal deficits, seizures; distinguished by CSF profile, MRI temporal-lobe changes and HSV CSF PCR)
Subacute meningitis presenting acutely: M. tuberculosis, Cryptococcus neoformans, Histoplasma, Coccidioides, Treponema pallidum
Mycoplasma pneumoniae CNS infection, which can mimic viral meningitis/encephalitis
# Infection mimics
Viral (aseptic) meningoencephalitis, especially enteroviral and HSV
Rocky Mountain spotted fever (Rickettsia rickettsii) with early maculopapular rash that can resemble meningococcemia
Ehrlichiosis/anaplasmosis (tick-borne) with fever, headache, confusion and sometimes rash
Focal suppurative CNS infections: subdural empyema, epidural empyema, brain abscess (consider when focal neurologic findings present)
Fungal, tuberculous, Listeria, Brucella, Coxiella, Leptospira meningitis (lymphocytic pleocytosis, hypoglycorrhachia may be absent early)
# Non infectious mimics
Subarachnoid hemorrhage (the major non-infectious consideration)
Medication-induced hypersensitivity (drug-induced) meningitis
Chemical meningitis from rupture of tumor contents into CSF (cystic glioma, craniopharyngioma, epidermoid/dermoid cyst)
Carcinomatous or lymphomatous (neoplastic) meningitis
Inflammatory disorders: sarcoidosis, systemic lupus erythematosus, Behcet's syndrome
Pituitary apoplexy
Uveomeningitic syndromes (Vogt-Koyanagi-Harada)
# Additional investigations
CSF opening pressure measurement: raised (>180 mmH2O) in over 90% of cases, >400 mmH2O in about 20%
CSF cell count and differential: PMN pleocytosis, >100 cells/uL in ~90%
CSF glucose and CSF/serum glucose ratio: low glucose (<40 mg/dL) and ratio <0.4 are highly suggestive (ratio also low in fungal/TB/carcinomatous meningitis)
CSF protein: elevated (>45 mg/dL) in ~90%
CSF Gram stain (organisms in >60%) and CSF culture (positive in >70-80%; required for susceptibility testing)
CSF multiplex PCR (e.g., FilmArray ME panel) for rapid detection of S. pneumoniae, N. meningitidis, E. coli, L. monocytogenes, H. influenzae, group B strep; does not include S. aureus, coagulase-negative staphylococci or many gram-negatives and cannot replace culture
MRI (preferred over CT) for cerebral edema/ischemia; gadolinium meningeal enhancement is non-specific and not diagnostic of meningitis
Skin lesion biopsy with Gram stain when petechiae/purpura are present
Serum procalcitonin and CSF protein as adjuncts to distinguish bacterial from aseptic meningitis (children)
# Red flags
Petechial or purpuric rash, which may herald fulminant meningococcal disease that can progress to death within hours of symptom onset
Signs of raised ICP: deteriorating consciousness, papilledema, dilated poorly reactive pupils, sixth-nerve palsy, decerebrate posturing, Cushing reflex (bradycardia, hypertension, irregular respirations)
Seizures at presentation or within the first 24 hours (associated with worse outcome)
Coma or markedly decreased level of consciousness on admission
Indications to obtain CT/MRI before LP: recent head trauma, depressed/abnormal consciousness, papilledema, or focal neurologic deficit (do not delay antibiotics for imaging)
# Pitfalls
Relying on the complete classic triad or on Kernig's/Brudzinski's signs to rule out meningitis; these are insensitive, particularly in elderly, very young or immunocompromised patients.
Delaying antibiotics and dexamethasone to obtain neuroimaging or LP first; treatment should not wait when bacterial meningitis is suspected.
Assuming a recent dose of antibiotics renders the LP useless; CSF chemistry, Gram stain and PCR usually remain informative for several hours.
Interpreting gadolinium meningeal enhancement on MRI as diagnostic of meningitis; it occurs with any condition that increases blood-brain barrier permeability.
Treating with dexamethasone in settings where it has not shown benefit: trials in low-income countries (late presentation, prior antibiotics, malnutrition, HIV, unproven diagnosis) failed to show efficacy, and patients with a negative CSF Gram stain and culture in those settings should not receive it.
Forgetting that neurologic injury and complications can progress even after CSF sterilization because much of the damage is immune-mediated.
Overlooking that a giving of IV glucose (e.g., D50) shortly before LP rarely alters CSF glucose unless several hours have elapsed, so a low CSF glucose remains meaningful.
# Deescalation
Once CSF culture confirms the organism, narrow to the single most targeted agent — e.g., confirmed penicillin-susceptible S. pneumoniae allows discontinuation of vancomycin; confirmed N. meningitidis allows narrowing to penicillin G or ceftriaxone alone.
# Completeness
Ceftriaxone + vancomycin covers the leading pathogens across most age groups; ampicillin must be added specifically for Listeria risk groups.
Organisms: S. pneumoniae; N. meningitidis; Listeria; H. influenzae.
Drugs: Ceftriaxone; Vancomycin; Ampicillin (if added).

# Coverage
Covers Listeria monocytogenes, which is intrinsically resistant to cephalosporins and would otherwise be missed by the standard regimen.

# Framework
Do not delay empiric antibiotics for lumbar puncture or imaging — give antibiotics immediately on clinical suspicion, then proceed to LP if safe and feasible.
Add ampicillin specifically for Listeria risk groups (age >50, pregnant, immunocompromised) — this is frequently missed and has serious consequences if omitted.
De-escalate promptly once CSF culture and Gram stain identify the specific organism and susceptibility.
