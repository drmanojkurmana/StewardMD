# Listeriosis
Listeriosis, Infectious Diseases.
Aliases: Listeria monocytogenes infection.
# Pathophysiology
L. monocytogenes is a ubiquitous, nonsporulating, facultatively anaerobic gram-positive rod (showing small zones of beta-haemolysis and characteristic tumbling motility) that lives in soil and decaying vegetation and can grow at refrigerator temperatures as low as 4 C and tolerate low-pH/high-salt environments. Infection follows ingestion of contaminated food; the infectious dose is likely very low in the severely immunocompromised, and raised gastric pH (e.g. from proton-pump inhibitors) probably promotes gut survival. After transcytosis across the intestinal epithelium the organism spreads via mesenteric lymph nodes and bloodstream to its target organs (liver and spleen) and can cross the blood-brain barrier and the placenta. Virulence factors include internalins (InlA, InlB) for invasion of nonphagocytic cells, listeriolysin O and phospholipases for escape from the phagocytic vacuole, and ActA for actin-based cell-to-cell spread that evades antibodies and complement. Iron promotes growth, explaining the association of listeriosis with iron-overload states such as haemochromatosis. Host defence is predominantly cell-mediated (interferon-gamma, TNF-alpha, and CD8+ T cells), accounting for the predilection for those with impaired cellular immunity.
# Prognosis
Invasive listeriosis carries substantial mortality: 18% of reported cases die and virtually all are hospitalised. In a large French (MONALISA) cohort the 3-month mortality was 46% for bacteraemia and 30% for neurolisteriosis; death was associated with multiorgan failure, neoplasia, worsening of preexisting comorbidities, monocytopenia (<200 cells/uL), older age and female sex, and (for neurolisteriosis) a positive blood culture and adjunctive dexamethasone use. Nearly half of neurolisteriosis survivors have long-term neurologic impairment. In pregnancy-associated disease, fetal/neonatal outcomes are poor: in a cohort of 107 pregnancies 24% ended in fetal loss, 45% in premature birth and only ~5% had a normal delivery and postpartum course; overall 3% of 189 live-born infants died.
# Severity classification
No formal severity score. Severity is judged clinically by syndrome, with prognostic markers including multiorgan failure and monocytopenia.
# Clinical pearls
Listeriosis is acquired almost always via foodborne transmission; implicated foods are refrigerated ready-to-eat items (deli/delicatessen meats, hot dogs), soft cheeses made with raw/unpasteurised milk, unpasteurised dairy, raw produce (packaged salad, sprouts, enoki mushrooms, cantaloupe, caramel apples, frozen vegetables) and pate/meat spreads.
Major risk groups for invasive disease are pregnant women and their neonates, older adults (risk rises with each decade over age 59), and immunocompromised persons (impaired cellular immunity from haematologic malignancy, transplant, HIV, glucocorticoids/immunosuppressants, or TNF-alpha inhibitors).
Bacteraemia without a focus is the most common manifestation of invasive listeriosis; presents with fever, chills, myalgias and arthralgias sometimes preceded by nausea/diarrhoea.
Neurolisteriosis is the second most common invasive manifestation; in a French cohort 84% of neurolisteriosis cases presented as meningoencephalitis (meningitis plus altered mental status, seizures, or focal findings).
Compared with other bacterial meningitides, CSF pleocytosis and neutrophil predominance are typically LESS pronounced; ~60-75% have CSF WBC <1000/uL and only ~30-40% have low CSF glucose, so normal-ish CSF does not exclude Listeria.
Empiric bacterial-meningitis cover (ceftriaxone + vancomycin) does NOT cover Listeria; add ampicillin for Listeria coverage in patients >50 years old or with comorbid/immunocompromising disease.
Rhombencephalitis (brainstem/cerebellar encephalitis) disproportionately affects otherwise healthy older adults, classically biphasic (fever and headache, then asymmetric cranial nerve palsies, ataxia, tremor, hemiparesis or hemisensory deficits); nearly half develop respiratory failure.
MRI is superior to CT for diagnosis of neurolisteriosis, including rhombencephalitis.
Diagnosis is by isolation from a normally sterile site (blood, CSF, amniotic fluid, placental or fetal tissue); blood cultures are positive only about half the time in pregnant women, and CSF multiplex PCR panels and MALDI-TOF aid rapid identification.
Pregnant women usually have only mild flulike illness and rarely develop neurolisteriosis or death, yet transmit infection transplacentally to the fetus with high fetal morbidity; prepartum antibiotics started >=1 day before delivery improve the chance of a healthy infant.
In invasive disease virtually all patients are hospitalised and 18% die; consider listeriosis acquired in hospital and investigate hospital-provided food when a hospitalised (especially immunocompromised) patient develops new symptoms.
TMP-SMX given as Pneumocystis jirovecii prophylaxis (e.g. in HIV or transplant recipients) also helps prevent listeriosis.
# Infection mimics
Other causes of community-acquired bacterial meningitis (S. pneumoniae, N. meningitidis, H. influenzae, group B streptococcus)
Fungal or tuberculous meningitis (low CSF glucose with mononuclear pleocytosis can mimic Listeria)
Brain abscess or other space-occupying CNS infection (Listeria abscess)
Other bacteraemic/septic illnesses presenting with fever without a focus
# Non infectious mimics
Primary or metastatic CNS malignancy (Listeria brain abscess may be misdiagnosed as tumour)
Neoplasia / haematologic malignancy presenting with fever (also a risk factor and prognostic comorbidity)
# Additional investigations
Blood cultures (note positive only ~half the time in pregnancy)
CSF culture and Gram stain (may show gram-positive rods, but often no organisms or misleading gram-positive cocci/diplococci/diphtheroids)
Culture of normally sterile sites: amniotic fluid, placental tissue, fetal tissue
Multiplex PCR panel on CSF (includes L. monocytogenes as a target)
MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry for rapid isolate identification
MRI brain (superior to CT for neurolisteriosis and rhombencephalitis)
Selective enrichment medium (PALCAM Listeria Selective Agar or Oxford Agar) for non-sterile specimens / outbreak work-up
Whole-genome sequencing for outbreak investigation
# Red flags
Signs of meningitis with altered mental status, seizures or focal neurologic deficits (meningoencephalitis)
Brainstem/cerebellar signs - asymmetric cranial nerve palsies, ataxia, tremor, hemiparesis, hemisensory deficits (rhombencephalitis), with respiratory failure in nearly half
Fever or signs of fetal distress in a pregnant woman (meconium-stained amniotic fluid, abnormal fetal heart rate)
Multiorgan failure and monocytopenia (<200 cells/uL) - markers associated with death
# Pitfalls
Cephalosporins are NOT effective against L. monocytogenes; standard empiric meningitis cover (ceftriaxone +/- vancomycin) misses Listeria, so ampicillin must be added empirically in patients >50 years or with comorbid/immunocompromising disease.
Febrile gastroenteritis form is rarely diagnosed because Listeria is not detected by routine stool cultures or rapid stool tests, and stool culture is NOT indicated in evaluating invasive listeriosis.
Gram-variable appearance as cocci, diplococci or diphtheroids can obscure the diagnosis; Listeria must be distinguished from other gram-positive rods, especially diphtheroids.
Relatively bland CSF (WBC often <1000/uL, frequently no organisms on Gram stain, only ~30-40% with low glucose) can falsely reassure - do not exclude Listeria meningitis on CSF profile alone.
Brain abscess due to Listeria may be misdiagnosed as primary or metastatic malignancy; CSF can be normal when abscess occurs without concurrent meningitis.
Adjunctive dexamethasone is associated with reduced survival in neurolisteriosis and should be avoided; it is also not recommended for established Listeria meningitis.
Treatment failures have been reported with meropenem despite in vitro susceptibility, and TMP-SMX resistance occurs - perform susceptibility testing if TMP-SMX is considered.
Testing/treatment is NOT indicated for an asymptomatic person who ate a recalled product, even if they have risk factors.
# Management
Give intravenous ampicillin or amoxicillin as first-line therapy, adding an aminoglycoside such as gentamicin for synergy in severe invasive disease
Use co-trimoxazole as the principal alternative in penicillin-allergic patients
Treat neurolisteriosis, endocarditis and immunocompromised patients with prolonged antibiotic courses
Add empirical ampicillin to standard meningitis cover in older or immunocompromised patients, since ceftriaxone plus vancomycin does not treat Listeria
Provide supportive care with fluid resuscitation and neurocritical care for meningoencephalitis and rhombencephalitis
Treat pregnant women urgently to prevent foetal loss and neonatal infection, with paediatric assessment of the neonate
