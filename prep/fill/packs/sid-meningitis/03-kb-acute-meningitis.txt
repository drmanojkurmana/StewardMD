# Acute Meningitis
Acute Meningitis, Central Nervous System.
Aliases: Bacterial meningitis; Viral meningitis; Aseptic meningitis; Meningoencephalitis.
# Pathophysiology
Bacterial meningitis is an acute purulent infection of the subarachnoid space accompanied by a CNS inflammatory reaction that may cause decreased consciousness, seizures, raised ICP, and stroke, with meninges, subarachnoid space, and parenchyma often jointly involved (meningoencephalitis). The leading organisms (S. pneumoniae, N. meningitidis) colonize the nasopharynx, cross or bypass epithelial tight junctions to enter the bloodstream, evade neutrophil phagocytosis and complement via a polysaccharide capsule, then reach the CSF through the choroid plexus or cerebral capillary endothelium. Bacteria multiply readily in CSF given its scarce leukocytes, complement, and immunoglobulin, which impair opsonization. Lysis of bacteria releases cell-wall components (LPS of gram-negatives, teichoic acid and peptidoglycan of pneumococci) that drive microglia, astrocytes, monocytes, and endothelium to produce TNF-alpha and IL-1 beta; these cytokines synergistically increase blood-brain barrier permeability (vasogenic edema, protein leakage), and the proteinaceous leukocytic exudate obstructs CSF flow and arachnoid resorption causing hydrocephalus and interstitial edema. Leukocyte adhesion and degranulation, excitatory amino acids, and reactive oxygen/nitrogen species produce cytotoxic edema and cell death (notably in the hippocampal dentate gyrus). Loss of cerebrovascular autoregulation, basal arterial narrowing/vasculitis with thrombosis, and venous/sinus thrombosis cause ischemia and infarction; combined interstitial, vasogenic, and cytotoxic edema raise ICP and may produce herniation. Viral (aseptic) meningitis produces meningeal irritation with a CSF inflammatory profile but, by definition, does not produce the parenchymal dysfunction of encephalitis.
# Prognosis
Mortality from bacterial meningitis varies by organism: about 3-7% for H. influenzae, N. meningitidis, or group B streptococci; ~15% for L. monocytogenes; and ~20% for S. pneumoniae. Risk of death rises with decreased consciousness on admission, seizures within 24 h, signs of raised ICP, age in infancy or over 50, comorbidities such as shock or need for mechanical ventilation, and delayed treatment; very low CSF glucose (<2.2 mmol/L) and very high CSF protein (>3 g/L) also predict worse outcomes. Moderate-to-severe sequelae occur in roughly 25% of survivors, including impaired intellect, memory loss, seizures, hearing loss, dizziness, and gait disturbance. In adults, recovery from viral meningitis is excellent, with only rare lingering headache, mild cognitive complaints, or asthenia; outcomes in infants/neonates under 1 year are less certain, with reported intellectual impairment, learning disability, and hearing loss.
# Severity classification
For distinguishing bacterial from aseptic meningitis, a prospectively validated bacterial meningitis score yields a negative predictive value of about 99.7% in children, and CSF protein >0.5 g/L and serum procalcitonin >0.5 ng/mL favor bacterial over aseptic meningitis.
# Clinical pearls
The classic triad of fever, headache, and nuchal rigidity each occurs in more than 80% of adults with acute bacterial meningitis, but the complete triad is frequently absent; a depressed level of consciousness is seen in over 75%.
Acute presentation can be either fulminant over hours or subacute over several days; nausea, vomiting, and photophobia are common.
Nuchal rigidity is the pathognomonic sign of meningeal irritation; Kernig's and Brudzinski's signs are classic but of uncertain sensitivity/specificity and may be absent in the very young, elderly, immunocompromised, or obtunded.
S. pneumoniae (~50%) and N. meningitidis (~25%) are the leading causes of community-acquired bacterial meningitis; group B streptococci (~15%) and L. monocytogenes (~10%) follow, with H. influenzae type b now <10%.
Petechial or purpuric skin lesions are an important clue to meningococcal infection; the rash starts as a maculopapular eruption resembling a viral exanthem then becomes petechial on trunk, extremities, mucosae, conjunctivae, and sometimes palms/soles.
Listeria meningitis should be considered in neonates, pregnant women, those over 60, and immunocompromised hosts; infection is acquired from contaminated foods such as soft cheeses, unpasteurized milk, deli meats, and uncooked hotdogs.
Seizures occur in 15-40% of bacterial meningitis cases; focal seizures suggest focal ischemia/infarction, cortical venous thrombosis with hemorrhage, or focal edema, while generalized seizures may reflect hyponatremia or cerebral anoxia.
More than 90% of bacterial meningitis patients have a CSF opening pressure above 180 mmH2O, and 20% exceed 400 mmH2O; raised ICP is the major cause of obtundation/coma.
Viral meningitis is distinguished from encephalitis by the absence of stupor, coma, marked confusion, seizures, or focal neurologic signs; their presence points toward encephalitis or another process.
Profound CNS depression, seizures, or focal deficits should never be attributed to uncomplicated viral meningitis.
A lymphocytic pleocytosis combined with a low CSF glucose should raise suspicion for fungal, tuberculous, or Listeria meningitis or noninfectious causes such as sarcoid or neoplastic meningitis rather than typical viral meningitis.
Neurologic injury in bacterial meningitis can progress even after CSF sterilization because much of the damage stems from the host inflammatory response rather than direct bacterial injury.
# Additional differentials
Herpes simplex virus encephalitis (HSV-1) and other viral meningoencephalitides
Rickettsial disease, notably Rocky Mountain spotted fever (R. rickettsii), and ehrlichiosis/anaplasmosis
Focal suppurative CNS infections: subdural or epidural empyema and brain abscess
Subarachnoid hemorrhage (the major noninfectious consideration) and pituitary apoplexy
Subacute meningitis from M. tuberculosis, Cryptococcus neoformans, Histoplasma capsulatum, Coccidioides immitis, and Treponema pallidum
Mycoplasma pneumoniae CNS infection, which can mimic viral meningitis/encephalitis
# Infection mimics
HSV encephalitis presenting with headache, fever, altered consciousness, focal deficits, and seizures
Rocky Mountain spotted fever with high fever, headache, and a rash evolving from maculopapular to petechial/purpuric, easily confused with meningococcemia
Ehrlichiosis/anaplasmosis with fever, headache, confusion, and leukopenia/thrombocytopenia with elevated transaminases
Untreated or partially treated bacterial meningitis mimicking aseptic meningitis
Early fungal, mycobacterial, or syphilitic (T. pallidum) meningitis with lymphocytic pleocytosis and not-yet-low glucose
Meningitis due to Mycoplasma, Listeria, Brucella, Coxiella, Leptospira, and Rickettsia species
# Non infectious mimics
Subarachnoid hemorrhage
Medication-induced hypersensitivity meningitis
Chemical meningitis from rupture of tumor contents (cystic glioma, craniopharyngioma, epidermoid or dermoid cyst) into CSF
Carcinomatous or lymphomatous (neoplastic) meningitis
Inflammatory disorders: sarcoidosis, systemic lupus erythematosus, Behcet's syndrome
Uveomeningitic syndromes including Vogt-Koyanagi-Harada syndrome, and pituitary apoplexy
# Additional investigations
CSF examination is the diagnostic cornerstone; classic bacterial findings are PMN pleocytosis >100/uL (90%), low glucose <2.2 mmol/L (<40 mg/dL) and/or CSF/serum glucose ratio <0.4 (~60%), elevated protein >0.45 g/L (90%), and raised opening pressure >180 mmH2O (90%)
CSF Gram stain positive in >60% and bacterial culture positive in >70-80%; culture remains required for susceptibility testing
Blood cultures should be obtained immediately when bacterial meningitis is suspected
CSF multiplex PCR (e.g., FilmArray Meningitis/Encephalitis panel) detects S. pneumoniae, N. meningitidis, E. coli, L. monocytogenes, H. influenzae, S. agalactiae, plus HSV-1/2, enteroviruses, VZV, HHV-6, and parechovirus, but does not replace culture
Neuroimaging before LP (CT or MRI) when immunocompromised, recent head trauma, depressed consciousness, papilledema, or focal deficits; MRI preferred for edema/ischemia and shows diffuse meningeal enhancement after gadolinium
For suspected viral meningitis: CBC with differential, liver and renal function, ESR, C-reactive protein, electrolytes, glucose, creatine kinase, aldolase, amylase, and lipase; CSF PCR is the diagnostic procedure of choice for enteroviral and HSV CNS infection
Biopsy of petechial skin lesions may reveal organisms on Gram stain in meningococcemia
# Red flags
Petechial or purpuric rash suggesting meningococcemia, which can be fulminant and fatal within hours of symptom onset
Deteriorating consciousness, papilledema, dilated poorly reactive pupils, sixth nerve palsy, decerebrate posturing, or the Cushing reflex (bradycardia, hypertension, irregular respirations) indicating raised ICP
Cerebral herniation, the most disastrous complication of raised ICP, reported in 1-8% of bacterial meningitis cases
Focal neurologic findings, which mandate prompt MRI to detect focal suppurative infection
Seizures or status epilepticus at presentation
Stupor, coma, or marked confusion in a presumed viral meningitis, which instead suggest encephalitis or an alternative diagnosis
# Pitfalls
Awaiting LP or neuroimaging to start therapy: blood cultures and empirical treatment must not be delayed in suspected bacterial meningitis
Assuming antibiotics given a few hours before LP invalidate the CSF; they do not significantly alter WBC count or glucose, nor reliably prevent Gram stain or PCR detection
Relying on multiplex PCR alone: it omits S. aureus, coagulase-negative staphylococci, and many gram-negatives, and cannot provide susceptibility data
Interpreting meningeal enhancement on MRI as diagnostic of meningitis; it occurs in any condition with increased blood-brain barrier permeability
Overreliance on Kernig's and Brudzinski's signs, which have uncertain sensitivity/specificity and may be absent in vulnerable groups; cervical spine disease in the elderly can cause false-positive nuchal rigidity
Misreading an early PMN pleocytosis in viral meningitis (e.g., echovirus 9, WNV, EEE, mumps) as bacterial; a PMN-predominant CSF without a diagnosis should prompt consideration of bacterial or parameningeal infection
Forgetting that giving IV 50% glucose before LP can take hours to alter CSF glucose, so it rarely confounds early sampling
# Management
Immediate empirical intravenous third-generation cephalosporin such as ceftriaxone or cefotaxime without waiting for lumbar puncture or imaging
Add vancomycin to cover penicillin-resistant pneumococcus and amoxicillin to cover Listeria in neonates, older, pregnant, or immunocompromised patients
Adjunctive dexamethasone given with or just before the first antibiotic dose, particularly for suspected pneumococcal disease
Narrow antimicrobial therapy once the organism and susceptibilities are known
Supportive management of raised intracranial pressure, seizures, and fluid balance, with intensive care for depressed consciousness or shock
Monitor and manage complications including hydrocephalus, seizures, and sensorineural hearing loss
Notify public health, give chemoprophylaxis with rifampicin or ciprofloxacin to close meningococcal contacts, and reinforce vaccination
