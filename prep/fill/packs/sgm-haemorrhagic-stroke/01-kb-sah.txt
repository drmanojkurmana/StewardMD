# Subarachnoid hemorrhage
Subarachnoid hemorrhage, Neurology / Vascular.
# Red flags
Thunderclap headache is SAH until proven otherwise — image immediately
# Investigations
Non-contrast CT head (urgent)
LP for xanthochromia if CT negative
CT angiography
# Pathophysiology
Excluding trauma, the commonest cause is rupture of a saccular ('berry') aneurysm, which forms at bifurcations of large- to medium-sized intracranial arteries (about 89% in the anterior circulation, mostly on the circle of Willis). Aneurysm walls show loss of the internal elastic lamina at the neck base with medial thinning and replacement of smooth muscle by connective tissue; rupture is usually at the thin-walled dome through a tear often <=0.5 mm. Rupture deposits blood in the subarachnoid space of the basal cisterns (and sometimes adjacent parenchyma), abruptly raising ICP. Other causes include vascular malformations (AVM, dural arteriovenous fistula) and subarachnoid extension of a primary intracerebral haemorrhage. Roughly 2% of adults harbour intracranial aneurysms and about 20% of affected patients have multiple aneurysms (frequently mirror-image bilateral). Delayed cerebral ischaemia is thought to arise from clot breakdown products acting on basal arteries to cause vasospasm and vessel-wall thickening, but also from vasospasm-independent mechanisms (microthrombosis, inflammation, microvascular dysregulation, cortical spreading depolarisation). Hyponatraemia results from natriuresis with volume depletion ('cerebral salt wasting'), mediated by atrial and brain natriuretic peptides.
# Prognosis
Overall mortality of aneurysmal SAH is about 35%, with roughly a third dying before reaching hospital; over half of survivors are left with significant neurologic deficits from the initial bleed, delayed cerebral ischaemia, or hydrocephalus. Outcome worsens with increasing presentation grade: grade 1 (Hunt-Hess) patients rarely die if treated, whereas mortality for grades 4-5 may reach about 60%. In an untreated aneurysm, rebleeding risk is about 20% in the first 2 weeks, 30% in the first month, then about 3% per year; rerupture (~30% in the first month, peaking in the first 7 days) carries about 50% mortality. Delayed cerebral ischaemia occurs in about 30% and is the leading cause of delayed morbidity and death among initial survivors. Unruptured aneurysms <7 mm had ~0% 5-year rupture risk in a large study (though many ruptured aneurysms are <6 mm); giant aneurysms (>25 mm) carry ~8-10% annual rupture risk.
# Severity classification
Two presentation-based clinical grading scales are used: the Hunt-Hess scale (1 = asymptomatic/minimal headache and slight nuchal rigidity through 5 = coma with reflex posturing or flaccidity) and the World Federation of Neurosurgical Societies (WFNS) scale based on Glasgow Coma Scale score and motor deficits (1 = GCS 15 no motor deficit through 5 = GCS 3-6). Higher grade predicts worse outcome. The modified Fisher CT grading system predicts symptomatic vasospasm by the amount and distribution of subarachnoid/intraventricular blood ('thin' <1 mm vs 'thick' >=1 mm), with risk rising from 0% (grade 0) to ~40% (grade 4, thick blood with intraventricular blood).
# Clinical pearls
The single most important feature of aneurysmal SAH headache is its sudden (abrupt) onset, even more than its severity; ruptures may occasionally present as only moderate headache or as a change in the patient's usual headache pattern.
Transient loss of consciousness occurs in nearly half of patients at the moment of rupture, reflecting an abrupt rise in intracranial pressure; many patients only report the headache after regaining consciousness.
A third nerve palsy with pupillary dilation and pain around/behind the eye suggests an expanding aneurysm at the posterior communicating artery-internal carotid junction; a sixth nerve palsy may indicate a cavernous-sinus aneurysm.
Quivering, jerking, or extensor posturing accompanying loss of consciousness at SAH onset is usually caused by the sharp ICP rise rather than true seizure activity.
ECG changes mimicking cardiac ischaemia (ST/T changes, long QT, peaked or deeply inverted T waves) and a reversible (catecholamine-mediated) cardiomyopathy with regional wall-motion abnormalities can occur after SAH; an asymptomatic troponin rise is common.
Nimodipine improves outcomes in aneurysmal SAH, apparently by limiting ischaemic injury rather than by reducing vasospasm itself (knowledge, no dose).
Perimesencephalic (idiopathic) SAH localised to the perimesencephalic cisterns is benign, probably venous/capillary in origin, and angiography is typically unrevealing.
# Additional differentials
Primary thunderclap headache / reversible cerebral vasoconstriction syndrome (RCVS) — many otherwise-undiagnosed thunderclap headaches are attributed to RCVS
Cervicocephalic arterial dissection
Cerebral venous (sinus) thrombosis
Ruptured arteriovenous malformation — but blood is usually intraparenchymal, not deposited in the basal cisterns, and symptomatic vasospasm is rare
Dural arteriovenous fistula (a recognised cause of SAH)
# Non infectious mimics
Thunderclap migraine — a migraine variant that can simulate SAH; a definitive workup to exclude aneurysm/other intracranial pathology is required before attributing sudden severe headache to it
# Additional investigations
Noncontrast CT detects >95% of cases when obtained within 72 h; sensitivity falls as time from ictus increases
Four-vessel conventional x-ray (catheter) angiography — gold standard to localise/define aneurysm anatomy and detect additional aneurysms; ruptured aneurysm can be treated at the same sitting
CT perfusion imaging — adjunct to identify potentially reversible ischaemic deficits in suspected delayed cerebral ischaemia
Transcranial Doppler ultrasound — daily/alternate-day bedside monitoring detects rising flow velocities of vasospasm
Serial (daily or twice-daily) serum electrolytes to detect early hyponatraemia
ECG and echocardiography to detect neurogenic cardiac changes and stress (catecholamine) cardiomyopathy
# Red flags
Sudden unexplained headache of any location may represent a sentinel bleed (small aneurysmal leak) heralding imminent major haemorrhage and must be investigated
Declining mental status with worsening headache 4-14 days after SAH suggests delayed cerebral ischaemia from vasospasm
High ICP refractory to treatment is a poor prognostic sign
# Pitfalls
A normal CT does not exclude SAH, especially as time elapses or with small or infratentorial bleeds; if CT is non-diagnostic and there is no mass lesion/obstructive hydrocephalus, lumbar puncture for xanthochromia is required.
Xanthochromia (yellow CSF from haemoglobin-to-bilirubin conversion) takes 6-12 h to appear, peaks at ~48 h, and persists 1-4 weeks; an LP performed too early may miss it.
Free-water restriction should NOT be used to treat SAH-associated hyponatraemia (cerebral salt wasting): the resulting hypovolaemia/hypotension can precipitate cerebral ischaemia.
Marked hyponatraemia of several days' duration must not be corrected too rapidly because of the risk of osmotic demyelination syndrome.
Lumbar puncture before imaging is appropriate only when CT is unavailable at the time of suspected SAH.
# Confirm
Sudden thunderclap (worst-ever) headache ± neck stiffness, photophobia, transient LOC. Non-contrast CT head; LP for xanthochromia if CT negative and high suspicion.
ICU + neurosurgery, urgent.
Nimodipine + BP/pain control (with early aneurysm securing)
# Steps
EMERGENCY — resuscitate (ABC), neurosurgery / stroke team immediately.
CT angiography to localise aneurysm; secure aneurysm early (endovascular coiling or clipping).
Nimodipine 60 mg PO q4h to reduce vasospasm-related deficits.
Control pain and BP (avoid extreme swings); bed rest, analgesia, antiemetics.
Monitor for hydrocephalus, rebleeding, vasospasm, hyponatraemia.
Symptom control (BP, pain, nausea)
Secure aneurysm early + nimodipine + BP control
