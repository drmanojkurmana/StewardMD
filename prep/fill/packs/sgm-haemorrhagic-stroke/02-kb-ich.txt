# Intracerebral hemorrhage
Intracerebral hemorrhage, Neurology / Vascular.
Red flags: Reverse anticoagulation; neurosurgical review.

# Pathophysiology
Hypertensive ICH usually results from spontaneous rupture of a small (~30-100 microns) penetrating artery deep in the brain, with chronic hypertension producing vascular injury preferentially at the basal ganglia (especially putamen), thalamus, cerebellum and pons. The clot may stay small or enlarge to compress adjacent tissue and cause herniation; dissection into the ventricles markedly increases morbidity and can produce hydrocephalus. Most hypertensive bleeds evolve over 30-90 minutes, whereas anticoagulant-related bleeds may keep expanding for 24-48 h. By ~48 h macrophages begin phagocytosing the haemorrhage at its surface, and over 1-6 months it resolves to a slit-like glial-scarred cavity with haemosiderin-laden macrophages. In cerebral amyloid angiopathy, arteriolar degeneration with amyloid deposition in cortical vessel walls causes single and recurrent lobar haemorrhages, the commonest cause of lobar bleeding in the elderly.
# Prognosis
Overall, about 35-45% of patients die within the first month, and roughly 40% with a hypertensive ICH die, though survivors can achieve good to complete recovery because peri-haematoma tissue is displaced rather than infarcted and often regains function as the clot resorbs over months. The validated ICH Score stratifies mortality and outcome but should not be used to precisely prognosticate an individual, since withholding early aggressive care risks a self-fulfilling prophecy of poor outcome. Deep pontine haemorrhage with coma usually ends in death or a locked-in state, but small bleeds are compatible with survival; in cerebellar haemorrhage, full recovery is common if the deep cerebellar nuclei are spared.
# Severity classification
ICH Score (Hemphill et al., Stroke 2001) - validated grading scale for mortality/outcome stratification, summing five factors: age >=80 (1 pt), haematoma volume >=30 cc (1 pt), intraventricular haemorrhage present (1 pt), infratentorial origin (1 pt), and Glasgow Coma Scale 13-15 (0), 5-12 (1) or 3-4 (2); total 0-6.
# Clinical pearls
ICH typically presents as abrupt-onset focal deficit; compared with ischaemic stroke, headache is more common, but imaging is still required to distinguish the two.
Putaminal haemorrhage damages the adjacent internal capsule, so contralateral hemiparesis is the sentinel sign, often with the eyes deviated away from the side of the weakness.
Thalamic haemorrhage produces a prominent all-modality contralateral sensory deficit and characteristic ocular signs from inferior extension into the upper midbrain (e.g. eyes deviated down-and-in 'looking at the nose', poorly reactive unequal pupils, vertical-gaze palsy).
Pontine haemorrhage causes rapid deep coma with quadriplegia, decerebrate rigidity and reactive 'pinpoint' (~1 mm) pupils.
Cerebellar haemorrhage classically gives occipital headache, repeated vomiting and gait ataxia; in mild cases gait ataxia may be the only sign.
Lobar haemorrhage symptoms mirror the lobe involved: occipital → hemianopia, left temporal → aphasia and confusion, parietal → hemisensory loss, frontal → arm weakness.
Seizures are uncommon at presentation but occur in roughly 6-15% of patients within the first 3 days.
Lumbar puncture is generally unnecessary and should usually be avoided in ICH because focal signs and raised ICP make it potentially herniation-inducing.
ICH accounts for about 10% of all strokes, with disproportionately high incidence in Asian and Black populations.
# Additional differentials
Hypertensive deep ICH (putamen, globus pallidus, thalamus, cerebellar hemisphere, pons)
Lobar ICH from cerebral amyloid angiopathy (degenerative, elderly, associated with dementia, rare <60 years)
Anticoagulant- or coagulopathy-related ICH (any site, often lobar or subdural; high risk of haematoma expansion)
Sympathomimetic drug-related ICH (cocaine, amphetamine/methamphetamine) - a leading cause in patients under 45
Haemorrhage into a brain tumour (choriocarcinoma, melanoma, renal cell, thyroid, lung, hepatocellular metastases; glioblastoma, pilocytic astrocytoma, medulloblastoma)
Vascular malformations: arteriovenous malformation, cavernous angioma, capillary telangiectasia, dural arteriovenous fistula
Ruptured intracranial aneurysm (mycotic or non-mycotic) with intraparenchymal extension
Haemorrhagic transformation of a prior ischaemic infarct
Venous/dural sinus thrombosis causing venous infarction with haemorrhage (e.g. vein of Labbe occlusion → posterior temporal/inferior parietal haematoma)
Vasculitis (e.g. polyarteritis nodosa, lupus) and haematologic disorders (leukaemia, aplastic anaemia, thrombocytopenic purpura)
Hypertensive encephalopathy with haemorrhage; primary intraventricular haemorrhage; Moyamoya disease
Infection mimics: Mycotic aneurysm rupture; Sepsis-associated petechial white-matter haemorrhages.
# Non infectious mimics
Ischaemic stroke (clinically overlapping; differentiated by CT)
Traumatic intracranial haemorrhage (intraparenchymal temporal/inferior frontal, subarachnoid, subdural, epidural) - consider in any unexplained acute deficit, especially after a fall
Hypertensive encephalopathy / posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome - headache, vomiting, seizures, confusion with posterior-predominant brain oedema; favoured over hypertensive ICH when there is a prodrome and altered mental status before any bleed
Brain tumour presenting with haemorrhage
# Additional investigations
Non-contrast CT head - highly sensitive/specific for acute intracranial blood and defines location; preferred for acute stroke evaluation
Platelet count, prothrombin time, partial thromboplastin time and INR to detect coagulopathy
CT angiography or post-contrast CT to seek a 'spot sign' (focal enhancement within the haematoma indicating ongoing bleeding)
MRA, CTA or conventional catheter angiography when the cause is uncertain - particularly in young or non-hypertensive patients or atypical (non-deep) haematoma location
Hemosiderin-sensitive MRI (susceptibility-weighted imaging) to detect cortical microbleeds suggesting cerebral amyloid angiopathy
MRI for posterior fossa lesions (more sensitive than CT but not usually needed for primary diagnosis); amyloid-beta PET can image CAA non-invasively
# Red flags
'Spot sign' on CTA/post-contrast CT - associated with increased haematoma expansion, higher mortality and lower likelihood of favourable outcome
Haematoma expansion: about a third of patients (even without coagulopathy) have significant expansion within the first day; anticoagulant-related bleeds may keep enlarging for 24-48 h
Progressive decline in consciousness with headache and vomiting signalling rising ICP; deterioration may continue over 24-96 h from perihaematoma oedema
Cerebellar haemorrhage with depressed consciousness or obstructive hydrocephalus, or radiographic hydrocephalus - high risk of rapid brainstem-compression deterioration requiring immediate neurosurgical evaluation
Signs of herniation (stupor/coma, dilated fixed pupil, decerebrate rigidity)
Intraventricular extension of blood - substantially increases morbidity and may cause hydrocephalus
# Pitfalls
Cerebellar haemorrhage can deteriorate precipitously from brainstem compression or obstructive hydrocephalus; immediate surgical evacuation before severe brainstem compression may be lifesaving, and hydrocephalus from a cerebellar haematoma should generally be managed by definitive evacuation rather than ventricular drainage alone.
Haemorrhage outside the typical deep hypertensive sites, or in a non-hypertensive patient, should prompt a search for other causes (amyloid angiopathy, tumour, vascular malformation, vasculitis, coagulopathy) rather than being assumed hypertensive.
Conflating hypertensive encephalopathy/PRES with hypertensive ICH leads to inappropriately aggressive BP lowering - hypertensive encephalopathy warrants less aggressive reduction.
ICP is often normal even with large ICHs, so it cannot be assumed; conversely induced hyperventilation can cause ischaemia and should be limited to acute resuscitation only.
Glucocorticoids are not recommended for treating ICH.
In ICH on antiplatelet drugs without thrombocytopenia, platelet transfusion showed no benefit and possible harm.
Despite the presence of haemorrhage, venous sinus thrombosis is an indication for anticoagulation to relieve venous hypertension.
# Intracerebral hemorrhage: treatment
Sudden focal deficit ± reduced consciousness, headache, vomiting; non-contrast CT shows intraparenchymal haemorrhage, ICU / neurosurgery.
Blood-pressure control + urgent anticoagulation reversal
# Steps
Resuscitate; protect airway if GCS ≤8.
Reverse anticoagulation urgently (vitamin K + PCC for warfarin; specific reversal agents for DOACs).
Lower SBP to ~140 mmHg (e.g. IV labetalol/nicardipine).
Manage raised ICP; neurosurgical referral (esp. cerebellar bleed, hydrocephalus).
Admit to stroke unit/ICU; VTE prophylaxis (mechanical first).
Anticoagulation reversal
Reverse anticoagulation + lower SBP to ~140 mmHg
