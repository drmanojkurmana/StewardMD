# Pathophysiology
SLE arises from overactive innate and adaptive immunity with autoantibodies directed at nuclear components (nucleic acids, nucleosomes, RNA-binding proteins). Impaired clearance of cellular debris — driven by high neutrophil turnover/NETosis and defective complement-mediated phagocytosis — overwhelms macrophages and dendritic cells. Accumulated nucleic acids engage toll-like receptors and nucleic-acid sensors in plasmacytoid dendritic cells, driving type 1 interferon production that primes the immune response. Autoreactive B cells (including expanded age-associated B cells) lose tolerance and, with help from T follicular/T peripheral helper cells, mature into autoantibody-secreting plasma cells, while regulatory T-cell function is defective. Resulting immune complexes deposit in tissues and cause inflammation, repair, and eventual fibrosis after recurrent flares. Strong genetic component (monozygotic twin concordance 24% vs 2% dizygotic; >150 GWAS loci, with the MHC the strongest and nearly half of loci linked to type 1 interferon; monogenic complement-pathway deficiencies — C1q, C1r/s, C2, C4 — cause severe SLE). Environmental triggers include UV light, smoking, crystalline silica, mercury/toxins, sex hormones, EBV, and gut microbiome/permeability changes.
# Prognosis
Prognosis and survival vary widely by geography, race/ethnicity, and access to care/medications. Renal disease, especially class IV nephritis, carries substantial risk of ESRD and death (~20% within 10 years), and is more severe in non-White patients. Accelerated atherosclerosis is a major cause of premature death and the leading cause after ~5 years of disease, driven by both traditional risk factors and lupus-specific factors (antiphospholipid antibodies, high disease activity, low C3, high glucocorticoid dose, high homocysteine/leptin). Glucocorticoid exposure independently accrues irreversible organ damage (cataracts, osteoporotic fractures, avascular necrosis, diabetes) even at doses as low as 5 mg prednisone equivalent. Class V (membranous) nephritis has a better prognosis than class III/IV. Sustained low disease activity (LLDAS) for 2+ years is associated with significantly less damage. Recent meta-analyses suggest an increased overall cancer risk and cancer-related mortality.
# Severity classification
Disease activity is graded for treatment using SLEDAI/SLEDAI-2K (low disease activity = SLEDAI 0-4; remission/DORIS = SLEDAI 0). EULAR severity tiers: mild (constitutional symptoms, mild arthritis, rash <=9% BSA, platelets 50-100, SLEDAI <=6); moderate (RA-like arthritis, rash 9-18% BSA, platelets 20-50, serositis, SLEDAI 7-12); severe (major organ-threatening disease e.g. cerebritis, myelitis, pneumonitis, mesenteric vasculitis; platelets <20; TTP-like or acute hemophagocytic syndrome; rash >18% BSA; SLEDAI >12). Lupus nephritis is graded by ISN/RPS renal biopsy classes I-V (class I mesangial deposits; II mesangial proliferative; III focal proliferative; IV diffuse proliferative; V membranous; III/IV +/- V combinations), with class III/IV (and III/IV+V) being proliferative forms warranting aggressive therapy.
# Clinical pearls
Roughly 90% of SLE patients are women, most of childbearing age; prevalence is highest among Black women, then Hispanic, White, and Asian/Pacific Islander women; disease tends to be more severe in non-White patients.
Autoantibodies can be detectable years before clinical diagnosis, so a positive serology in a symptomatic patient supports attributing nonspecific complaints to SLE.
Most patients have active disease despite therapy or recurrent flares; treatment goals are low disease activity (SLEDAI 0-4) or remission on ≤5 mg/day prednisone equivalent.
A proposed clinical split distinguishes type 1 lupus (classic inflammatory/organ manifestations such as nephritis, arthritis, vasculitis that respond to immunosuppression) from type 2 lupus (fatigue, diffuse pain, brain fog, depression, sleep disturbance that respond poorly to immunosuppression).
Anti-dsDNA titers and C3/C4 levels track disease activity: anti-dsDNA is produced by short-lived plasmablasts that turn over quickly, and complement falls during high activity as it is consumed by immune complexes.
Rising anti-dsDNA and falling C3/C4 can serve as warning signs of impending active nephritis even in an asymptomatic patient.
Lupus arthritis is typically non-erosive (Jaccoud-like reversible deformity from ligamentous laxity); overlap with rheumatoid arthritis is termed 'rhupus'.
Pain out of proportion in a single hip, shoulder, or knee — especially with prior corticosteroid use — should raise concern for avascular necrosis of bone.
Accelerated atherosclerosis is a major cause of premature death, notably myocardial infarction and stroke in young patients without traditional risk factors, and is the leading cause of death after ~5 years of disease.
Antiphospholipid antibodies/APS coexist in about a third of SLE patients and markedly raise risk of arterial/venous thrombosis, stroke (reported in up to 19%), Libman-Sacks endocarditis, and pregnancy loss.
# Additional differentials
Rosacea (malar rash mimic — but rosacea involves the nasolabial folds, whereas lupus malar rash spares them)
Rheumatoid arthritis, or RA-SLE overlap ('rhupus')
Drug-induced lupus (antihistone antibodies; ~6-12% of lupus cases; rarely renal/CNS; resolves on stopping the drug)
Other systemic autoimmune diseases sharing ANA subtypes: myositis/dermatomyositis, Sjögren's syndrome, systemic sclerosis, mixed connective tissue/overlap syndromes (anti-RNP)
Migraine or tension headache (lupus headache can be difficult to distinguish)
Glucocorticoid-induced psychosis (must be excluded before attributing psychosis to neuropsychiatric lupus)
# Infection mimics
Acute lupus pneumonitis — pulmonary infiltrates can be radiographically indistinguishable from pneumonia
Infectious encephalitis — should be ruled out before attributing acute psychosis/confusional state to CNS lupus
Infection as a cause of cytopenias — SLE cytopenias are multifactorial (disease activity, medication, or infection)
Epstein-Barr virus and other viral infections — may both trigger and mimic flare via interferon induction
# Non infectious mimics
Atypical hemolytic-uremic syndrome / TTP-like microvascular thrombotic crisis (hemolysis, thrombocytopenia, microvascular thrombosis, schistocytes, low ADAMTS13, high LDH)
Antiphospholipid syndrome thrombotic events presenting as stroke, DVT/PE, Budd-Chiari, or Libman-Sacks embolism
Glucocorticoid-induced complications mimicking active disease (steroid myopathy, hyperglycemia, psychosis, avascular necrosis)
Macrophage activation syndrome / hemophagocytic syndrome (acute hemophagocytic syndrome listed among severe SLE manifestations)
# Additional investigations
ANA by immunofluorescence is the best screening test (positive in ~95%; titer ≥1:80 favors SLE); repeated negatives make SLE unlikely but ~5% are ANA-negative
Anti-dsDNA (highly specific, ~40-70% prevalence) and anti-Sm (most specific, ~30%) — the only ANA subtypes in the EULAR/ACR criteria
Anti-Ro/SSA — useful when ANA-negative but clinically suspicious; linked to neonatal lupus, congenital heart block, sicca, photosensitivity, SCLE
Antihistone antibodies — suggest drug-induced lupus (positive in ~75% of DIL)
Antiphospholipid panel: anticardiolipin and anti-beta-2-glycoprotein-1 (IgG/IgM/IgA) plus lupus anticoagulant by dilute Russell viper venom time (~50% prevalence)
Random urine protein-to-creatinine ratio (UPCr) or 24-h urine protein with eGFR and serum creatinine; renal biopsy when UPCr >0.5 and/or declining function
Renal biopsy with ISN/RPS lupus nephritis classification (the only single finding that alone meets EULAR/ACR criteria — class III or IV)
CK and aldolase with muscle biopsy if inflammatory myopathy suspected
Emerging urinary biomarkers of renal inflammation/fibrosis: urinary IL-16, CD163, type 1 IFN, TGF-beta
Quarterly screening of CBC and renal indices in asymptomatic patients, since hematologic and renal flares can be silent
# Red flags
Rapidly declining eGFR, rising proteinuria, or active urinary sediment — class III/IV proliferative lupus nephritis; ~20% of class IV patients die or reach ESRD within 10 years
Crescentic / rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis (cellular crescents or fibrinoid necrosis) — poor prognostic features
Diffuse alveolar hemorrhage with capillaritis
Mesenteric vasculitis — risk of intestinal perforation, bleeding, and ischemia
Neuropsychiatric flare: seizures, acute psychosis/confusion, transverse myelitis
Atypical HUS/TTP-like crisis: hemolysis, thrombocytopenia, schistocytes, low ADAMTS13, microvascular thrombosis
Severe thrombocytopenia (<20-50 x10^9/L) with bleeding risk; severe hemolytic anemia
Stroke or arterial/venous thrombosis suggesting APS
Pulmonary arterial hypertension, prior arterial thrombosis, or severe end-organ damage — highest maternal mortality; pregnancy should strongly be reconsidered
# Pitfalls
Distinguishing flare from infection is a central challenge: cytopenias and constitutional symptoms can stem from disease activity, medication, OR infection, and acute lupus pneumonitis can look identical to pneumonia on imaging — infection must be excluded before escalating immunosuppression.
Classification criteria (2019 EULAR/ACR; 2012 SLICC) were built for trial enrollment, not diagnosis — diagnosis remains an individualized clinical judgment; relying on criteria alone can mislead, and ANA-negative or incomplete-lupus patients are excluded by EULAR/ACR.
A positive ANA or even antihistone antibody alone does not confirm SLE — many drugs induce a positive ANA in patients who never develop symptoms, and antihistone antibodies occur in most SLE cases too.
Drug-induced lupus can be hard to separate from SLE because skin biopsy and many labs overlap; presence of additional SLE-specific autoantibodies (rarely seen in DIL) and symptom resolution after stopping the drug help distinguish.
Acute psychosis/confusion may be glucocorticoid-induced rather than neuropsychiatric lupus — and infectious encephalitis must also be ruled out.
Single-joint pain out of proportion may be avascular necrosis (steroid-related), not arthritis flare.
Estrogen-containing contraceptives/HRT should be avoided in patients with antiphospholipid antibodies or moderate-severe/active lupus due to thromboembolism risk.
# SLE / autoimmune flare: treatment
Multisystem flare (rash, arthritis, serositis, cytopenias, nephritis) in known/new SLE; raised anti-dsDNA, low complement, Rheumatology; admit if organ-threatening.
Hydroxychloroquine backbone ± corticosteroid (mild–moderate flare)
# Steps
Assess organ involvement and severity (esp. renal, CNS, haematological).
Mild: hydroxychloroquine ± short NSAID/low-dose steroid.
Moderate–severe / organ-threatening: high-dose corticosteroids + immunosuppression (mycophenolate/cyclophosphamide; biologics).
Hydroxychloroquine for essentially all patients (reduces flares).
Exclude/treat concurrent infection (mimics flare and complicates immunosuppression); sun protection, vaccination.
Organ-threatening / severe flare — pulse steroids + immunosuppression
Exclude infection; escalate immunosuppression by severity
