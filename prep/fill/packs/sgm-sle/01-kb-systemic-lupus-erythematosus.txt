# Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE): diagnosis and treatment
Adults
Outpatient rheumatology clinic, ward
Give hydroxychloroquine to every patient (up to 5 mg/kg actual body weight daily), keep maintenance prednisolone at 5 mg/day or less and stop it if possible, add immunosuppressants or biologics (belimumab, anifrolumab) early to spare steroids, and refer suspected lupus nephritis urgently for biopsy.
Aliases: SLE; lupus flare; lupus nephritis; LN; HCQ; ANA; APS; LLDAS; EULAR 2023 SLE.
# Recognise
Multisystem autoimmune disease, mostly in women aged 15 to 45: fatigue, fever, inflammatory arthritis, photosensitive or malar rash, oral ulcers, non-scarring alopecia, serositis, Raynaud phenomenon, cytopenias, nephritis and neuropsychiatric features.
2019 EULAR/ACR classification: ANA titre 1:80 or more is the entry criterion, then weighted clinical and immunological criteria totalling 10 or more points. A negative ANA makes SLE very unlikely.
Grade severity: mild (skin, joints, constitutional), moderate (serositis, extensive skin or joint disease, moderate cytopenia), severe organ-threatening (nephritis, CNS lupus, severe haemolysis or thrombocytopenia, vasculitis, myocarditis, alveolar haemorrhage).
Consider drug-induced lupus (hydralazine, procainamide, isoniazid, minocycline, TNF inhibitors): usually anti-histone positive and resolves on withdrawal.
# Investigations
ANA, anti-dsDNA, anti-Sm, anti-Ro/SSA and anti-La/SSB, C3 and C4, antiphospholipid antibodies (lupus anticoagulant, anticardiolipin, anti-beta2 glycoprotein I).
CBC, direct Coombs test, reticulocytes, creatinine and eGFR, liver enzymes, urinalysis with microscopy and urine protein:creatinine ratio (uPCR) at every visit. ESR is often raised; a high CRP suggests infection or serositis.
Kidney biopsy, with nephrology, for proteinuria of 0.5 g/day (uPCR 50 mg/mmol) or more, active urinary sediment (dysmorphic red cells, casts) or an unexplained fall in eGFR.
Before immunosuppression: hepatitis B and C, HIV and latent TB screening, pregnancy test; baseline lipids, glucose, BP and fracture risk.
# Foundation therapy (all patients)
Hydroxychloroquine for all unless contraindicated, continued indefinitely including in remission and pregnancy (fewer flares, less damage, lower mortality). Target up to 5 mg/kg actual body weight/day (EULAR 2023); ACR 2025 allows short periods at 5 to 6.5 mg/kg/day at initiation or to regain control.
Glucocorticoid at the lowest dose for the shortest time: maintenance at 5 mg/day prednisolone equivalent or less and withdraw when possible (EULAR 2023); ACR 2025 strongly advises tapering to 5 mg/day or less within 6 months.
Moderate to severe flares: IV methylprednisolone 250 to 1000 mg daily for 1 to 3 days, then oral prednisolone about 0.3 to 0.5 mg/kg/day with taper, rather than prolonged 1 mg/kg/day.
Add an immunosuppressant (methotrexate, azathioprine or mycophenolate) and/or a biologic (belimumab or anifrolumab) early if there is no response to hydroxychloroquine or prednisolone cannot be reduced to 5 mg/day or less.
Sun protection with broad-spectrum sunscreen and smoking cessation for all; cutaneous lupus: topical glucocorticoid or calcineurin inhibitor in addition to hydroxychloroquine.
# Organ-threatening disease
Lupus nephritis: urgent nephrology referral. Induction with mycophenolate or low-dose IV cyclophosphamide (Euro-Lupus) plus glucocorticoid pulses; consider add-on belimumab or a calcineurin inhibitor (voclosporin or tacrolimus) (EULAR 2023). Continue immunosuppression for at least 3 years after response.
Nephritis supportive care: ACE inhibitor or ARB for proteinuria and hypertension, tight BP control, statin if indicated.
Severe neuropsychiatric lupus, systemic vasculitis or myocarditis (after excluding infection, drugs and thrombosis): pulse glucocorticoid plus IV cyclophosphamide, mycophenolate or rituximab. Life-threatening vasculitis such as alveolar haemorrhage: consider plasma exchange or IVIG (ACR 2025).
Severe thrombocytopenia (below 30 x 10^9/L) or haemolysis: high-dose or pulse glucocorticoid, IVIG if bleeding, and add an immunosuppressant or rituximab.
Refractory disease: rituximab (off-label) or combination therapy with specialist input.
# Antiphospholipid antibodies and pregnancy
High-risk antiphospholipid profile without thrombosis: low-dose aspirin for primary prevention. Thrombotic APS: long-term warfarin (INR 2 to 3); avoid DOACs, especially in triple-positive or arterial APS.
Plan pregnancy when lupus has been quiescent for at least 6 months; continue hydroxychloroquine. Compatible drugs: hydroxychloroquine, azathioprine, low-dose prednisolone, tacrolimus, ciclosporin.
Stop teratogens before conception and switch to a compatible drug: mycophenolate at least 6 weeks before, methotrexate 1 to 3 months before; avoid cyclophosphamide.
Low-dose aspirin (75 to 150 mg daily) from 12 weeks to reduce pre-eclampsia risk. Anti-Ro/SSA positive: fetal heart monitoring for congenital heart block from about 16 to 26 weeks. Joint obstetric and rheumatology care.
# Monitoring and comorbidity
Assess disease activity at every visit (for example SLEDAI-2K) and damage annually. Target remission or low disease activity on the lowest glucocorticoid dose, ideally 5 mg/day or less.
Stable disease: CBC, creatinine, urinalysis, uPCR, C3, C4 and anti-dsDNA every 3 to 6 months; more often during flares or on immunosuppressants.
Hydroxychloroquine retinal screening: baseline, then annually after 5 years (sooner with CKD, tamoxifen use or doses above 5 mg/kg/day).
Manage cardiovascular risk (BP, lipids, smoking), bone health on glucocorticoids, and vaccination (influenza, pneumococcal, recombinant zoster; no live vaccines on immunosuppression).
# Escalate
Admit and involve rheumatology urgently (with nephrology, neurology or haematology as needed) for nephritis with rising creatinine, CNS lupus, severe cytopenia, pulmonary haemorrhage, myocarditis, mesenteric vasculitis or suspected catastrophic APS.
Fever on immunosuppression: treat as infection until proven otherwise and take cultures before increasing glucocorticoid.
Pregnant patient with rising BP, proteinuria or a flare: same-day joint obstetric and rheumatology review to distinguish lupus nephritis from pre-eclampsia.
# Pitfalls
Attributing fever to a flare without excluding infection.
Hydroxychloroquine above 5 mg/kg actual body weight long term, or stopping it in remission or pregnancy.
Missing lupus nephritis: check urine at every visit, even when the patient feels well.
Long-term prednisolone above 5 mg/day instead of adding steroid-sparing therapy.
Mycophenolate, methotrexate or cyclophosphamide without contraception counselling.
# Drugs
Hydroxychloroquine
# Dose
200 to 400 mg orally daily; do not exceed 5 mg/kg actual body weight/day long term
All patients. Reduce in CKD. Retinal screening.
Methylprednisolone (IV pulse), 250 to 1000 mg IV once daily for 1 to 3 days, Moderate to severe or organ-threatening flares; follow with oral taper.
Prednisolone
# Dose
About 0.3 to 0.5 mg/kg/day orally after pulses, tapered to 5 mg/day or less (ideally stopped)
ACR 2025: reach 5 mg/day or less within 6 months. Bone protection.
Mycophenolate mofetil
# Dose
2 to 3 g/day orally in 2 divided doses for lupus nephritis induction; lower doses for maintenance
Teratogenic: effective contraception; stop at least 6 weeks before conception. CBC monitoring.
Cyclophosphamide (Euro-Lupus low dose)
500 mg IV every 2 weeks for 6 doses
Nephritis or severe organ disease, then maintenance with mycophenolate or azathioprine. Gonadotoxic; discuss fertility preservation.
Azathioprine, 1 to 2 mg/kg/day orally (check TPMT activity first), Compatible with pregnancy. Avoid with allopurinol or febuxostat unless dose is reduced.
Belimumab
# Dose
10 mg/kg IV at weeks 0, 2 and 4, then every 4 weeks; or 200 mg subcutaneously weekly
Add-on for active SLE and lupus nephritis.
Anifrolumab, 300 mg IV every 4 weeks, Add-on for moderate to severe non-renal SLE, especially skin and joints; more herpes zoster.
