# Drug-induced vasculitis
Drug-induced vasculitis, Multisystem, Rheumatology.
Aliases: Drug-induced vasculitis; Drug-associated vasculitis; Drug-induced ANCA-associated vasculitis.
# Pathophysiology
Drug-induced vasculitis arises when a drug or its metabolites trigger an immune-mediated attack on blood vessel walls. In hypersensitivity small-vessel vasculitis the drug acts as a hapten, driving immune-complex formation and deposition in postcapillary venules with complement activation and neutrophil-mediated leukocytoclastic injury. In drug-induced ANCA-associated vasculitis certain agents promote the generation of antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies, often against myeloperoxidase; some drugs are thought to alter neutrophil extracellular trap formation or unmask sequestered antigens, breaking tolerance. The result ranges from purely cutaneous disease to a necrotising small-vessel vasculitis with renal and pulmonary involvement indistinguishable from idiopathic disease.
# Prognosis
Cutaneous-limited disease usually resolves after the culprit drug is stopped, but organ-threatening ANCA-associated forms can cause lasting renal impairment if not treated promptly. Rechallenge with the offending agent risks recurrence and should be avoided.
# Clinical pearls
Drug-induced vasculitis most often manifests as a small-vessel leukocytoclastic vasculitis with palpable purpura on dependent areas, typically days to weeks after starting the culprit drug.
A distinct drug-induced ANCA-associated vasculitis is well recognised, classically with antimyeloperoxidase antibodies, and can affect the kidneys and lungs.
Antithyroid drugs, hydralazine, minocycline, certain anti-tumour necrosis factor agents and cocaine adulterated with levamisole are among the well-described triggers.
Very high-titre antimyeloperoxidase antibodies, antibodies to multiple antigens, or combined MPO and PR3 positivity should raise suspicion of a drug cause.
Withdrawal of the offending agent is the cornerstone of management and often leads to resolution of milder cutaneous disease.
Severe organ-threatening disease (glomerulonephritis, alveolar haemorrhage) may still require immunosuppression despite drug cessation.
Levamisole-adulterated cocaine characteristically causes retiform purpura with ear involvement, neutropenia and positive ANCA.
A detailed drug and substance history, including over-the-counter agents and illicit drugs, is essential to identify the trigger.
