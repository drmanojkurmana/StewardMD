# Septic Shock
Septic Shock, Systemic.
# Pathogens
Septic shock has the highest mortality of the sepsis spectrum and the broadest reasonable empiric coverage is justified initially, since the cost of inadequate empiric coverage (each hour of delay increases mortality) outweighs the stewardship cost of initial broad coverage — provided aggressive de-escalation follows.
# Likely
Gram-positive cocci (S. aureus, streptococci) depending on source
# Possible

Immediate empiric antibiotics required
# Reason
Suspected infection with persistent hypotension requiring vasopressors despite adequate fluid resuscitation, and elevated lactate (>2 mmol/L), meets criteria for septic shock — the most severe and time-critical sepsis presentation. Empiric broad-spectrum antibiotics must be given within 1 hour, alongside aggressive fluid resuscitation, vasopressor support, and urgent source identification/control.
# Pathophysiology
Septic shock is the most common form of distributive shock, in which the primary hemodynamic derangement is a fall in systemic vascular resistance (vasoplegia) rather than a primary loss of preload or pump function. The vasodilatory profile is distinctive: SVR is reduced, cardiac output is typically increased as a compensatory response, and both central venous pressure (CVP) and pulmonary capillary wedge pressure (PCWP) tend to be low. Arterial and venous vasodilation is mediated in part by excess nitric oxide; vasopressin deficiency may also contribute to refractory hypotension. The dysregulated host response begins when pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs, e.g. LPS, microbial nucleic acids) and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs, e.g. histones, HMGB1, mitochondrial DNA, ATP) are sensed by pattern-recognition receptors (TLRs, CLRs, RAGE, RIG-I, NOD receptors) on neutrophils, monocytes/macrophages, and parenchymal cells, triggering proinflammatory cytokine/chemokine release and upregulation of endothelial adhesion molecules. Activated neutrophils release reactive oxygen species and neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs); endothelial activation/injury, glycocalyx degradation, and loss of VE-cadherin tight junctions cause capillary leak, interstitial edema, and increased oxygen-diffusion distance. A prothrombotic state arises from tissue-factor-driven coagulation, platelet/vWF microthrombi, complement activation (C3a, C5a, C5b-9 membrane attack complex), reduced anticoagulants (antithrombin, tissue factor pathway inhibitor, activated protein C), and impaired fibrinolysis (elevated PAI-1); consumption of clotting factors and platelets can then produce hemorrhage. The net effect is hypotension (from vasodilation, myocardial depression, and hypovolemia), microcirculatory dysfunction, and mitochondrial/metabolic failure, so that oxygen delivery, uptake, and utilization are all impaired and cellular injury occurs even when bulk oxygen delivery appears adequate. Sepsis-induced cardiomyopathy can add a cardiogenic component, producing mixed shock.
# Prognosis
Septic shock carries up to ~40% mortality, far higher than the ~15% overall mortality among US hospitalized adults with sepsis. In emergency-department shock cohorts, septic and cardiogenic shock have the highest 90-day mortality (reported ~56% and ~52% respectively), and septic shock predominates among ICU shock (~62% of ICU shock patients in one multicenter series), whereas hypovolemic shock has lower mortality. Mortality rises with age and is higher in men; comorbidities such as diabetes, obesity, neurologic/respiratory/cardiac disease, renal or hepatic insufficiency, cancer, and immunosuppression worsen outcomes. SOFA scores predict mortality at the population level but not for individual patients; proposed clinical 'phenotypes' (alpha/beta/gamma/delta) carried roughly 2%, 5%, 15%, and 32% mortality. Survivors face substantial long-term sequelae: physical (fatigue, muscle loss, weakness), cognitive and psychological (cognitive decline, dementia, depression), and increased risk of cardiovascular events, recurrent infection, and readmission. Roughly half of survivors are rehospitalized within 1 year and about one in six die within the first year, with most post-sepsis deaths in the first 6 months and elevated risk persisting up to 2 years.
# Severity classification
Sepsis-3 spectrum: (1) Sepsis = life-threatening organ dysfunction from a dysregulated host response to infection, operationalized as an acute increase of >=2 points in the SOFA score in a patient with suspected/confirmed infection. (2) Septic shock = a subset of sepsis with profound circulatory, cellular, and metabolic abnormalities and higher mortality, operationally defined as a septic patient who, despite adequate fluid resuscitation, requires vasopressors to maintain MAP >65 mmHg AND has serum lactate >2 mmol/L. qSOFA (RR>=22, GCS<15, SBP<=100; score >=2) is a rapid risk-stratification screen, not a diagnosis. The historical Sepsis-1/2 terms 'severe sepsis' and SIRS-based septic shock (SBP<90 or drop >=40 mmHg) have been superseded.
# Clinical pearls
Septic shock under Sepsis-3 is a subset of sepsis with profound circulatory, cellular, and metabolic derangements that carry a higher mortality than sepsis alone; sepsis itself is life-threatening organ dysfunction from a dysregulated host response to infection.
Sepsis-3 abandoned the older SIRS criteria (used by Sepsis-1/2) because of poor specificity for distinguishing infection from other inflammatory states, and eliminated the term 'severe sepsis'.
Sepsis is operationalized as an acute rise of at least 2 points in the SOFA score from baseline in a patient with suspected or confirmed infection.
qSOFA (respiratory rate >=22/min, altered mentation with GCS <15, systolic BP <=100 mmHg) is a rapid bedside screen; a score >=2 is more specific but less sensitive than SIRS and flags patients at higher risk of death, ICU admission, and longer stay.
Sepsis presentation can be nonspecific (fever, tachycardia, lethargy, myalgias) with or without localizing findings; there is no single gold-standard diagnostic test, so the diagnosis integrates clinical, laboratory, microbiologic, and imaging data.
Approximately 88% of sepsis cases are community onset (detected within 48 h of admission); hospital-onset sepsis (after 48 h) tends to involve more comorbidities, intraabdominal infection, and Enterococcus/Candida/Pseudomonas, with higher ICU admission and mortality.
Among culture-positive community-onset sepsis, the most common primary sites are urinary tract (~49%), respiratory tract (~33%), intraabdominal (~14%), and skin/soft tissue (~10%).
About 53% of US sepsis cases are bacterial culture positive with a roughly even gram-positive / gram-negative split; common gram-positives are S. aureus, Streptococcus, and Enterococcus, and common gram-negatives are E. coli, Klebsiella, and P. aeruginosa.
# Additional differentials
Other causes of distributive shock: pancreatitis, severe burns, liver failure, anaphylaxis, neurogenic shock from brain/spinal cord injury, and adrenal insufficiency/adrenal crisis
Cardiogenic shock (myocardial infarction, myocarditis, arrhythmia, acute valvular failure)
Hypovolemic shock (hemorrhagic from trauma or GI bleeding; nonhemorrhagic from vomiting, diarrhea, osmotic diuresis in DKA or diabetes insipidus, or skin loss)
Obstructive shock (tension pneumothorax, cardiac tamponade, constrictive pericarditis, pulmonary embolism, air or fat embolism, aortic dissection)
Mixed shock, where more than one mechanism coexists (e.g. distributive sepsis plus sepsis-induced cardiomyopathy)
# Infection mimics
Noninfectious pneumonitis or lung injury that mimics pneumonia/respiratory sepsis
Noninfectious abdominal syndromes such as mesenteric ischemia and inflammatory bowel disease, which can mimic an intraabdominal source
# Non infectious mimics
Noninfectious inflammatory febrile syndromes including connective tissue diseases and vasculitides
Heart failure and cardiac arrest (the most common cardiovascular mimics in ICU series, where ~25% of presumed septic patients were later judged to have a mimic)
Noninfectious chronic obstructive pulmonary disease exacerbation (common respiratory mimic)
Hypotension from noninfectious causes: hypovolemia, autonomic dysfunction, and adrenal insufficiency
# Additional investigations
Complete blood count with differential, basic metabolic panel, and liver function tests to assess end-organ dysfunction
Coagulation panel (PT/PTT/INR, platelets) to detect sepsis-induced coagulopathy/DIC
Cultures of blood and other potentially infected sites (urine, sputum, wound) ideally before antimicrobials, plus point-of-care pathogen testing when available
Urinalysis and urine sediment to evaluate for pyuria/urinary source
Cardiac enzymes/troponin to detect a primary or contributory cardiac process
Venous or arterial blood gas (arterial if hypoxemia present) to quantify acidosis
Focused imaging (chest x-ray, CT, or ultrasound) of suspected sites; point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS, e.g. RUSH/ACES protocols) helps classify undifferentiated and mixed shock
SOFA score components for severity (GCS, MAP/vasopressor use, PaO2/FiO2, bilirubin, creatinine, platelets)
# Red flags
Persistent hypotension requiring vasopressors to keep MAP >65 mmHg plus lactate >2 mmol/L despite adequate fluid resuscitation defines septic shock and marks the highest-mortality end of the spectrum
Acute rise of >=2 SOFA points from baseline with suspected infection indicates new organ dysfunction
qSOFA >=2 (RR>=22, GCS<15, SBP<=100) signals high risk of death and ICU need
Elevated and rising serial lactate, which correlates with worse outcome and reflects tissue hypoperfusion
Sepsis-associated encephalopathy (altered consciousness, cognition, or behavior) signaling CNS organ dysfunction
New oliguria / acute kidney injury, occurring in up to two-thirds of septic patients
Signs of coagulopathy/DIC with consumption of platelets and clotting factors and bleeding risk
# Pitfalls
Relying on SIRS criteria to define or exclude sepsis: SIRS lacks specificity, which is why Sepsis-3 replaced it.
Treating shock as a single entity: distributive (septic) shock has low SVR with high cardiac output and low filling pressures, the opposite hemodynamic profile of cardiogenic and hypovolemic shock, so misclassification leads to wrong resuscitation.
Assuming a single shock type: sepsis frequently coexists with a cardiogenic component (sepsis-induced cardiomyopathy) or hypovolemia, producing mixed shock that POCUS can help dissect.
A normal hemoglobin/hematocrit early in concurrent hemorrhage, or a normal WBC, does not exclude the relevant process; leukocytosis is suggestive but not diagnostic of infection.
Anchoring on sepsis when infection is not confirmed: about 25% of presumed septic ICU patients had a mimic, so alternative diagnoses must be continually reconsidered.
Failure to identify and control the source (e.g. infected catheter, abscess, endocarditis) undermines management even with appropriate antibiotics.
# Deescalation
Mandatory reassessment at 48-72 hours: narrow to source/organism-directed therapy, discontinue empiric antifungal or vancomycin if not supported by cultures, and target the shortest effective duration once source control is achieved.

# Coverage
Considered in patients with multiple risk factors for invasive candidiasis (prolonged broad-spectrum antibiotics, central line, TPN, recent abdominal surgery) who remain unstable despite antibacterial therapy.
# Framework
Broad empiric coverage is justified in septic shock specifically because of the mortality cost of delay — but this must be paired with mandatory reassessment and de-escalation at 48-72 hours once cultures return.
Do not continue maximal broad-spectrum therapy beyond what culture data supports — septic shock survivors are at high risk of unnecessarily prolonged broad-spectrum exposure if this step is skipped.
Source control (drainage of abscess, removal of infected line/device, debridement) is as important as antibiotics and must be pursued in parallel, not after stabilization.
