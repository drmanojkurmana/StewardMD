# Acute Cholangitis
Acute Cholangitis, Hepatobiliary.
# Pathogens
Cholangitis pathogens reflect ascending enteric flora from the duodenum into an obstructed biliary tree, dominated by gram-negative Enterobacteriaceae, with anaerobes and enterococci becoming more relevant with prior instrumentation (ERCP, stents) or recurrent disease.
Very likely: Escherichia coli; Klebsiella pneumoniae.
Likely: Enterococcus species; Enterobacter species.
# Possible
Anaerobes (Bacteroides species) — particularly with prior biliary instrumentation or surgery
Red flags: Immediate empiric antibiotics required.
# Investigations
Blood cultures, Frequently positive in cholangitis and identifies organism/susceptibility for de-escalation.
Liver function tests, Cholestatic pattern (elevated bilirubin, ALP, GGT) supports the diagnosis and tracks resolution after drainage.
Abdominal ultrasound, First-line imaging to identify biliary dilation and the level of obstruction.
MRCP or CT abdomen (if ultrasound inconclusive)
Better characterizes the cause and location of obstruction (stone, stricture, malignancy) to guide definitive drainage strategy.
Bile culture (at time of ERCP/drainage), Identifies the causative organism directly from the infected source for the most targeted de-escalation.
Immediate empiric antibiotics required
# Reason
Right upper quadrant pain, fever, and jaundice (Charcot's triad)
, together with hypotension and altered sensorium (Reynold's pentad),
are consistent with acute cholangitis, a condition that can progress rapidly to septic shock from biliary obstruction. Empiric broad-spectrum antibiotics must be started immediately, with urgent biliary drainage (ERCP or percutaneous) as the definitive source control measure — antibiotics alone are insufficient if obstruction is not relieved.
# Pathophysiology
Acute cholangitis arises from infection within an obstructed biliary tree, most often due to choledocholithiasis (common duct stones complicate about 10-15% of cholelithiasis). At least partial obstruction to bile flow combined with bacterial colonization of bile produces inflammation; rising intrabiliary pressure in an obstructed system promotes translocation of organisms into the bloodstream, causing bacteremia and biliary sepsis. In suppurative cholangitis, pus accumulates under pressure within a completely obstructed duct, driving severe toxicity.
# Prognosis
Nonsuppurative acute cholangitis generally responds well to antibiotics and supportive care. Suppurative acute cholangitis carries a far worse prognosis: response to antibiotics alone is poor and mortality approaches 100% unless prompt endoscopic or surgical relief of the obstruction with drainage of infected bile is achieved. Reynolds's pentad and undue delay in drainage both predict increased morbidity and mortality.
# Severity classification
Clinically, Charcot's triad (pain, jaundice, fever/rigors) defines the typical syndrome, and progression to Reynolds's pentad (adding shock and confusion) marks the severe/suppurative end of the spectrum requiring urgent biliary decompression.
# Clinical pearls
The classic presentation is Charcot's triad: biliary pain, jaundice, and spiking fevers with rigors; it is present in roughly 70% of patients with ascending cholangitis.
Addition of shock and confusion to Charcot's triad constitutes Reynolds's pentad, which signals high mortality and should trigger urgent biliary drainage.
Bile cultures are positive in approximately 75% of patients with acute cholangitis early in the symptomatic course, and blood cultures are frequently positive.
Most acute cholangitis is the nonsuppurative form, which often responds relatively quickly to supportive care and antibiotics.
Initial medical management (fluids and IV antibiotics) usually stabilizes the patient and buys a window of about 24 hours within which biliary drainage should be established, typically by ERCP.
The bile duct may not yet be dilated on early ultrasound during acute biliary obstruction, so a normal-caliber duct does not exclude cholangitis.
# Additional differentials
Biliary colic / choledocholithiasis without infection
Acute cholecystitis
Gallstone pancreatitis
Obstructive jaundice from malignancy of the pancreatic head, bile ducts, or ampulla of Vater (often painless jaundice)
Primary sclerosing cholangitis with superimposed bacterial cholangitis
# Infection mimics
Acute cholecystitis
Hepatic (pyogenic) abscess
Septic shock from another occult source (e.g., pneumonia, pyelonephritis) presenting before the biliary source is evident
# Non infectious mimics
Biliary colic from choledocholithiasis (pain without fever or rigors)
Painless obstructive jaundice from neoplastic biliary obstruction
IgG4-associated (sclerosing) cholangitis, which causes biliary stricturing and is glucocorticoid-responsive
Gallstone pancreatitis
# Additional investigations
Serum alkaline phosphatase (almost always elevated in biliary obstruction; rise may precede clinical jaundice)
Serum aminotransferases (two- to tenfold elevation, especially with acute obstruction)
ERCP with endoscopic sphincterotomy as the preferred initial procedure for both definitive diagnosis and therapeutic drainage
EUS as a less invasive alternative for detecting duct stones when the pretest probability of a persistent stone is lower
# Red flags
Reynolds's pentad (Charcot's triad plus shock and confusion) indicating suppurative cholangitis and high mortality
Mental confusion, bacteremia, and septic shock signaling suppurative cholangitis with pus under pressure
Failure to respond to antibiotics, which in suppurative disease is associated with multiple hepatic abscesses
Delay in establishing biliary drainage beyond the ~24 h stabilization window, risking recrudescent sepsis
# Pitfalls
Relying on antibiotics alone in suppurative cholangitis; biliary decompression is required because antibiotic response is poor and mortality is very high without it.
Excluding cholangitis because the bile duct appears non-dilated on early ultrasound, which can occur in acute obstruction.
Awaiting the full Charcot's triad before acting, since not all patients display all three components.
Mistaking the relentless toxicity of suppurative disease for simple sepsis without recognizing the need for urgent source control.
# Deescalation
Once biliary drainage is achieved and blood/bile cultures identify a susceptible organism, narrow to the most targeted single agent and limit total duration to 4-7 days from the time of adequate source control.
# Completeness
Piperacillin-tazobactam alone covers enterococcus in addition to gram-negatives and anaerobes, making it a more complete single-agent choice.
Organisms: E. coli/Klebsiella; Enterococcus; Anaerobes.

# Framework
Antibiotics alone do not resolve cholangitis if biliary obstruction persists — urgent source control (ERCP, percutaneous drainage, or surgery) is essential and should not be delayed while awaiting antibiotic response.
Limit duration to 4-7 days once adequate source control (drainage) has been achieved — antibiotics are frequently continued longer than necessary in this setting.
De-escalate based on bile/blood culture results once drainage is achieved and the patient is improving.
