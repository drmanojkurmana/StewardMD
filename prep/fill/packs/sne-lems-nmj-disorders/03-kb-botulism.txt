# Botulism
Botulism, Infectious Diseases.
# Pathophysiology
Disease is caused by botulinum neurotoxins (BoNTs) produced by Clostridium botulinum (rarely C. butyricum or C. baratii), gram-positive, spore-forming, anaerobic bacteria whose heat-resistant spores are ubiquitous in soil. Serotypes A, B, E, and F cause human disease. BoNT is a metalloprotease whose heavy chain mediates neurospecific binding and internalization and whose light chain enters the cytosol and cleaves SNARE-family proteins (e.g., SNAP-25, VAMP), disrupting synaptic vesicle fusion and blocking acetylcholine release at the neuromuscular junction, producing flaccid paralysis of voluntary muscles. Toxin binding is effectively irreversible, so recovery requires regeneration of nerve endings over weeks to months. Routes differ by form: foodborne (ingestion of preformed toxin in improperly preserved foods, often home-canned low-acid vegetables), wound (spore germination in wounds/necrotic tissue, notably from black tar heroin injection), infant and adult intestinal colonization (in situ toxin production by colonizing Clostridium), iatrogenic (high-dose therapeutic BoNT injection), and inhalational (aerosolized toxin as a bioweapon).
# Prognosis
More than 95% of noninfant botulism patients in the United States recover, often after protracted intensive care and rehabilitation, even without antitoxin; the survival rate for infant botulism is near 100%. Paralysis lasts weeks to months while affected nerve endings regenerate. Serotype A is associated with more rapid progression, more frequent respiratory compromise and mechanical ventilation, and longer-duration paralysis; type B is milder and shorter; type F progresses rapidly but recovers faster. Untreated, early death results from airway obstruction and inadequate tidal volume from respiratory muscle paralysis.
# Severity classification
At the mild end, some untreated patients progress no further than ptosis and mild palsy of one or two cranial nerves; at the severe end, others develop fulminant cranial nerve palsies and rapidly progressive descending flaccid paralysis affecting most or all voluntary muscles with respiratory failure requiring intubation and mechanical ventilation within hours.
# Clinical pearls
Classic syndrome: bilateral cranial nerve palsies followed by symmetric, descending flaccid paralysis, in a patient who is afebrile and fully conscious with normal sensorium.
First neurologic manifestation is usually ptosis, which can be striking; ocular findings include blurred/fuzzy vision or diplopia from extraocular palsies of cranial nerves III, IV, and VI.
Bulbar onset: dysarthria, difficulty swallowing, and nasal/oral regurgitation reflect cranial nerve involvement; descending weakness then spreads to neck, shoulders, and limbs, with proximal groups affected before distal.
Paralysis descends symmetrically and distal tendon reflexes diminish symmetrically; constipation from intestinal paralysis develops in almost all patients.
In a U.S. series of 332 cases, the most common reported symptoms were difficulty swallowing (86%), fatigue (85%), blurred vision (80%), and slurred speech (78%); signs included afebrile temperature (99%), descending paralysis (93%), alert/oriented status (93%), and ptosis (81%).
