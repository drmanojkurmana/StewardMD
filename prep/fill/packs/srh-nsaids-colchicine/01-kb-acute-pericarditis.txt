# Acute pericarditis
Adults
Emergency department, outpatient clinic, ward
Diagnose with 2 of 4 criteria (typical pain, rub, widespread ST elevation or PR depression, effusion), admit if red flags, otherwise treat as an outpatient with high-dose aspirin or an NSAID plus colchicine for 3 months, tapering by symptoms and CRP; avoid corticosteroids as first line.
Aliases: acute pericarditis; recurrent pericarditis; viral pericarditis; pericardial effusion; tuberculous pericarditis; TB pericarditis.
# Recognise and diagnose
Diagnosis needs at least 2 of: pericarditic chest pain (sharp, pleuritic, better sitting forward), pericardial friction rub, new widespread ST elevation or PR depression, new or worsening pericardial effusion.
Supporting findings: raised CRP, ESR or white cell count, and pericardial inflammation on CT or cardiac MRI.
Myopericarditis: pericarditis with raised troponin but without new global or regional LV dysfunction; perimyocarditis when LV function is impaired.
Causes: mostly idiopathic or viral; also post-MI or post-cardiac surgery, autoimmune disease, uraemia, malignancy, trauma, drugs. In India, tuberculosis is an important cause, especially with effusion, subacute course, fever, weight loss or HIV infection.
Distinguish from STEMI: pericarditis gives concave ST elevation across coronary territories with PR depression and no reciprocal ST depression (except aVR and V1).
# Investigations
ECG, CRP, CBC, troponin, creatinine, liver function and chest X-ray for everyone; echocardiography in all patients to assess effusion, tamponade and LV function.
Cardiac MRI if troponin is raised, LV function is abnormal or the diagnosis is uncertain.
Targeted tests: TB work-up (chest X-ray, sputum, IGRA or tuberculin test, HIV test), ANA and other autoimmune tests, thyroid function, blood cultures if febrile.
Pericardiocentesis for tamponade, or for suspected bacterial, tuberculous or malignant effusion: send fluid for cell count, Gram and AFB stain, culture, GeneXpert or TB PCR, adenosine deaminase (ADA above 40 U/L supports TB) and cytology.
# Red flags: admit
Major high-risk features: fever above 38 degrees C, subacute onset over days to weeks, large effusion (diastolic echo-free space above 20 mm), cardiac tamponade, no response to aspirin or NSAID after at least 1 week.
Minor high-risk features: myopericarditis, immunosuppression, trauma, oral anticoagulant therapy.
Any high-risk feature: admit and search for a specific cause. Patients without any can be managed as outpatients with review in about 1 week.
Tamponade (hypotension, raised JVP, pulsus paradoxus, RA or RV diastolic collapse on echo): urgent echo-guided pericardiocentesis; avoid diuretics and vasodilators.
# First-line treatment
High-dose aspirin or an NSAID (ibuprofen or indomethacin) plus colchicine, with PPI gastroprotection. Choose aspirin after MI or when antiplatelet therapy is already needed.
Continue full-dose aspirin or NSAID until symptoms resolve and CRP normalises (usually 1 to 2 weeks), then taper over the following weeks, guided by symptoms and CRP.
Colchicine for 3 months (without a loading dose) halves recurrence; for recurrent pericarditis continue at least 6 months.
Corticosteroids are second line: only if aspirin, NSAIDs and colchicine are contraindicated or have failed, after excluding infection (including TB). Use low doses (prednisolone 0.2 to 0.5 mg/kg/day) and taper slowly.
Restrict strenuous exercise until symptoms, CRP, ECG and echo have normalised. Competitive athletes need specialist clearance before return (traditionally at least 3 months); myopericarditis needs longer restriction, guided by cardiology.
# Recurrent and specific pericarditis
Recurrence (after a symptom-free interval of 4 to 6 weeks or more): restart aspirin or NSAID with colchicine for at least 6 months, with slower taper guided by CRP.
CRP-positive recurrences despite colchicine, or corticosteroid dependence: refer for anti-interleukin-1 therapy (anakinra or rilonacept); azathioprine or IV immunoglobulin are alternatives in specialist care.
Tuberculous pericarditis: full anti-TB treatment per the national programme (NTEP); watch for constriction and involve a specialist regarding adjunctive corticosteroids.
Purulent pericarditis: urgent drainage (pericardiocentesis or surgery) plus IV antibiotics; high mortality if missed.
# Special groups
Pregnancy: aspirin or NSAIDs only before 20 weeks' gestation; after 20 weeks use low-dose prednisolone. Seek specialist advice on colchicine.
CKD and elderly: reduce colchicine dose and avoid it in severe renal or hepatic impairment; watch NSAID nephrotoxicity.
Colchicine interactions: strong CYP3A4 or P-gp inhibitors (clarithromycin, azole antifungals, ciclosporin, verapamil) raise levels and toxicity; statins add myopathy risk.
Anticoagulated patients: pericarditis is a minor red flag (risk of haemopericardium); admit and review the anticoagulant.
# Pitfalls
Using corticosteroids first line: they increase recurrence.
Stopping NSAIDs abruptly or before CRP normalises, and stopping colchicine early.
Missing STEMI, aortic dissection or tamponade.
Not considering tuberculosis in effusive or subacute pericarditis in India.
# Drugs
Aspirin
# Dose
750 to 1000 mg orally every 8 hours for 1 to 2 weeks, then reduce by 250 to 500 mg every 1 to 2 weeks
Preferred after MI or if on antiplatelet therapy. Add PPI.
Ibuprofen
# Dose
600 mg orally every 8 hours for 1 to 2 weeks, then reduce by 200 to 400 mg every 1 to 2 weeks
Add PPI. Avoid in CKD, heart failure and after 20 weeks of pregnancy.
Indomethacin
# Dose
25 to 50 mg orally every 8 hours (start at the low end), then taper by 25 mg every 1 to 2 weeks
Alternative NSAID. Add PPI.
Colchicine
# Dose
0.5 mg orally once daily if under 70 kg, or 0.5 mg twice daily if 70 kg or more; 3 months for a first episode, at least 6 months for recurrence
No loading dose. Diarrhoea is common. Reduce in CKD and elderly; many interactions.
Prednisolone
# Dose
0.2 to 0.5 mg/kg/day orally, continued until symptoms resolve and CRP normalises, then slow taper over weeks to months
Second line only, after infection (including TB) is excluded.
Pantoprazole, 40 mg orally once daily while on high-dose aspirin or NSAID, Gastroprotection.
Anakinra, 1 to 2 mg/kg/day SC, up to 100 mg once daily, Specialist use in recurrent, CRP-positive, colchicine-resistant and corticosteroid-dependent pericarditis.
