# NSAIDs, colchicine and analgesics (grounding reference: Kelley & Firestein, Harrison, ACR/EULAR criteria)

Rheumatology topic srh-nsaids-colchicine: overview of pathophysiology, diagnostic criteria, clinical features, and management guidelines.

Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) inhibit cyclooxygenase enzymes (COX-1 and COX-2), blocking the conversion of arachidonic acid to prostaglandins; constitutive COX-1 maintains gastric mucosal protection and renal blood flow, while inducible COX-2 drives inflammatory pain, fever, and vascular endothelial prostacyclin (PGI2) synthesis.

The landmark PRECISION trial evaluated cardiovascular, gastrointestinal, and renal safety among 24,000 osteoarthritis and RA patients; moderate-dose celecoxib (100 to 200 mg daily) demonstrated non-inferiority to prescription naproxen and ibuprofen for cardiovascular events, with significantly lower gastrointestinal bleeding events.

Nephrotoxic mechanisms of NSAIDs stem from inhibition of vasodilatory prostaglandins (PGE2 and PGI2) that normally preserve renal blood flow and GFR during hypovolemia or heart failure; acute NSAID use triggers prerenal acute kidney injury via afferent arteriolar vasoconstriction, hyperkalemia (hyporeninemic hypoaldosteronism), and acute interstitial nephritis.

All NSAIDs (with the possible exception of low-dose naproxen) increase the risk of myocardial infarction, stroke, heart failure exacerbations, and systemic hypertension, caused by selective or non-selective suppression of endothelial COX-2-derived anti-thrombotic prostacyclin without concomitant suppression of platelet COX-1-derived pro-thrombotic thromboxane A2.

Colchicine exerts its primary anti-inflammatory mechanism by binding unpolymerized tubulin heterodimers, inhibiting microtubule polymerization and elongation; this disrupts neutrophil chemotaxis, lysosomal degranulation, phagocytosis, and suppresses activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome.

The AGREE trial established that low-dose colchicine (1.2 mg immediately followed by 0.6 mg 1 hour later, total 1.8 mg over 1 hour) achieves equivalent pain reduction to high-dose regimens (4.8 mg over 6 hours) in acute gout flares, while exhibiting a favorable gastrointestinal adverse effect profile indistinguishable from placebo.

Colchicine is metabolized hepatically by CYP3A4 and actively exported by the P-glycoprotein (ABCB1) efflux transporter; co-administration of strong CYP3A4 or P-glycoprotein inhibitors (clarithromycin, erythromycin, ketoconazole, cyclosporine) in renal or hepatic impairment causes fatal colchicine toxicity (neuromyopathy, bone marrow aplasia, shock).

Low-dose colchicine (0.5 mg daily) exerts vascular cardioprotective benefits: the LoDoCo2 and COLCOT randomized trials demonstrated that low-dose colchicine significantly reduced major adverse cardiovascular events (cardiovascular death, myocardial infarction, ischemic stroke) in chronic coronary disease and post-myocardial infarction.
