# Gout: acute flare and urate-lowering therapy
Adults
Emergency department, outpatient clinic, ward
Treat a flare early with low-dose colchicine, an NSAID or a glucocorticoid (prednisolone 30 to 35 mg daily for 5 days, or intra-articular). When indicated, start allopurinol at 100 mg/day or less (lower in CKD) and titrate to serum urate below 6 mg/dL, with 3 to 6 months of flare prophylaxis.
Aliases: gout flare; acute gouty arthritis; ULT; urate lowering therapy; treat to target; HLA-B*5801; tophaceous gout.
# Recognise
Rapid onset (peak within 24 hours) of severe pain, swelling and redness of one joint: first MTP joint (podagra), midfoot, ankle, knee, wrist or olecranon bursa. Flares settle over 1 to 2 weeks even untreated.
Serum urate may be normal during a flare; recheck 2 to 4 weeks after it settles.
Aspirate the joint when possible: negatively birefringent needle-shaped urate crystals confirm gout. If fever, immunosuppression, a prosthetic joint or diagnostic doubt, send Gram stain and culture: septic arthritis and gout can coexist.
Precipitants and risk factors: CKD, thiazide and loop diuretics, alcohol (especially beer), sugary drinks, obesity, metabolic syndrome, ciclosporin, and anti-TB drugs pyrazinamide and ethambutol (common in India).
# Investigations
Serum urate, creatinine and eGFR, liver enzymes, CBC, glucose or HbA1c, lipids; urinalysis.
X-ray of chronic joints (punched-out erosions with overhanging edges); ultrasound (double-contour sign) or dual-energy CT when the diagnosis is uncertain and aspiration is not possible.
Assess cardiovascular risk, hypertension, diabetes, CKD and renal stones, which often accompany gout.
# Flare treatment (start as early as possible)
Colchicine, an NSAID or a glucocorticoid are all first-line (ACR 2020, EULAR 2016); choose by comorbidity. Combine two agents for severe polyarticular flares. Ice and rest help.
Colchicine, low dose only, ideally within 12 hours of onset: 1 mg then 0.5 mg one hour later (EULAR; 0.5 mg tablets in India), or 1.2 mg then 0.6 mg one hour later (ACR). From the next day 0.5 mg once or twice daily until the flare settles. High-dose regimens add toxicity without benefit.
Avoid colchicine with strong CYP3A4 or P-glycoprotein inhibitors (clarithromycin, erythromycin, ketoconazole, itraconazole, ciclosporin, verapamil, diltiazem, ritonavir) and in severe renal or hepatic impairment.
NSAID at full dose for a short course, for example naproxen 500 mg twice daily, with a proton pump inhibitor if GI risk; avoid in CKD, heart failure, peptic ulcer, or with anticoagulants.
Prednisolone 30 to 35 mg orally once daily for 5 days (no taper needed) when colchicine and NSAIDs are unsuitable, including CKD.
Intra-articular corticosteroid for 1 or 2 accessible large joints once infection is excluded (for example triamcinolone acetonide 40 mg for a knee, less for smaller joints). IM or IV glucocorticoid if oral treatment is not possible.
IL-1 inhibitors (anakinra, canakinumab) only when all of the above are contraindicated or ineffective (specialist use).
# Urate-lowering therapy (ULT): treat to target
Start ULT (ACR 2020, strong): 1 or more tophi, radiographic damage from gout, or 2 or more flares a year. Conditional: more than 1 previous flare but fewer than 2 a year, or a first flare with CKD stage 3 or worse, urate above 9 mg/dL or urolithiasis. EULAR 2016 advises discussing ULT from the first presentation.
Allopurinol is first-line, including CKD stage 3 or worse. Start at 100 mg/day or less (50 mg/day or less in CKD) and increase by 100 mg (50 mg in CKD) every 2 to 4 weeks, guided by serum urate, up to 800 mg/day. Doses above 300 mg/day are often needed and are safe when titrated.
Target serum urate below 6 mg/dL (360 micromol/L); below 5 mg/dL (300 micromol/L) in severe gout with tophi, chronic arthropathy or frequent flares until crystals dissolve (EULAR). Check urate after each dose change.
Starting ULT during a flare is acceptable (ACR, conditional). Never stop established ULT because of a flare.
Flare prophylaxis for 3 to 6 months when starting ULT: colchicine 0.5 to 1 mg daily (0.5 mg daily or less in CKD), or a low-dose NSAID, or low-dose prednisolone.
Febuxostat if allopurinol is not tolerated or contraindicated: start 40 mg daily, increase to 80 mg if needed. ACR advises switching away from febuxostat in established cardiovascular disease.
Continue ULT indefinitely in most patients (ACR, conditional).
# Allopurinol safety and HLA-B*5801
Allopurinol hypersensitivity (DRESS, SJS/TEN) is most likely in the first months, with high starting doses, CKD, and in HLA-B*5801 carriers.
ACR 2020: test for HLA-B*5801 before allopurinol in people of Southeast Asian descent (Han Chinese, Korean, Thai) and African American patients; universal testing is not advised for others. The allele also occurs in South Asians, so test where available if the patient is at high risk (for example CKD); if positive, use febuxostat.
Where testing is unavailable: start low, titrate slowly, and tell the patient to stop allopurinol and seek care at the first rash, fever, mouth ulcers or sore eyes.
Allopurinol and febuxostat greatly increase azathioprine and mercaptopurine toxicity: avoid the combination or use only with specialist dose reduction and blood count monitoring.
# Lifestyle and co-medication (ACR 2020)
Limit alcohol, purine-rich foods (organ meat, some seafood) and high-fructose drinks; weight loss if overweight. Vitamin C supplements are not recommended.
Switch hydrochlorothiazide to another antihypertensive where feasible; losartan is preferred as it lowers urate. Do not stop low-dose aspirin taken for a valid indication; do not add fenofibrate just to lower urate.
Give a written flare plan with a supply of colchicine or NSAID to start at the first symptoms ('pill in the pocket').
# Pitfalls
Missing septic arthritis because urate is high, or excluding gout because urate is normal during the flare.
Old high-dose colchicine regimens, or colchicine with clarithromycin or in dialysis patients (fatal toxicity).
Starting allopurinol at 300 mg in CKD, or leaving patients on a fixed 100 mg dose without titrating to target.
Starting ULT without flare prophylaxis, or stopping ULT during a flare.
Co-prescribing allopurinol with azathioprine without dose adjustment.
# Drugs
Colchicine (flare)
# Dose
1 mg then 0.5 mg one hour later on day 1 (or 1.2 mg then 0.6 mg); then 0.5 mg once or twice daily until the flare settles
Best started within 12 hours of onset; less effective after 36 hours. Avoid with strong CYP3A4 or P-gp inhibitors and in severe renal or hepatic impairment.
Prednisolone
30 to 35 mg orally once daily for 5 days
Useful when NSAIDs and colchicine are contraindicated (CKD, heart failure, anticoagulation). Monitor glucose in diabetes.
Naproxen, 500 mg orally twice daily until 1 to 2 days after resolution, Add a PPI if GI risk. Avoid in CKD, heart failure, peptic ulcer, anticoagulation.
Triamcinolone acetonide (intra-articular), 40 mg into a knee; smaller doses for smaller joints, Only after excluding infection.
Allopurinol
# Dose
Start 100 mg daily (50 mg daily or less in CKD); increase by 100 mg (50 mg in CKD) every 2 to 4 weeks to reach urate below 6 mg/dL; maximum 800 mg daily
