# Hematopoietic Cell Transplantation
Hematopoietic Cell Transplantation, Hematology/Oncology.
Aliases: HCT; Bone marrow transplantation; Stem cell transplantation; Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation; HSCT.
# Pathophysiology
A single hematopoietic stem cell can regenerate the entire lympho-hematopoietic system, including red cells, granulocytes, B and T lymphocytes, platelets, and fixed-tissue macrophages (Kupffer cells, alveolar macrophages, osteoclasts, Langerhans cells). Infused cells home to the marrow via selectin/integrin adhesion and CXCL12-CXCR4 signaling. In allogeneic transplant, the central immunologic axis is HLA matching: HLA molecules present antigenic peptides to T cells, so mismatched 'major antigens' provoke strong T-cell reactions while even HLA-matched pairs can react against 'minor' endogenous antigens. Donor T cells reacting against host tissue cause graft-versus-host disease, whereas residual immunocompetent host cells can mediate graft rejection if conditioning is insufficiently immunosuppressive. The same alloreactivity underlies the beneficial graft-versus-tumor effect. Conditioning regimens combine antineoplastic activity, myelosuppression, and (for allogeneic) immunosuppression to eradicate disease and prevent rejection.
# Prognosis
Outcomes are highly disease- and stage-dependent (CIBMTR estimates, transplants 2010-2020). Reported approximate 3-year survival: severe combined immunodeficiency ~95% allogeneic; aplastic anemia ~98% allogeneic; thalassemia ~95% allogeneic; AML 57% (first remission) and 53% (second remission); ALL 64% (first remission) and 45% (second remission); CML 71% (chronic phase), 67% (accelerated), 28% (blast crisis); CLL ~64%; myelodysplasia ~50%; multiple myeloma ~80% autologous as initial therapy; non-Hodgkin lymphoma 45% allogeneic / 55% autologous (first relapse/second remission); Hodgkin disease 68% allogeneic / 89% autologous (first relapse/second remission). Matched-unrelated-donor survival now essentially equals matched-sibling survival. Sinusoidal obstruction syndrome carries ~30% mortality; chronic GVHD mortality averages ~15% (range 5-50% by severity). HLA-matched sibling allogeneic transplant carries roughly a 1-3% graft rejection risk and ~15% risk of severe acute GVHD.
# Severity classification
Acute GVHD is staged per organ and combined into an overall grade. Skin stage 1-4 (rash <25% body surface up to generalized desquamation/bullae); liver stage by bilirubin from 34-51 umol/L [2-3 mg/dL] up to >257 umol/L [>15 mg/dL]; gut stage by diarrhea volume from 500-1000 mL/d up to ileus. Overall grade I (skin 1-2 only) is clinically insignificant; grades II-IV reflect progressively greater organ involvement, worse survival, and need for aggressive therapy. Myelodysplasia transplant timing uses the International Prognostic Scoring System (IPSS), with transplant generally reserved for Int-2 or higher (or selected Int-1 with poor features).
# Clinical pearls
Hematopoietic cell transplantation (HCT) is the umbrella term that has replaced 'bone marrow transplantation,' reflecting that hematopoietic stem cells can be sourced from marrow, growth-factor-mobilized peripheral blood, or umbilical cord blood.
It is used both to replace a defective but non-malignant lympho-hematopoietic system with a normal donor system and to treat malignancy, the latter by permitting higher myelosuppressive doses and, in allogeneic transplant, by adding an immunologic graft-versus-tumor effect.
Transplant categories are syngeneic (identical twin, ~1% of cases, no GVHD and no tumor contamination), allogeneic (genetically non-identical donor, risk of GVHD and graft rejection), and autologous (patient's own stored cells, no GVHD/rejection but lacks a graft-versus-tumor effect and may carry contaminating tumor cells).
The chance any one full sibling is HLA-matched is 1 in 4; the probability a patient has an HLA-identical sibling is 1 - (0.75)^n where n is the number of siblings.
With registries exceeding 40 million volunteer donors, an HLA-matched unrelated donor can be found for about 60% of patients, with higher rates in Caucasians and lower rates in minorities and people of mixed ancestry; a search and scheduling typically takes 3-4 months.
Autologous engraftment is reliable when more than 2.5 x 10^6 CD34+ cells/kg are infused; the 5-10% of patients who mobilize poorly with growth factor alone may benefit from plerixafor, a CXCR4 antagonist that releases stem cells from the marrow niche.
Stem cell homing depends on P- and E-selectin/integrin (e.g., VLA-4) interactions and on niche-derived CXCL12 binding CXCR4 on the stem cell; human HSCs tolerate cryopreservation, enabling autologous storage.
After marrow grafting, granulocytes recover to ~100/uL by about day 16 and ~500/uL by day 22; mobilized peripheral blood speeds recovery by ~1 week while cord blood delays it by ~1 week.
Innate immunity recovers quickly, but adaptive (T- and B-cell) immunity may take 1-2 years; early T-cell recovery is dominated by infused donor CD8+ T cells with a limited repertoire, with diverse de novo CD4+/CD8+ cells emerging months later.
Reduced-intensity and nonmyeloablative conditioning rely heavily on the graft-versus-tumor effect; they carry higher relapse but lower transplant-related mortality, favoring older patients and those with comorbidities, whereas high-dose conditioning is preferred when measurable disease is present and the patient can tolerate it.
Evidence for the graft-versus-tumor effect: relapse is lowest in patients who develop acute and chronic GVHD, higher without GVHD, and highest after T-cell-depleted allogeneic or syngeneic grafts; donor lymphocyte infusions can induce remission in post-transplant relapse.
Worldwide more than 100,000 transplants were performed in 2022, with frequency closely tracking gross national income per capita.
# Additional differentials
Acute GVHD versus conditioning regimen toxicity, drug eruption, or infection as a cause of post-transplant rash, diarrhea, and hepatic dysfunction (biopsy of skin, liver, or gut often needed for confirmation)
Sinusoidal obstruction syndrome versus other causes of tender hepatomegaly, jaundice, and fluid retention early after transplant
Transplant-associated thrombotic microangiopathy versus other microangiopathic processes (schistocytes, elevated LDH, thrombocytopenia, acute kidney injury)
# Infection mimics
Idiopathic/diffuse interstitial pneumonia from conditioning toxicity can mimic infectious pneumonia early post-transplant
Cryptogenic organizing pneumonia and bronchiolitis obliterans (late, often GVHD-associated) can mimic chronic respiratory infection
# Non infectious mimics
Many noninfectious conditions can mimic acute GVHD, so histologic confirmation is usually required
Post-transplant autoimmune phenomena (autoimmune hemolytic anemia, immune thrombocytopenia) in 3-5% can mimic relapse-related or drug-induced cytopenias
# Additional investigations
HLA typing of HLA-A, -B, -C, and -D (and DRB1) to assess donor-recipient matching; AML/ALL candidates should be HLA-typed soon after diagnosis
Screening recipients for donor-directed anti-HLA antibodies before transplant, since their presence predicts poor engraftment
Chimerism/engraftment documentation by fluorescence in situ hybridization of sex chromosomes (sex-mismatched pairs) or short tandem repeat polymorphism analysis after DNA amplification
CD34 antigen quantification or colony-forming unit assays to gauge stem cell dose adequacy
PCR monitoring of blood for CMV (and other viral) DNA to guide preemptive therapy
Minimal residual disease assessment by multidimensional flow cytometry or molecular methods to refine transplant candidacy in acute leukemia
GVHD severity biomarkers including ST2, REG3a, and TNF R1
# Red flags
Tender hepatomegaly, jaundice, ascites, and fluid retention within the first month (peak ~day 16) suggest sinusoidal obstruction syndrome, which carries ~30% mortality and can progress to fatal hepatorenal syndrome
Erythematous maculopapular rash, persistent anorexia or diarrhea, and rising bilirubin/transaminases/alkaline phosphatase in the first 3 months point to acute GVHD; grades II-IV require aggressive therapy
Schistocytes, elevated LDH, thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury about a month post-transplant indicate transplant-associated thrombotic microangiopathy
Diffuse fluffy pulmonary infiltrates with alveolar hemorrhage on lavage suggest conditioning-related diffuse alveolar damage rather than infection
Failure of marrow function to return, or loss after initial engraftment, signals graft failure/rejection; persistence of host-origin lymphocytes indicates immunologic rejection
Fever once the granulocyte count falls below 500/uL prompts broad-spectrum empiric antibacterial therapy because infection risk is so high
Community respiratory viruses (RSV, parainfluenza, influenza, metapneumovirus) can be life-threatening or fatal in transplant recipients
# Pitfalls
Premedication is not a reliable solution: in transfusion-related GVHD analogies aside, here the key pitfall is assuming infectious causes for early rash/diarrhea/hepatic dysfunction; acute GVHD typically needs biopsy because many conditions mimic it
Grade I acute GVHD is clinically minor, does not affect survival, and does not require treatment; over-treating it adds unnecessary immunosuppression
Mobilized peripheral blood gives faster engraftment but, in matched-unrelated-donor transplant, increases chronic GVHD without a compensatory survival advantage
No prospective randomized trial has shown that purging autologous grafts of tumor cells reduces relapse or improves survival
Standard high-dose conditioning is poorly tolerated if given within 100 days of a first transplant due to cumulative toxicity; reduced-intensity regimens may be needed for graft failure salvage
Patients with Fanconi anemia are abnormally sensitive to alkylating agents, so less intensive conditioning must be used
Vaccine-preventable antibody titers wane after transplant unless recipients are revaccinated; live vaccines (MMR/MMR-V) are generally avoided until 2 years post-transplant
Reinfusing donor stem cells into a patient with immunologic graft rejection is usually futile unless preceded by a second immunosuppressive conditioning regimen
# Management
Select graft type (syngeneic, allogeneic or autologous) and source (marrow, mobilised peripheral blood or cord blood) matched to the underlying disease and donor availability
Optimise HLA matching for allogeneic transplant, screen for recipient donor-directed anti-HLA antibodies, and mobilise autologous stem cells with growth factor, adding the CXCR4 antagonist plerixafor in poor mobilisers
Deliver a conditioning regimen of myeloablative or reduced-intensity chemoradiotherapy to achieve immunosuppression and disease control before stem-cell infusion
Prevent and treat graft-versus-host disease with calcineurin-inhibitor-based immunosuppression such as ciclosporin or tacrolimus with methotrexate, and post-transplant cyclophosphamide where used
Provide antimicrobial prophylaxis and pre-emptive therapy, with PCR monitoring of blood for cytomegalovirus and other viruses to guide treatment
Support the cytopenic period with transfusion, neutropenic-sepsis management and nutritional support
Document engraftment and chimerism by short-tandem-repeat or FISH analysis and monitor minimal residual disease to detect relapse
Manage in a specialist transplant centre with long-term surveillance for chronic graft-versus-host disease, late effects and secondary malignancy
