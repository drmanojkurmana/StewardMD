Adult pelviureteric junction obstruction has two classic mechanisms. The intrinsic form is an aperistaltic segment where the ureteral muscle cannot propagate a wave, so urine stalls at the junction. The extrinsic form is compression by crossing lower pole accessory renal vessels, found in 30 to 50 percent of cases, which kink the junction from outside. Both produce the same upstream hydronephrosis, and both are fixed by operations that remove or bypass the faulty segment.

Diuretic renography separates true obstruction from a merely capacious pelvis. After furosemide, 99mTc-MAG3 clearance half-time above 20 minutes confirms obstruction, 10 to 20 minutes is equivocal and under 10 minutes is normal drainage. The test is physiological rather than anatomical, so it answers the exact question the surgeon asks: does this junction need an operation or will it drain once diuresis ends.

The Anderson-Hynes dismembered pyeloplasty remains the definitive repair and succeeds in over 95 percent of cases by open, laparoscopic, or robotic routes. The stenotic segment is excised completely, the pelvis is trimmed and re-anastomosed to the spatulated ureter over a stent, and any crossing vessel is transposed behind the repair. Because the diseased tissue is removed rather than patched, recurrence stays minimal across decades of follow-up.

Endopyelotomy trades a smaller operation for narrower indications. Through an endoscope or percutaneous track, the surgeon makes a full-thickness posterolateral incision through the stenotic ring, deliberately cutting away from anterior crossing vessels, then stents the split segment while it heals open. It suits primary strictures under 1.5 cm with minimal hydronephrosis and a well-preserved kidney, while long or redo strictures still need formal pyeloplasty.

Non-dismembered repairs keep the ureter in continuity for selected anatomy. The Fenger plasty opens the stricture longitudinally and closes it transversely, widening the lumen without transection. The Foley Y-V pyeloplasty advances a V-shaped pelvic flap into the upper ureter, which corrects a high ureteral insertion at the same time. Both are reserved for secondary strictures or high insertions where no crossing vessel compresses the junction.

Dietl crisis is the dramatic presentation of intermittent obstruction. In patients with movable kidneys, walking or standing lets the kidney drop, kinking the junction and ballooning the pelvis until severe flank pain, nausea, and vomiting force the patient to lie down. Recumbency unkinks the ureter, the pelvis drains, and the pain vanishes, only to return with the next long walk. The episodic pattern points straight at a mobile kidney with nephroptosis.

The Whitaker test measures obstruction directly with pressure. Saline is perfused antegrade into the renal pelvis at 10 mL per minute while pelvic and bladder pressures are recorded together, and a differential above 22 cmH2O proves obstruction regardless of what the images suggest. It is invasive, so it is kept for equivocal renography or for kidneys where the drainage question decides between reconstruction and removal.

Function decides urgency. A symptomatic obstruction in a solitary functioning kidney threatens the patient's entire renal reserve, and a differential function sliding below 40 percent signals a kidney losing ground. Either finding warrants prompt decompression with a stent or nephrostomy followed by definitive correction, because waiting converts a reconstructable kidney into a nephrectomy specimen.
