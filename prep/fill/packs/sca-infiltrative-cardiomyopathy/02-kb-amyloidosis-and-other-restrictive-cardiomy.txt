# Amyloidosis and Other Restrictive Cardiomyopathies
Amyloidosis and Other Restrictive Cardiomyopathies, Cardiovascular.
Aliases: Cardiac amyloidosis; Restrictive cardiomyopathy; RCM; AL amyloid cardiomyopathy; Transthyretin cardiac amyloidosis; ATTR cardiomyopathy; ATTRwt; ATTRv.
# Pathophysiology
Restrictive cardiomyopathy results from a noncompliant ventricle with markedly impaired diastolic filling; most cases are 'infiltrative,' due to deposition of abnormal substances between myocytes (amyloid being the most common), with additional causes from intramyocyte storage of metabolic products (e.g., iron in hemochromatosis, Fabry disease, glycogen storage disease) or acquired fibrosis (radiation, scleroderma, endomyocardial/hypereosinophilic injury, carcinoid). In amyloidosis, fibrillar proteinaceous material is deposited extracellularly; amyloid stains with Congo red and shows apple-green birefringence under polarized light, and electron microscopy reveals nonbranching fibrils about 10 nm in diameter. Extracellular amyloid deposition expands the extracellular space, increasing heart mass and ventricular wall thickness while reducing compliance; the left ventricular cavity may be relatively small (restrictive physiology) and both atria dilate from chronically elevated biventricular filling pressures. AL amyloidosis is a plasma cell disorder closely related to multiple myeloma in which excess abnormal free light chains (more often lambda than kappa) deposit in multiple organs, with heart and kidney most commonly affected. TTR amyloidosis arises from liver-produced transthyretin, either wild-type (ATTRwt) or from a destabilizing genetic variant (ATTRv); variant TTR is generally considered less stable than wild-type. Both wild-type and variant TTR cardiomyopathy have a subclinical phase of probably several years, and by the time heart failure occurs 30-50% of the involved heart weight may be amyloid.
# Prognosis
Prognosis depends heavily on amyloid type. AL cardiac amyloidosis is aggressive and, with cardiac involvement, carries the worst survival, with median survival from onset of heart failure to death of about 6 months in untreated patients, underscoring the urgency of diagnosis. Among TTR forms, untreated Val122Ile variant disease tends to progress relatively rapidly with median survival of 2-3 years from onset of heart failure, whereas wild-type TTR amyloidosis has an untreated median survival of about 4-5 years, with death usually from progressive congestive heart failure rather than sudden death; wild-type disease occurring in women tends to present later and follow a more indolent course. There is a high risk of thromboembolism in cardiac amyloidosis, and sudden cardiac death may occur (more common in advanced AL disease). Overall, cardiac amyloidosis is now recognized as far more common than previously appreciated, and earlier diagnosis with the advent of disease-modifying therapy has improved outlook.
# Severity classification
Adverse markers include disproportionately elevated NT-proBNP (often 1000-2000 pg/mL even with mild heart failure) and elevated high-sensitivity troponin, with the proportion of heart weight composed of amyloid reaching 30-50% by the time heart failure occurs in TTR disease. AL cardiac involvement confers the worst prognosis among the types.
# Clinical pearls
Restrictive cardiomyopathy is dominated by abnormal diastolic function in a noncompliant ventricle with usually only mildly reduced contractility; ejection fraction is often 30-50% and both atria are typically enlarged, sometimes massively.
Despite biventricular elevation of filling pressures, restrictive disease and cardiac amyloidosis tend to present with predominantly right-sided symptoms such as peripheral edema, abdominal discomfort, and ascites.
In sinus rhythm a fourth heart sound is more typical than a third in RCM generally, but in cardiac amyloidosis both an S3 and an S4 are unusual because restrictive physiology impairs relaxation and the infiltrated atrium generates little contractile pressure; jugular venous pressure is elevated and may rise paradoxically on inspiration (positive Kussmaul sign).
The two precursor proteins causing most cardiac amyloidosis are transthyretin (TTR, produced in the liver) and light chains (produced by abnormal plasma cells); precise typing of the precursor is essential because therapy is type-specific and incorrect typing leads to inappropriate treatment.
Periorbital purpura is virtually pathognomonic of AL amyloidosis (occurring in about 20-30% of cases) and macroglossia occurs in roughly 10%; an unusually hard liver may reflect amyloid hepatic infiltration, which occurs in AL but not TTR amyloidosis.
A history of bilateral carpal tunnel syndrome, spinal stenosis, or a ruptured biceps tendon — often 5-8 years before heart failure — points toward TTR amyloidosis, reflecting TTR's predilection for ligaments and tendons.
Longitudinal strain imaging classically shows relatively preserved apical strain with severely impaired basal strain, producing a 'bull's-eye' color pattern highly suggestive of cardiac amyloidosis.
On cardiac MRI, abnormal nulling of the myocardium with poor myocardium-to-cavity distinction plus extensive transmural late gadolinium enhancement and markedly increased extracellular volume are near-pathognomonic of amyloid infiltration.
Bone-avid tracers (technetium pyrophosphate/PYP, DPD, HMDP) are normally not taken up by the heart but show avid myocardial uptake in TTR amyloidosis; a positive scan with a negative plasma cell dyscrasia workup confirms TTR amyloidosis without biopsy.
In the United States the most common variant causing TTR amyloid cardiomyopathy is Val122Ile, present in about 3.5% of people of African descent, presenting as a late-onset restrictive cardiomyopathy usually from the seventh decade with incomplete penetrance.
Wild-type TTR amyloidosis (ATTRwt) is now considered the most common form of amyloidosis, affecting predominantly older men (men roughly 20 times more likely than women); the heart is usually the sole clinically involved organ, though lung and gut deposits are often found histologically.
Atrial arrhythmia, especially atrial fibrillation or flutter, is a common presenting feature (particularly in TTR amyloidosis) and its onset often precipitates or worsens heart failure; refractory atrial arrhythmia in a patient in the mid-60s or older without other cause should raise suspicion for TTR cardiac amyloidosis.
# Additional differentials
Constrictive pericardial disease (the classic and most important differential, sharing dominant right-sided failure, edema, ascites, elevated JVP and Kussmaul sign; echocardiography readily distinguishes the two today)
Hypertensive heart disease (usually shows LVH on ECG and requires years of severe uncontrolled hypertension to reach amyloid-degree wall thickening)
Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (rarely presents with peripheral edema and usually has LVH on ECG; distinction from amyloidosis is usually fairly clear)
Fabry disease and other infiltrative cardiomyopathies (may have typical skin manifestations and usually LVH on ECG)
Mitochondrial cardiomyopathy (echocardiographic appearance can mimic amyloidosis but with distinct noncardiac features such as maternally inherited diabetes, deafness, and recurrent strokes)
Advanced diabetic heart disease with heavy proteinuria (obvious diabetes history and echocardiogram lacking amyloid features)
# Non infectious mimics
Constrictive pericarditis
Hypertensive heart disease with left ventricular hypertrophy
Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (including sarcomere-variant forme fruste presenting as RCM)
Fabry disease and other infiltrative or storage cardiomyopathies
Mitochondrial cardiomyopathy
Hemochromatosis (iron storage)
# Additional investigations
Echocardiography showing increased LV wall thickness without dilation, frequent biatrial enlargement, increased RV free-wall thickness, restrictive diastolic filling pattern, and a small transmitral A wave (the latter associated with increased thromboembolic risk)
Longitudinal strain imaging demonstrating apical-sparing 'bull's-eye' pattern
Cardiac magnetic resonance with extracellular volume measurement, abnormal myocardial nulling, and extensive late gadolinium enhancement
Bone-avid radionuclide imaging (technetium PYP, DPD, or HMDP) with SPECT and ideally cardiac CT to confirm myocardial rather than blood-pool uptake
Serum free light chains plus serum and urine protein electrophoresis and immunofixation to detect or exclude a plasma cell dyscrasia (immunofixation is more sensitive than electrophoresis for the low paraprotein levels of AL amyloidosis)
Tissue biopsy (subcutaneous fat pad aspirate or deep-skin biopsy first; endomyocardial biopsy if needed, positive in well over 90% of cardiac amyloid cases) with amyloid typing ideally by mass spectrometry
TTR gene sequencing to distinguish familial (variant) from wild-type TTR when AL is not clearly established
NT-proBNP (often disproportionately high for the degree of heart failure, frequently 1000-2000 pg/mL even with mild symptoms) and high-sensitivity troponin (frequently mildly elevated, risking misdiagnosis as ischemic disease)
ECG, which may show abnormally low voltage (particularly in AL) though not invariably
# Red flags
Heart failure with a thick left ventricle but without a history of severe hypertension and without LVH on ECG
Disproportionately elevated NT-proBNP relative to clinical heart failure severity
Periorbital purpura or macroglossia (suggesting AL amyloidosis)
Refractory atrial fibrillation/flutter in a patient in the mid-60s or older without other cause (suggesting TTR amyloidosis)
Bilateral carpal tunnel syndrome, spinal stenosis, or ruptured biceps tendon preceding heart failure (suggesting TTR amyloidosis)
Abnormal postural blood-pressure drop indicating autonomic neuropathy (AL or some variant TTR forms)
Unexplained nonpostural dizziness or syncope with bifascicular or left bundle branch block in TTR amyloidosis, indicating risk of high-degree atrioventricular block
Significant proteinuria with hypoalbuminemia (renal AL involvement)
# Pitfalls
Mildly elevated high-sensitivity troponin may lead to misdiagnosis as ischemic heart disease.
AL amyloidosis can occasionally show myocardial uptake on bone-avid tracer scans, so a positive nuclear scan must never be interpreted as diagnostic of TTR without first excluding a plasma cell dyscrasia.
Monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS) is common in older patients and not uncommonly coexists with TTR amyloidosis, so abnormal plasma cell dyscrasia testing does not automatically equal AL amyloidosis and may require hematology input.
Serum and urine protein electrophoresis may miss the low-level paraprotein of AL amyloidosis; immunofixation is more sensitive and should be performed.
The sedimentation rate is usually normal or only minimally elevated in AL amyloidosis, unlike the marked elevation in multiple myeloma, so a normal ESR does not exclude AL amyloidosis.
Immunohistochemical typing of amyloid is fraught with diagnostic uncertainty and can be misleading; typing is ideally done by mass spectrometry.
There is no definitive noninvasive test for AL cardiac amyloidosis, so a tissue biopsy is always required to confirm it.
