Bladder injuries divide by the peritoneum. Extraperitoneal rupture, a tear leaking outside the bladder sac, causes 60% to 70% of bladder injuries and follows pelvic fractures that lacerate the bladder neck. The intact peritoneum walls off the urine, so large bore urethral catheter drainage for 10 to 14 days heals most tears. Intraperitoneal rupture, a tear venting into the belly, causes 25% to 35% and follows a blow to a distended bladder that bursts the bladder dome, the weakest roof. Urine in the peritoneal cavity causes chemical peritonitis, so urgent formal operative repair in layers with absorbable sutures is mandatory.

Retrograde cystography or CT cystography proves a rupture. The bladder must be distended with 300 mL to 350 mL of contrast run in under gravity, because an underfilled bladder hides tears and returns false negative films. A post drainage film completes the study, and flame shaped plumes of escaped contrast mark the leak.

Posterior urethral disruption tears the membranous urethra at the bulbomembranous junction when a pelvic ring fracture shears the prostate off the urethra. The classic triad is blood at meatus (blood seen at the external opening), inability to void, and a high-riding prostate felt as a boggy displaced gland on rectal examination. Retrograde urethrography, abbreviated RUG, must precede any catheter attempt: contrast is injected into the meatus and an intact channel to the bladder must be demonstrated, because blind cannulation drives a false passage through the tear.

Anterior injuries come from straddle trauma, a perineal blow onto a bar that crushes the bulbar urethra against the inferior pubic symphysis. Perineal bruising with blood at meatus and a normally positioned prostate point anteriorly. When the superficial Colles fascia of the perineum is breached, urine extravasation spreads through the scrotum, penis, and anterior abdominal wall as high as the clavicles, halting only at the fascia lata of the thighs. An intact deep Buck fascia of the penis confines the leak to the shaft.

Complete posterior urethral distraction is managed first with a suprapubic cystostomy, a bladder drain placed through the lower belly, and definitively with delayed end to end anastomotic urethroplasty at 3 to 6 months, once the fibrous scar has matured for excision. Early endoscopic primary realignment, railroading a catheter across the defect over guidewires, is reserved for select haemodynamically stable patients, because delayed repair carries the highest durable patency.
