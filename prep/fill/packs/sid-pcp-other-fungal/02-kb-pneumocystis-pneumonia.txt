# Pneumocystis pneumonia (PCP) in adults
Adults
Emergency department, ward, ICU, ART clinic
Treat for 21 days with high-dose co-trimoxazole (trimethoprim 15 to 20 mg/kg/day), add prednisolone within 72 hours if PaO2 is below 70 mmHg or the A-a gradient above 35 mmHg, start ART within 2 weeks, and continue secondary prophylaxis until CD4 recovery.
Aliases: PCP; PJP; Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia; Pneumocystis carinii; Septran; Bactrim; HIV pneumonia; PCP prophylaxis.
# Recognise
HIV: subacute progressive exertional breathlessness, dry cough and fever over days to weeks, usually with CD4 below 200 cells/mm3; oral thrush is common. Chest examination is often normal.
Without HIV (corticosteroids, transplant, chemotherapy, biologics): onset is usually more abrupt and mortality higher.
Hypoxaemia is the hallmark, with desaturation on exertion. Chest X-ray shows bilateral perihilar interstitial infiltrates or may be normal early; pneumothorax should raise suspicion of PCP.
Severity: mild PaO2 above 70 mmHg (9.3 kPa) with A-a gradient below 35 mmHg; moderate A-a gradient 35 to 45 mmHg; severe A-a gradient above 45 mmHg.
Cavities, effusion or lymphadenopathy suggest another or additional diagnosis; 13% to 18% have a concurrent lung infection (TB, bacterial pneumonia) or Kaposi sarcoma.
# Investigations
Room-air arterial blood gas (PaO2 and A-a gradient) and SpO2 at rest and on exertion. LDH is often raised but non-specific.
Induced sputum or bronchoalveolar lavage for staining (silver, Giemsa, immunofluorescence) or PCR. Expectorated sputum is not suitable. Serum beta-D-glucan supports the diagnosis.
CT chest showing patchy ground-glass change supports PCP when the chest X-ray is normal.
HIV test and CD4 count; sputum TB molecular test; blood cultures; CBC, creatinine and potassium; G6PD before dapsone or primaquine.
Start treatment before confirmation when suspicion is high: organisms persist in samples for days to weeks after treatment starts.
# Antimicrobial treatment (21 days)
Moderate to severe: co-trimoxazole, trimethoprim 15 to 20 mg/kg/day with sulfamethoxazole 75 to 100 mg/kg/day, IV in 3 or 4 divided doses (6 or 8-hourly); switch to oral after clinical improvement. Total 21 days.
Mild to moderate: the same daily dose orally in 3 divided doses, or 2 double-strength tablets (160/800 mg each) three times daily, for 21 days.
Reduce the dose in renal impairment. Do not add folinic acid (higher failure rate).
Moderate to severe alternatives: pentamidine 4 mg/kg IV once daily over at least 60 minutes (some use 3 mg/kg for toxicity), or primaquine 15 to 30 mg base orally daily plus clindamycin 600 to 900 mg IV 6 to 8-hourly.
Mild to moderate alternatives: dapsone 100 mg daily + trimethoprim 5 mg/kg three times daily; or primaquine 15 to 30 mg base daily + clindamycin 300 to 450 mg orally 6 to 8-hourly; or atovaquone 750 mg twice daily with food.
Do not use aerosolised pentamidine for treatment. Treat through mild rash or fever with antihistamines and antipyretics before abandoning co-trimoxazole.
# Adjunctive corticosteroids
Indication: PaO2 below 70 mmHg on room air or A-a gradient above 35 mmHg. Start as early as possible and within 72 hours of starting PCP treatment.
Prednisolone (prednisone) 40 mg orally twice daily on days 1 to 5, then 40 mg once daily on days 6 to 10, then 20 mg once daily on days 11 to 21.
If oral treatment is not possible, give IV methylprednisolone at 75% of the prednisolone dose.
Benefit if started after 72 hours is unknown, but most clinicians still give them for moderate to severe disease.
The trial evidence for steroids comes from people with HIV; in PCP without HIV the benefit is not established, so decide case by case.
# Monitoring and treatment failure
Early, reversible worsening in the first 3 to 5 days is common without steroids. Define failure as no improvement or worsening gas exchange after at least 4 to 8 days, and wait this long before switching for lack of efficacy.
On failure, exclude a concurrent infection (repeat bronchoscopy with lavage) and switch to IV pentamidine or primaquine plus clindamycin.
Co-trimoxazole toxicity: rash (including Stevens-Johnson syndrome), fever, leucopenia, thrombocytopenia, hepatitis, hyperkalaemia and rising creatinine. Check CBC, potassium and creatinine regularly.
Pentamidine: hypoglycaemia or hyperglycaemia, hypotension, pancreatitis, nephrotoxicity, electrolyte disturbance and arrhythmia. Dapsone and primaquine: methaemoglobinaemia and haemolysis (G6PD deficiency).
Rising oxygen requirement: escalate early to HDU or ICU. Up to half of ventilated patients survive, so offer intensive care to patients with reasonable functional status.
# Prophylaxis and ART
Secondary prophylaxis straight after treatment: co-trimoxazole 1 double-strength tablet (960 mg) or 1 single-strength tablet (480 mg) orally once daily.
Primary prophylaxis, US criteria: CD4 below 200 cells/mm3 or oral candidiasis; consider if CD4 percentage is below 14% or after an AIDS-defining illness. WHO criteria: CD4 350 or below or WHO stage 3 or 4, and everyone where malaria or severe bacterial infections are common.
Stop primary or secondary prophylaxis when CD4 rises above 200 cells/mm3 for more than 3 months on ART; restart if CD4 falls below 200.
Co-trimoxazole intolerance: after a non-life-threatening reaction, try reintroduction or desensitisation. Alternatives: dapsone 100 mg daily (check G6PD), atovaquone 1500 mg daily, or aerosolised pentamidine 300 mg monthly.
Not on ART: start ART within 2 weeks of starting PCP treatment. Unlike cryptococcal or TB meningitis, PCP does not need ART to be deferred.
