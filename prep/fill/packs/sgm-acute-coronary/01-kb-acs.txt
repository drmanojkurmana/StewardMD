# Pathophysiology
Myocardial ischemia (angina pectoris) arises from an imbalance between myocardial oxygen demand (raised by heart rate, ventricular wall stress, and contractility) and oxygen supply (set by coronary blood flow and arterial oxygen content). ACS is typically precipitated by acute coronary atherothrombosis. When ischemia is sufficiently severe and prolonged (as little as ~20 min), irreversible cellular injury and MI occur. Symptoms can also be driven predominantly by increased demand (e.g., intense psychological stress) or reduced delivery (anemia, hypoxia, hypotension), underlying the type 2 MI concept.
# Prognosis
Among unselected ED chest-pain populations, only ~10-20% (as low as 5%) are ultimately diagnosed with ACS, while gastrointestinal causes predominate; historically 2-6% of patients discharged with presumed non-ischemic pain were later found to have had a missed MI. In patients stratified as low risk (excluding ST elevation or definite noncardiac pain), the estimated 30-day major cardiovascular event rate was ~2.5%. Validated clinical decision pathways combining ECG and serial high-sensitivity troponin can achieve ACS miss rates <0.5%, and a single very low hs-troponin >2-3 h after symptom onset carries a negative predictive value >99% for MI.
# Severity classification
ACS is categorized along two clinical decision-making lines. (1) Diagnostic/disposition risk in undifferentiated chest pain uses clinical decision pathways such as the HEART score (history, ECG, age, risk factors; low risk 0-3) and EDACS, combined with serial high-sensitivity troponin, to classify patients as low/intermediate/high risk. (2) Prognostic risk in established ACS uses the TIMI and GRACE risk scores to estimate event risk - these are explicitly not intended for diagnostic use. Common high-risk elements across tools: typical ACS symptoms, older age, known/atherosclerotic risk factors, ischemic ECG changes, and elevated troponin.
# Clinical pearls
ACS encompasses unstable angina, NSTEMI, and STEMI, and the term is generally reserved for ischemia precipitated by acute coronary atherothrombosis.
Unstable ischemic heart disease is sub-classified by whether acute myocardial injury is detectable and whether ST-segment elevation is present: unstable angina = no detectable acute injury; NSTEMI/STEMI = acute injury without/with ST elevation.
Type 1 MI (acute coronary thrombosis) is distinguished from type 2 MI, which results from another imbalance of myocardial oxygen supply and demand; type 2 may be driven by anemia, hypoxia, hypotension, or intense psychological stress.
An ECG is recommended within 10 minutes of presentation, primarily to identify ST-segment elevation candidates for immediate reperfusion.
Resting ECG sensitivity for ischemia is poor (as low as ~20%), so a normal ECG does not exclude ACS; serial ECGs every 30-60 min are recommended early in suspected ACS.
Physical examination findings may be entirely normal in unstable ischemic heart disease, so an unremarkable exam is not reassuring.
Relief of pain by nitroglycerin is neither sensitive nor specific for ischemia (esophageal spasm also responds); relief delayed beyond ~10 min suggests either a non-ischemic cause or severe ischemia such as acute MI.
Chest wall tenderness on palpation does not exclude myocardial ischemia.
# Additional differentials
Stable angina (chronic, exertional)
Esophageal spasm (can closely mimic angina and may respond to nitroglycerin)
Gastroesophageal reflux / peptic ulcer disease
Costochondritis / chest wall (musculoskeletal) syndromes
Cervical radiculitis / disk disease
Panic disorder and related psychiatric conditions (up to ~10% of ED chest pain)
# Non infectious mimics
Acute pericarditis (pleuritic pain, relieved by sitting forward, friction rub, diffuse ST elevation with PR depression)
Acute myocarditis (non-ischemic myocardial injury; troponin may rise but should not be labeled MI)
Takotsubo (stress) cardiomyopathy (abrupt chest pain/dyspnea after emotional or physical stress; may show ST elevation and elevated biomarkers mimicking MI)
Acute aortic syndrome / aortic dissection (sudden tearing or knifelike pain, may radiate to back)
Pulmonary embolism (massive PE can cause substernal pain mimicking MI)
Pulmonary hypertension (anginal-quality chest pain)
Tension pneumothorax
Esophageal rupture (Boerhaave) / Mallory-Weiss
# Additional investigations
High-sensitivity cardiac troponin at presentation, repeated at 1-3 h (1-2 h rapid rule-out possible with hs-assays); conventional troponin repeated at 3-6 h
Right-sided ECG leads when ischemia is suspected but standard 12-lead is nondiagnostic
Chest radiograph (often normal in ACS; may show pulmonary edema; helps exclude pneumothorax, wide mediastinum)
Echocardiography for regional wall-motion abnormality, mechanical complications, or tamponade when diagnosis is uncertain or there is hemodynamic instability
Coronary CT angiography for low-intermediate probability (high negative predictive value; also excludes dissection, effusion, PE)
Stress nuclear perfusion imaging or stress echocardiography for intermediate-risk patients eligible for further testing
Cardiac MRI to characterize troponin elevation without definite CAD and to discriminate ischemic from non-ischemic injury
B-type natriuretic peptide and D-dimer as adjuncts (for heart failure and PE exclusion respectively)
# Red flags
ST-segment elevation on ECG (candidate for immediate reperfusion)
ST-segment depression or symmetric T-wave inversions >=0.2 mV (higher risk of death or recurrent ischemia)
Hypotension and significant tachycardia (suggest cardiogenic shock or other severe cause)
New murmur of mitral regurgitation or ventricular septal defect (mechanical complication of STEMI)
Pulmonary edema / S3 / rales (high-risk LV dysfunction)
Pain prolonged >=30 min and not relieved by rest (suggests MI rather than stable angina)
Syncope or presyncope with chest pain (consider ischemic arrhythmia, also PE and dissection)
# Pitfalls
Relying on a normal resting ECG or unremarkable physical exam to exclude ACS - both can be normal in unstable ischemic heart disease.
Treating nitroglycerin response as diagnostic - esophageal spasm also responds and ischemic relief is non-specific.
Attributing pain to the chest wall because palpation reproduces it - tenderness does not exclude ischemia.
Some patients with ischemia deny 'pain' and report only dyspnea or vague anxiety; women and the elderly may present with anginal equivalents (dyspnea, diaphoresis, nausea, fatigue, faintness).
Inferior MI may present with nausea/vomiting (vagal / Bezold-Jarisch reflex), simulating a GI disorder.
High-sensitivity troponin detects injury in many non-ACS cardiopulmonary conditions; without a dynamic rise/fall and clinical correlation, a single elevated value can be misinterpreted as MI - chronic elevation occurs with structural heart disease, end-stage renal disease, or interfering antibodies.
Non-ischemic injury (e.g., myocarditis) can raise troponin but should not be labeled MI.
Confusing diagnostic decision-aids (HEART, EDACS) with prognostic risk scores (TIMI, GRACE), which are intended for patients with already-established ACS, not for diagnosis.
# Acute coronary syndrome: treatment
Ischaemic chest pain ± ECG changes and troponin rise. Distinguish STEMI from NSTE-ACS by ECG.
CCU / cath lab.
Dual antiplatelet + anticoagulation + reperfusion & secondary prevention
# Steps
Aspirin 300 mg + a second antiplatelet (ticagrelor/clopidogrel); analgesia, oxygen only if hypoxic.
STEMI: primary PCI (or thrombolysis if PCI unavailable) — time-critical.
NSTE-ACS: anticoagulate (e.g. fondaparinux/LMWH) and risk-stratify (GRACE) for early angiography.
Start high-intensity statin, beta-blocker and ACE-inhibitor as tolerated.
Continuous monitoring; treat arrhythmia/heart failure.
Time-critical — reperfusion + dual antiplatelet
