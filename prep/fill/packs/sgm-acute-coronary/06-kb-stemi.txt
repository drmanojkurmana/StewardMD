# ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI): reperfusion
Adults
Emergency department, non-PCI hospital, cath lab, CCU
Read the ECG within 10 minutes; primary PCI if it can be done within 120 minutes of diagnosis, otherwise fibrinolysis within 10 minutes and transfer for angiography at 2 to 24 hours (pharmacoinvasive), with aspirin, a P2Y12 inhibitor and a parenteral anticoagulant.
Aliases: STEMI; acute MI; AMI; heart attack; primary PCI; PPCI; rescue PCI; STEMI equivalent.
# Recognise and diagnose
Ischaemic chest pain (or equivalents: breathlessness, epigastric pain, sweating, syncope; common in women, the elderly and people with diabetes) with persistent ST elevation. Record and read a 12-lead ECG within 10 minutes of first medical contact.
ST elevation at the J point in 2 or more contiguous leads: at least 2.5 mm in men under 40, 2 mm in men 40 or older and 1.5 mm in women in V2 to V3, and at least 1 mm in all other leads (in the absence of LVH or LBBB).
Record V7 to V9 (posterior MI: ST depression in V1 to V3, ST elevation 0.5 mm or more in V7 to V9) and V3R to V4R in inferior MI to look for right ventricular involvement.
Manage with an immediate reperfusion strategy: bundle branch block or paced rhythm with ongoing ischaemic symptoms, and suspected ongoing ischaemia with haemodynamic instability, life-threatening arrhythmia or resuscitated cardiac arrest.
Do not wait for troponin. Before fibrinolysis, actively exclude aortic dissection (tearing back pain, pulse or BP differential between arms, new aortic regurgitation, widened mediastinum).
# First minutes: choose the reperfusion strategy
Cardiac monitor with defibrillator at hand, IV access. Oxygen only if SpO2 is below 90%. Titrated IV opioid for severe pain (opioids delay absorption of oral P2Y12 inhibitors).
Aspirin 150 to 300 mg chewed (non-enteric-coated) at once unless true allergy.
If the expected time from STEMI diagnosis to PCI-mediated reperfusion (wire crossing) is 120 minutes or less: primary PCI. If longer, and symptom onset was within 12 hours: fibrinolysis within 10 minutes of diagnosis, provided there is no contraindication.
Primary PCI targets: diagnosis to wire crossing within 60 minutes if the patient is already in a PCI centre and within 90 minutes if transferred. Take the patient straight to the cath lab, bypassing the emergency department where possible.
Onset over 12 hours: primary PCI if symptoms continue, or haemodynamic instability or life-threatening arrhythmia. Routine PCI should be considered at 12 to 48 hours; do not routinely open an occluded infarct artery after 48 hours in a stable, pain-free patient.
Nitrates (sublingual or IV) for ongoing ischaemic pain unless hypotension, RV infarction or recent PDE5 inhibitor use.
# Primary PCI: antithrombotics and technique
P2Y12 inhibitor: prasugrel (if no prior stroke or TIA) or ticagrelor; clopidogrel 600 mg only when these are unavailable or contraindicated. Pretreatment before angiography may be considered in STEMI going to primary PCI.
Anticoagulant at PCI: UFH 70 to 100 IU/kg IV bolus (50 to 70 IU/kg if a GP IIb/IIIa inhibitor is used), ACT-guided. Enoxaparin 0.5 mg/kg IV or bivalirudin are alternatives. Do not use fondaparinux for primary PCI.
Radial access and drug-eluting stents are standard. GP IIb/IIIa inhibitors only for bailout (no-reflow or thrombotic complication).
Multivessel disease without shock: complete revascularisation during the index PCI or within 45 days. In cardiogenic shock, treat the culprit lesion only at the index procedure.
# Fibrinolysis and the pharmacoinvasive strategy
Absolute contraindications: prior intracranial haemorrhage or stroke of unknown origin, ischaemic stroke in the last 6 months, CNS neoplasm or AV malformation, major trauma, surgery or head injury in the last month, GI bleeding in the last month, known bleeding disorder, aortic dissection, non-compressible puncture in the last 24 hours (liver biopsy, lumbar puncture).
Relative contraindications: TIA in the last 6 months, oral anticoagulant therapy, pregnancy or within 1 week postpartum, refractory hypertension (SBP above 180 or DBP above 110 mmHg), advanced liver disease, infective endocarditis, active peptic ulcer, prolonged or traumatic CPR.
Prefer a fibrin-specific agent: tenecteplase (single weight-banded bolus: under 60 kg 30 mg, 60 to under 70 kg 35 mg, 70 to under 80 kg 40 mg, 80 to under 90 kg 45 mg, 90 kg or more 50 mg; half dose at age 75 or older), reteplase or alteplase. Streptokinase is acceptable where it is the only agent available; never give it a second time.
The STEMI tenecteplase dose is different from the stroke dose (0.25 mg/kg, max 25 mg). Never interchange them; check the vial strength and the indication before drawing up.
Co-therapy: aspirin, clopidogrel 300 mg loading dose if under 75 years; at 75 years or older, 75 mg with no loading dose (ACC/AHA 2025; ESC 2023 says older than 75); then 75 mg daily, and an anticoagulant (enoxaparin preferred over UFH) until revascularisation or for the hospital stay up to 8 days. Do not use ticagrelor or prasugrel as the P2Y12 inhibitor with fibrinolysis.
Transfer every lysed patient to a PCI centre. Assess at 60 to 90 minutes: less than 50% ST-segment resolution, persisting pain, or haemodynamic or electrical instability means failed fibrinolysis: do immediate rescue PCI.
After successful fibrinolysis, do angiography and PCI of the infarct artery 2 to 24 hours after the bolus; emergency angiography for recurrent ischaemia, reocclusion, heart failure or shock.
# Complications and cardiogenic shock
Cardiogenic shock (SBP below 90 mmHg with hypoperfusion): immediate angiography and culprit PCI whatever the delay since symptom onset; urgent echo for ventricular septal rupture, papillary muscle rupture, free wall rupture or tamponade (need emergency surgery).
Do not routinely insert an intra-aortic balloon pump. Short-term mechanical circulatory support can be considered in selected patients in experienced centres; noradrenaline is the preferred vasopressor.
RV infarction with hypotension: avoid nitrates and diuretics, give cautious IV fluid, correct bradyarrhythmias and keep AV synchrony.
VF or pulseless VT: immediate defibrillation. Resuscitated cardiac arrest with persistent ST elevation follows the primary PCI pathway.
Advanced AV block after inferior MI is often transient: atropine, then temporary pacing if haemodynamically compromised.
# Before discharge: secondary prevention
DAPT for 12 months by default (aspirin 75 to 100 mg daily plus prasugrel or ticagrelor). High bleeding risk: consider single antiplatelet therapy after 1 month of DAPT. Add a PPI when GI bleeding risk is raised.
High-intensity statin as early as possible; LDL-C goal below 55 mg/dL (1.4 mmol/L) with at least 50% reduction from baseline. Add ezetimibe, then a PCSK9 inhibitor, if not at goal.
Echocardiography before discharge. LVEF 40% or less or heart failure: beta-blocker, ACE inhibitor (ARB if intolerant) and MRA. ACE inhibitor also for hypertension, diabetes or CKD.
Early IV beta-blocker only for patients without heart failure, SBP above 120 mmHg and no contraindication; do not give in Killip class II or more or with hypotension or bradycardia.
Cardiac rehabilitation, smoking cessation, BP and diabetes control. Do not give NSAIDs.
# Pitfalls
Delaying reperfusion for troponin, echocardiography or transfer paperwork.
Lysing aortic dissection, pericarditis or benign early repolarisation; check for dissection features before any fibrinolytic.
Full-dose tenecteplase at age 75 or older: halve the dose to reduce intracranial haemorrhage.
Mixing up tenecteplase doses: STEMI uses the weight bands (30 to 50 mg); the stroke dose (0.25 mg/kg, max 25 mg) is different.
Stopping after successful fibrinolysis: the pharmacoinvasive strategy requires angiography within 24 hours.
Missing posterior and right ventricular infarction, or giving nitrates to a hypotensive RV infarction.
