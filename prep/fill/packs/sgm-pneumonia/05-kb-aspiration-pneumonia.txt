# Aspiration Pneumonia
Aspiration Pneumonia, Respiratory.
# Pathogens
Aspiration introduces oropharyngeal flora directly into the lower respiratory tract, making anaerobes a leading consideration alongside the typical community pathogens, particularly when foul-smelling sputum or dental disease/poor oral hygiene is present.
Very likely: Oral anaerobes (Peptostreptococcus, Prevotella, Fusobacterium); Streptococcus pneumoniae.
Likely: Staphylococcus aureus; Enterobacteriaceae (if hospital-associated aspiration).
# Possible
Klebsiella pneumoniae (classically associated with aspiration in alcoholics)

# Pathophysiology
Macroaspiration of oropharyngeal or gastric contents delivers a high microbial burden directly into the lower airways; lung involvement reflects this rather than invasion of a sterile space, since the lungs normally harbor a microbiota. Mechanical defenses (nasal hairs/turbinates, branching airways, mucociliary clearance, gag and cough reflexes) reduce but do not fully block bacterial access to the lower respiratory tract. Bacterial aspiration pneumonia develops over several days to weeks (not hours) through a combination of increased bacterial burden, increased virulence of aspirated organisms, and potential airway damage from aspirated gastric fluid. This contrasts with chemical pneumonitis (Mendelson syndrome), where aspirated acidic gastric juice causes rapid, direct destruction of the alveolar lining with fluid transudation into the alveolar space within hours.
# Clinical pearls
Aspiration pneumonia is best viewed as a point on a continuum that also includes community-acquired and hospital-acquired pneumonia, defined by a phenotype with macroaspiration risk factors and involvement of characteristic gravity-dependent lung locations.
Aspiration pneumonia is estimated to account for roughly 5-15% of community-acquired pneumonia cases; reliable figures for the hospital-acquired setting are not available.
The historical view that aspiration pneumonia is caused mainly by anaerobes has shifted: when acquired in the community or hospital, the likely pathogens mirror those of CAP or HAP, with anaerobes still relevant mainly in poor dentition, lung abscess, necrotizing pneumonia, or empyema.
Anaerobic aspiration infections tend to follow an indolent course, a clinical clue that helps separate them from more acutely presenting conditions such as chemical pneumonitis or pneumococcal pneumonia.
Sputum in bacterial aspiration pneumonia is characteristically NOT malodorous unless the process has been ongoing for at least about a week.
Expectorated sputum is unreliable for anaerobic culture because of inevitable contamination by normal oral microbiota; reliable anaerobic specimens historically required transtracheal or transthoracic aspiration, techniques rarely used now.
In CAP overall, no specific etiology is identified in more than half of cases despite careful history, examination, and imaging.
# Additional differentials
Foreign-body/food aspiration causing major-airway obstruction with atelectasis and nonspecific inflammation, managed by removal rather than antibiotics
