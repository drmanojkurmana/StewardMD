Blunt mechanisms cause over 85 to 90 percent of civilian renal trauma, led by motor vehicle collisions and falls, with rapid deceleration shearing the pedicle and direct blows crushing parenchyma against ribs. Children with less fat are more vulnerable, and flank bruising, lower rib fractures, or haematuria should raise suspicion.

AAST Grade I is contusion or subcapsular haematoma without tear, and Grade II adds a non expanding perirenal haematoma or cortical tear under 1 cm with an intact collecting system; both heal with bed rest and haematocrit checks. Grade III is a cortical tear deeper than 1 cm without urinary extravasation, tamponaded within Gerota fascia and usually observed. Grade IV tears the collecting system so urine extravasates from pelvis or calyx, with contained segmental vascular injury; stable patients heal with ureteral stent plus Foley diversion, and contained arterial bleeding is stopped by selective angioembolization. Grade V is a shattered kidney, hilar avulsion of the pedicle, or main vessel thrombosis killing perfusion, presenting unstable and demanding emergency exploration with likely nephrectomy.

Stable patients are imaged with multiphasic contrast CT: arterial maps bleeders, nephrographic defines tears, and delayed excretory images at 10 to 15 minutes catch leaks that declare the grade. Exploration is absolute for persistent haemodynamic instability despite resuscitation, expanding or pulsatile retroperitoneal haematoma, and Grade V avulsion. Before Gerota fascia is opened, the main renal artery and vein are clamped at the aorta and IVC, because early vascular control keeps the field calm and cuts nephrectomy rates.

Penetrating wounds are explored when the hilum is involved or instability persists, while stable stab injuries without hilar damage may be watched like blunt trauma. Ureteral stenting with Foley drainage heals most urinary leaks within weeks, and a repeat delayed film confirms closure before tubes are removed. Long term follow up watches blood pressure and renal function, because injured kidneys carry higher hypertension risk.
