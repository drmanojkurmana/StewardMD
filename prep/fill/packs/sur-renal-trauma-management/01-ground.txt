Renal trauma in civilian practice is overwhelmingly blunt, with over 85 to 90 percent caused by motor vehicle collisions and falls rather than penetrating wounds. The kidney's retroperitoneal position cushions it, so injury usually means significant force transfer, and associated liver, spleen, and rib injuries are common. Bedside extended focused assessment with sonography in trauma sorts unstable patients for the operating theatre while stable patients travel to computed tomography.

Grade I injuries are contusions and subcapsular hematomas without any laceration of the parenchyma. The capsule stays intact, no urine leaks, and the kidney heals with bed rest and observation alone. Grade II steps up to a non-expanding perirenal hematoma or a shallow cortical laceration under 1 cm deep, still without any tear into the collecting system. Both grades are managed conservatively in the stable patient, with haemoglobin monitoring and repeat imaging only if the clinical course changes.

Grade III crosses the 1 cm depth threshold with a cortical laceration deeper than 1 cm, yet the collecting system remains intact and no urine extravasates. Most Grade III injuries still settle without an operation provided the patient stays hemodynamically stable and the hematoma does not expand. The boundary matters because depth predicts bleeding risk, and crossing it moves the patient into high-grade territory where angioembolisation enters the plan for persistent contrast blush.

Grade IV reaches the collecting system, so urine extravasates alongside parenchymal tearing, or segmental vessels are injured with active bleeding contained by Gerota fascia. Urinary extravasation alone often resolves with a ureteral stent and drainage, while arterial bleeding that continues despite resuscitation calls for selective renal angioembolisation. The fascia's tamponade effect is what keeps many Grade IV injuries out of the operating theatre.

Grade V destroys or devascularises the organ: a completely shattered kidney, avulsion of the renal hilum, or thrombosis of the main renal artery or vein. Hilar avulsion bleeds catastrophically into the retroperitoneum and is one of the few renal injuries that forces immediate exploration. Main vessel thrombosis leaves a non-perfused kidney that will atrophy, and delayed hypertension from the ischaemic organ may later force nephrectomy.

Imaging choice follows stability. The hemodynamically stable patient goes to multiphasic contrast-enhanced CT with arterial, nephrographic, and delayed excretory phases taken at 10 to 15 minutes. The delayed phase is non-negotiable because only it shows urinary extravasation from collecting system tears, separating Grade III from Grade IV. Contrast blush marks active arterial bleeding and directs the interventional radiologist to the bleeding branch for embolisation.

Exploration has absolute indications and they are all about uncontrolled bleeding. Persistent hemodynamic instability despite resuscitation, an expanding or pulsatile retroperitoneal hematoma, and Grade V hilar avulsion mandate opening the abdomen. Stable hematomas, even large ones, are left undisturbed because opening Gerota fascia releases the tamponade and converts a contained injury into an operative nephrectomy.

When exploration is unavoidable, vascular control comes first. The surgeon secures the main renal artery and vein at the aorta and inferior vena cava before opening Gerota fascia, which cuts nephrectomy rates dramatically. With inflow controlled, the kidney can be inspected calmly, bleeding segmental branches ligated, collecting system tears repaired over drainage, and salvage attempted before accepting nephrectomy.
