# Primary Immunodeficiency
Primary Immunodeficiency, Immunology.
Aliases: PID; common variable immunodeficiency; CVID.
# Pathophysiology
PIDs are predominantly Mendelian genetic diseases that arise whenever the expression or function of a nonredundant effector or mediator of the immune system is genetically impaired. More than 550 conditions have been described, with deleterious mutations identified in roughly 500 genes; estimated prevalence is 5-10 per 100,000 (probably an underestimate). They are increasingly grouped within the broader category of inborn errors of immunity that also includes autoinflammatory syndromes. Defects can involve any aspect of innate or adaptive immunity, cell differentiation, effector function, or immune regulation. The clinical consequence is multiple levels of vulnerability to infection, ranging from extremely broad (as in SCID, where T-cell development is blocked) to narrowly restricted to a single class of organism (as in Mendelian susceptibility to mycobacterial disease, an IL-12-IFN-gamma axis defect). Antibody (B-cell) defects (agammaglobulinaemia from BTK and pre-B-cell-receptor gene defects, hyper-IgM from class-switch-recombination defects such as CD40L/CD40, IgA deficiency, and CVID) impair production or class switching of immunoglobulins, leaving patients unable to opsonise and neutralise pyogenic encapsulated bacteria. Cellular (T-cell) defects impair help for antibody production, macrophage activation, and cytotoxicity, causing susceptibility to viral, fungal (Candida), and opportunistic infections (e.g., Pneumocystis jirovecii, Cryptosporidium). Combined defects (SCID) abolish both arms. Innate defects affect phagocyte number (severe congenital neutropenia), adhesion (LAD), or microbial killing (chronic granulomatous disease, from defective NADPH-oxidase reactive-oxygen-species production), as well as complement components and innate receptor/signalling pathways (TLR adaptors IRAK4/MYD88). Beyond infection, PIDs can drive immunopathology including allergy, lymphoproliferation, autoimmunity, and an increased risk of malignancy (notably lymphoma).
# Prognosis
Prognosis varies widely with the molecule and arm of immunity affected. T-cell and combined defects generally carry a poor prognosis and require early diagnosis and intervention; SCID is fatal in infancy without treatment, but allogeneic HSCT offers a high curative potential in patients in otherwise reasonable condition, and gene therapy has succeeded in X-linked (gamma-c) SCID, ADA-SCID, and Artemis deficiency. Functional T-cell defects carry a worse HSCT prognosis than SCID because many patients are already chronically infected at diagnosis. For antibody deficiencies, lifelong immunoglobulin replacement combined with management of chronic lung disease reduces respiratory infections and prevents progression to bronchiectasis and cor pulmonale; in CVID, lymphoproliferation, granulomatous disease, autoimmunity, and lymphoma define long-term prognosis. DNA-repair disorders such as ataxia-telangiectasia are progressive and carry a very high risk of lymphoma, leukaemia, and (in adulthood) carcinoma. Newborn TREC screening, by enabling treatment of uninfected SCID patients, maximises the chance of cure. Early diagnosis with referral to experienced centres is essential to optimal outcome.
# Severity classification
PIDs are classified along two axes: (1) the arm of the immune system that is defective and (2) the mechanism of the defect (when known). The two main divisions are deficiencies of the innate immune system (phagocytic cells - impaired production [severe congenital neutropenia], asplenia, impaired adhesion [LAD], impaired killing [CGD]; innate-immunity receptors/signal transduction [TLR-signalling defects, MSMD]; complement deficiencies of the classical/alternative/lectin pathways and the lytic phase) and deficiencies of the adaptive immune system (T lymphocytes - SCID, DiGeorge, combined immunodeficiencies, hyper-IgE, DOCK8, CD40L, Wiskott-Aldrich, ataxia-telangiectasia and DNA-repair defects; B lymphocytes - X-linked/AR agammaglobulinaemia, hyper-IgM, CVID, IgA deficiency), together with regulatory defects (autoinflammatory syndromes, severe colitis, HLH, ALPS, autoimmunity such as IPEX and APECED). The classification is acknowledged to involve some arbitrary decisions because of overlap and incomplete data.
# Clinical pearls
The most frequent symptom prompting the diagnosis of a PID is recurrent or unusually severe infection; recurrent allergic or autoimmune manifestations may also be the alerting feature.
PIDs are classified by (1) the arm of the immune system that is defective and (2) the mechanism of the defect; the major categories are antibody/B-cell defects (agammaglobulinaemia, CVID, hyper-IgM, IgA deficiency), T-cell/cellular defects, combined defects (SCID), and innate-immune defects of phagocytes, complement, or innate receptors/signalling.
The site of infection and the causal microorganism point to the affected compartment: respiratory-tract (sinus, bronchial) infection mostly suggests defective antibody responses; invasive bacterial infection can result from complement deficiency, innate-signalling defects, asplenia, or antibody defects; viral, recurrent Candida, and opportunistic infections suggest impaired T-cell immunity; skin infections and deep abscesses primarily reflect innate (phagocyte) defects such as chronic granulomatous disease.
B-lymphocyte (antibody) deficiencies are the most frequent PIDs, accounting for 60-70% of all cases; T-cell deficiencies account for ~20% and innate-immune defects for ~10%.
Encapsulated/pyogenic bacteria (S. pneumoniae, H. influenzae, M. catarrhalis) and recurrent sinopulmonary infection are the hallmark of defective antibody production; untreated recurrent bronchial infection leads to bronchiectasis, cor pulmonale, and death.
Invasive Neisseria infection points to deficiencies of the complement lytic phase (C5-C9) or the alternative pathway (factors D, properdin); classic-pathway and lytic-phase complement deficiencies may also predispose to autoimmune disease, notably SLE.
