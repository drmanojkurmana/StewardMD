# Mitral Stenosis
Mitral Stenosis, Cardiology.
Aliases: MS.
# Pathophysiology
In normal adults the mitral valve orifice area is 4-6 cm2. Significant obstruction occurs when the orifice is reduced to less than about 2 cm2, so blood can only flow from the left atrium to the left ventricle if propelled by an abnormally elevated left atrioventricular pressure gradient, the hemodynamic hallmark of MS. When the orifice is below 1.5 cm2 (severe MS), an LA pressure of about 25 mmHg is required to maintain normal cardiac output. Elevated pulmonary venous and PA wedge pressures reduce pulmonary compliance and cause exertional dyspnea. Because tachycardia shortens diastole proportionately more than systole, an increased heart rate (including rapid AF) augments the transvalvular gradient and further elevates LA pressure. Chronic LA pressure elevation leads to LA enlargement, predisposing to AF and to thrombus formation (particularly in the LA appendage) with risk of systemic embolization. Pulmonary hypertension develops from passive backward transmission of elevated LA pressure, reactive pulmonary arteriolar constriction (the so-called second stenosis), interstitial edema of small pulmonary vessels, and, at end stage, obliterative pulmonary vascular changes; severe pulmonary hypertension produces RV enlargement, secondary tricuspid and pulmonic regurgitation, and right-sided heart failure.
# Prognosis
In temperate climates the latent period between the initial attack of rheumatic carditis and symptomatic MS is generally about two decades, with disability typically beginning in the fourth decade. Historical studies before surgical valvotomy showed that once a patient became seriously symptomatic, the disease progressed inexorably to death within 2-5 years. Onset of persistent AF generally marks acceleration of symptom progression.
# Severity classification
Severity is graded by mitral valve area. The normal orifice is 4-6 cm2; significant obstruction begins below about 2 cm2. A valve area below 1.5 cm2 is termed severe MS (with an LA pressure of about 25 mmHg required to maintain normal cardiac output), and an area below 1 cm2 is very severe, at which cardiac output is subnormal at rest. Echocardiographic measurement of mitral orifice area and transvalvular gradient is the basis for grading; commissurotomy is generally indicated for symptomatic patients with valve area below about 1 cm2/m2 body surface area or below 1.5 cm2 in normal-sized adults.
# Clinical pearls
Rheumatic fever is the leading cause of mitral stenosis; pure or predominant MS occurs in roughly 40% of patients with rheumatic heart disease and a history of rheumatic fever.
Although declining in temperate, middle- to high-income climates, MS remains a major problem in low-income countries, especially sub-Saharan Africa, India, Southeast Asia, and Oceania.
Auscultation classically reveals an accentuated first heart sound (S1), an opening snap (OS) of the mitral valve heard best in expiration at or just medial to the apex, followed by a low-pitched rumbling diastolic murmur heard best at the apex in the left lateral recumbent position.
The A2-to-opening-snap interval varies inversely with severity of MS: a shorter interval indicates more severe stenosis.
In patients in sinus rhythm, the diastolic murmur often reappears or becomes louder during atrial systole (presystolic accentuation).
Development of persistent atrial fibrillation often marks a turning point in the patient's course and is associated with acceleration of symptom progression.
Echocardiography (TTE with Doppler) provides mitral orifice area, transvalvular gradients, leaflet calcification/restriction, and suitability for percutaneous mitral balloon commissurotomy; TEE is especially indicated to exclude LA thrombus before intervention.
Mitral valve area 1-1.5 cm2 denotes severe MS; an LA pressure of about 25 mmHg is needed to maintain normal cardiac output when the orifice falls below 1.5 cm2.
# Additional differentials
Mitral regurgitation (may have an apical diastolic flow murmur, but it begins slightly later, S1 is soft or absent, and there is no opening snap; LV enlargement is common)
Severe aortic regurgitation with an Austin Flint murmur (apical mid-diastolic murmur not intensified in presystole and softened by amyl nitrite/arterial vasodilators)
Tricuspid stenosis (rare without MS; its diastolic murmur increases with inspiration and the jugular venous y descent is delayed)
Atrial septal defect (RV enlargement and increased pulmonary vascularity, but no LA enlargement or Kerley B lines, with fixed splitting of S2)
Left atrial myxoma (obstructs LA emptying causing a positional diastolic murmur; often with systemic features such as weight loss, fever, anemia, emboli, elevated IgG/IL-6)
# Infection mimics
Infective endocarditis with large vegetations is itself a recognized (uncommon) cause of left ventricular inflow obstruction mimicking MS
# Non infectious mimics
Congenital mitral valve stenosis (parachute valve, cor triatriatum)
Severe mitral annular calcification with extension onto the leaflets
Systemic lupus erythematosus and rheumatoid arthritis involving the valve
Left atrial myxoma
# Additional investigations
Transthoracic echocardiography with color flow and spectral Doppler for mitral inflow velocities, peak/mean gradients, mitral orifice area, leaflet calcification and restriction, and suitability for percutaneous mitral balloon commissurotomy
Transesophageal echocardiography when TTE is inadequate, and specifically to exclude left atrial thrombus before commissurotomy
Exercise (stress) echocardiography to assess mean mitral diastolic gradient, PA pressures, and RV function when clinical and resting hemodynamic findings are discrepant
ECG showing left atrial enlargement (P-wave changes), and right axis deviation/RV hypertrophy when severe pulmonary hypertension supervenes
Chest X-ray showing straightening of the left cardiac border, prominent main PAs, upper-lobe pulmonary venous dilation, an enlarged LA, and Kerley B lines when mean LA pressure exceeds about 20 mmHg
Left and right heart catheterization when clinical and noninvasive findings are discrepant; CT coronary angiography to screen for coronary disease before valve surgery
# Red flags
Atrial fibrillation, which accelerates symptom progression and markedly increases thromboembolic risk
Systemic embolization (incidence 10-20%), more frequent with AF, age over 65, and reduced cardiac output; may be the presenting feature even in mild MS
Hemoptysis from rupture of pulmonary-bronchial venous connections due to pulmonary venous hypertension
Pulmonary hypertension and right-sided heart failure (hepatomegaly, ankle edema, ascites, pleural effusion)
Recurrent pulmonary emboli, sometimes with infarction, an important late cause of morbidity and mortality
Decompensation precipitated by tachycardia, fever, severe anemia, paroxysmal AF, thyrotoxicosis, exertion, or pregnancy
In pregnancy, increased blood volume, cardiac output, and tachycardia can raise the transmitral gradient and cause pulmonary edema or tachyarrhythmias, with the immediate postpartum period a time of particular concern due to rapid volume shifts
# Pitfalls
When cardiac output is markedly reduced, the typical auscultatory findings including the diastolic rumble may be undetectable (silent MS) and may reappear only as compensation is restored
Systemic embolization may be the presenting feature in otherwise asymptomatic patients with only mild MS
The Graham Steell murmur of pulmonic regurgitation can be indistinguishable from aortic regurgitation, though it may increase with inspiration and is accompanied by a loud, often palpable P2
A history of rheumatic carditis is increasingly rare and often cannot be elicited
