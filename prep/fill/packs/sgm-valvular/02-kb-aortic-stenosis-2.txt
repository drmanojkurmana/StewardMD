# Aortic stenosis (syncope)
Aortic stenosis (syncope), Cardiology.
Red flags: Exertional syncope is a red flag.
# Investigations
Echocardiogram
ECG
Examine for ejection systolic murmur
# Pathophysiology
Aortic stenosis is most often caused by gradual progression of valvular calcification of either normal trileaflet or congenitally bicuspid valves, producing fixed left ventricular outflow obstruction. The pressure overload drives left ventricular hypertrophy, which manifests on the ECG as LVH that increases with severity. The combination of increased myocardial oxygen demand and impaired endocardial perfusion can cause ischemia/angina even with normal coronaries. Syncope in AS reflects a structural disorder compromising cardiac output, and inappropriate reflex vasodilation may be provoked by increased ventricular contractility against the fixed obstruction.
# Prognosis
Patients with good left ventricular function can tolerate severe AS for a considerable period, so valve area or gradient alone should not dictate timing of surgery, which remains a clinical decision.
# Severity classification
Echo: peak instantaneous velocities of 3.0 m/s or higher (often >4.0 m/s) correspond to gradients of ~36 and ~64 mmHg and indicate at least moderate AS; aortic valve area <1.0 cm2 is generally severe and <0.6 cm2 critical. Catheterization: a valve area <1.0 cm2 with a mean gradient >40 mmHg indicates severe AS. Aortic sclerosis (non-obstructive) is defined by a Doppler jet velocity of 2.5 m/s or less.
# Clinical pearls
The murmur of AS is a crescendo-decrescendo (midsystolic/ejection) murmur, usually loudest in the second right intercostal space and radiating into the carotids; it is the most common midsystolic murmur in adults.
Apical transmission of the AS murmur, where it becomes higher-pitched, is common (Gallavardin effect) and may be mistaken for mitral regurgitation; the AS murmur intensifies in the beat following a premature beat and varies directly with cardiac output, whereas the MR murmur stays constant beat-to-beat.
Auscultatory features pointing to severe AS include a soft or absent A2, paradoxical splitting of S2, a late-peaking systolic murmur, an apical S4, and (at normal output) a systolic thrill and a grade 4 or louder murmur.
An early ejection sound (click) in children/young adults signifies a flexible, noncalcified bicuspid valve and localizes obstruction to the valvular level rather than sub- or supravalvular.
Bicuspid aortic valve is one of the most common congenital anomalies and predisposes to both aortic stenosis and aortic regurgitation.
AS can precipitate myocardial ischemia and anginal chest pain even without obstructive coronary atherosclerosis, by creating extreme myocardial oxygen demand with impaired endocardial blood flow (type 2 ischemia mechanism).
Heyde's syndrome is the association of aortic stenosis with bleeding gastrointestinal vascular ectasias (acquired von Willebrand disease); such patients appear to benefit from aortic valve replacement.
# Additional differentials
Aortic sclerosis (focal leaflet thickening/calcification without obstruction; Doppler jet velocity 2.5 m/s or less) can produce a grade 2-3 midsystolic murmur acoustically identical to AS.
Hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy (subvalvular dynamic outflow obstruction).
Subaortic membrane (fixed subvalvular obstruction that can mimic valvular AS).
Supravalvular aortic stenosis (exceedingly rare).
Pulmonic stenosis (midsystolic murmur, second left interspace).
Atrial septal defect with increased pulmonic flow producing a midsystolic murmur with fixed splitting of S2.
Mitral regurgitation, given the apical Gallavardin transmission of the AS murmur.
# Non infectious mimics
Hyperdynamic flow murmurs without structural disease (fever, thyrotoxicosis, pregnancy, anemia, normal childhood/adolescence) can produce midsystolic murmurs.
# Additional investigations
Transthoracic echocardiography to assess valve anatomy (tricuspid/bicuspid, leaflet calcification, excursion), stenosis severity, LV size/wall thickness/function, and aortic root and proximal ascending aorta.
Doppler estimation of transvalvular peak velocity and pressure gradient.
Continuity-equation estimation of aortic valve area, the most accurate echo technique, especially when gradients are underestimated by LV dysfunction.
Dobutamine stress echocardiography to evaluate low-flow/low-gradient AS and determine whether the valve can open further.
Cardiac MRI for quantitative assessment of severity.
Carotid pulse examination for parvus et tardus contour.
# Red flags
A weak and delayed carotid pulse (pulsus parvus et tardus) indicates severe AS.
Soft or absent A2, paradoxical splitting of S2, late-peaking murmur, and an apical S4 indicate severe AS.
A softening AS murmur in the setting of heart failure/low cardiac output is misleading and does not indicate mild disease.
Syncope (including reflex/exertional) from AS reflects structural compromise of cardiac output.
# Pitfalls
Avoid aggressive preload/afterload reduction: aggressive treatment of cardiac failure with diuretics and/or vasodilators can precipitate orthostatic hypotension in patients with structural disease such as AS.
Transvalvular pressure gradients underestimate severity in severe LV dysfunction; rely on valve area by continuity equation.
In low-flow/low-gradient AS the valve may behave stenotic simply because of the low gradient; provocative dobutamine testing is needed to distinguish true severe stenosis.
The carotid parvus-et-tardus sign is less discriminatory in older patients with stiffened arteries.
Aortic sclerosis can produce a murmur indistinguishable on auscultation from significant AS.
# Aortic stenosis (syncope): treatment
Exertional syncope/angina/dyspnoea with an ejection-systolic murmur radiating to carotids, slow-rising pulse. Echo confirms severity, Cardiology referral; admit if decompensated.
Valve replacement — no disease-modifying pharmacotherapy
# Steps
Echocardiography to grade severity; refer cardiology.
Avoid aggressive vasodilators/nitrates in severe AS (preload-dependent — risk of collapse).
Symptomatic severe AS → aortic valve replacement (surgical or TAVI).
Treat heart failure cautiously; manage concomitant CAD.
Endocarditis awareness/dental hygiene.
Cardiology referral — definitive Rx is valve replacement
