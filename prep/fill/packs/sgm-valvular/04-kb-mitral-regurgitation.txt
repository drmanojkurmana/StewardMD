# Mitral Regurgitation
Mitral Regurgitation, Cardiology.
Aliases: MR.
# Pathophysiology
MR can arise from disease of any of the five components of the mitral apparatus: leaflets, annulus, chordae tendineae, papillary muscles, and subjacent myocardium. MR reduces resistance to LV emptying (lowers afterload); the LV decompresses into the LA during ejection, so initial compensation is more complete LV emptying. Over time LV volume rises as regurgitation worsens and contractile function deteriorates, and forward cardiac output falls; LV compliance is often increased so LV diastolic pressure does not rise until late. The regurgitant volume varies directly with LV systolic pressure and the size of the regurgitant orifice (influenced by LV and annular dilation and leaflet morphology). LA compliance governs the clinical picture: in acute severe MR the regurgitant volume enters a normal-sized, noncompliant LA so LA and pulmonary venous pressures rise sharply with a prominent v wave and pulmonary edema is common; in chronic severe MR the LA enlarges and becomes more compliant, buffering pressure so pulmonary congestion is initially less prominent and fatigue from low forward output predominates. Chronic secondary MR results from annular dilation, ventricular remodeling, papillary muscle displacement, and leaflet tethering (e.g., after MI, in dilated cardiomyopathy once LV end-diastolic dimension reaches ~6 cm, or atrial functional MR from chronic AF with annular dilation).
# Prognosis
Chronic mild-to-moderate isolated MR is well tolerated and usually asymptomatic. Chronic severe MR tends to be progressive ('MR begets MR') and, when secondary, severity correlates inversely with prognosis, with adverse outcome tied to the degree of LV remodeling and myocardial dysfunction. With surgery, repair carries substantially lower risk than replacement; a 2023 STS database analysis reported mean perioperative mortality of about 1.16% (under 0.5% in patients younger than 65) for repair of primary MR, durable repair is feasible in up to 95% of degenerative cases at high-volume centers, and reoperation runs about 1% per year over the first 10 years. In ischemic MR with severely impaired LV function (EF below 30%), surgical risk is higher, recovery of LV performance is incomplete, and long-term survival is reduced.
# Severity classification
Chronic severe MR (Doppler/CMR quantitative criteria) is defined by a regurgitant volume of at least 60 mL/beat, regurgitant fraction of at least 50%, and effective regurgitant orifice area of at least 0.40 cm2. ACC/AHA disease staging (Stages B, C1, C2, D) is applied: Stage C is asymptomatic severe MR (C1 normal LV systolic function with LVEF above 60% and ESD below 40 mm; C2 LV systolic dysfunction with LVEF at or below 60% or ESD at or above 40 mm) and Stage D is symptomatic severe MR.
# Clinical pearls
Distinguish primary (degenerative) MR, where the leaflets and/or chordae are themselves abnormal, from secondary (functional) MR, where leaflets and chordae are usually normal and regurgitation is driven by LV/LA remodeling, annular dilation, papillary muscle displacement, dyssynchrony, or leaflet tethering; assessment, treatment, and prognosis differ significantly between the two.
The characteristic murmur of chronic severe MR is a holosystolic, plateau-configuration systolic murmur of at least grade III/VI, most prominent at the apex and radiating to the axilla.
Murmur radiation reflects jet direction: with posterior leaflet prolapse/flail the eccentric anteriorly-directed jet strikes the LA wall near the aortic root and the murmur radiates to the base (can mimic aortic stenosis), whereas anterior leaflet prolapse/flail directs the jet and murmur toward the axilla.
Ruptured chordae tendineae may give the systolic murmur a cooing or 'seagull' quality; a flail leaflet may produce a musical murmur.
The murmur of chronic MR not due to MVP is intensified by isometric handgrip and reduced during the strain phase of Valsalva (decreased LV preload).
In acute severe MR the murmur is early in timing and decrescendo, ending well before S2, reflecting the rapidly rising LA pressure and diminishing LV-LA gradient — in contrast to the holosystolic plateau murmur of chronic MR.
Because EF rises in severe MR when LV function is normal, even a modest fall in EF (below 60%) reflects significant contractile dysfunction.
Atrial fibrillation is almost invariably present once the LA dilates significantly in chronic severe MR; LA enlargement and AF support the diagnosis on ECG.
'MR begets MR': LA enlargement tensions the posterior leaflet pulling it away from the orifice, and LV dilation increases regurgitation, enlarging LA and LV further in a vicious cycle.
With post-MI papillary muscle rupture the posteromedial papillary muscle is involved far more often than the anterolateral muscle because of its single blood supply.
# Additional differentials
Aortic stenosis (basally-radiating MR murmur from posterior leaflet prolapse/flail can be confused with AS)
Mitral stenosis (a brief early-diastolic LA-LV gradient with rapid filling sound and mid-diastolic rumble in pure severe MR can masquerade as MS)
Ventricular septal defect and other causes of a holosystolic murmur
# Additional investigations
Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) with Doppler to define mechanism and severity (jet width/area, regurgitant volume, regurgitant fraction, effective regurgitant orifice area, LV volumes/EF, LA/LV size, chordal integrity, annular calcification)
Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) for greater anatomic detail
Estimation of pulmonary artery systolic pressure from the tricuspid regurgitation jet velocity
Exercise (stress) echocardiography to assess exercise capacity and dynamic change in MR severity, PA pressures, and biventricular function when clinical and resting findings disagree
Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) for quantitative regurgitant measures when TTE is discordant or insufficient (noted as not widely available)
ECG (LA enlargement, possible RA enlargement with pulmonary hypertension, AF, eccentric LVH)
Chest X-ray (LA/LV enlargement, pulmonary venous congestion, Kerley B lines, mitral/annular calcification; asymmetric pulmonary edema possible in acute MR)
Left and right heart catheterization with contrast ventriculography and coronary angiography when noninvasive findings are discrepant or to identify coronary disease before surgery
# Red flags
Acute severe MR is a surgical emergency: papillary muscle rupture post-MI or leaflet destruction/perforation in infective endocarditis can cause sudden pulmonary edema and hemodynamic collapse, requiring urgent stabilization and emergent surgery
Acute pulmonary edema, hypotension with narrow pulse pressure, and prominent signs of pulmonary congestion with a non-displaced apex point to acute severe MR
Onset of palpitations or atrial fibrillation in chronic severe MR
Late-onset right heart failure with hepatic congestion, ankle edema, distended neck veins, ascites, and secondary tricuspid regurgitation, indicating pulmonary vascular disease and pulmonary hypertension
Falling LVEF (to 60% or below) or rising LV end-systolic dimension (40 mm or more), even when asymptomatic, signals contractile dysfunction warranting surgical referral
# Pitfalls
In secondary MR the severity can be underappreciated by echocardiographic/Doppler techniques, and lesser degrees of regurgitation may carry relatively greater prognostic weight.
An S3 in MR may reflect rapid LV filling, but in ischemic or dilated cardiomyopathy an S3 may instead signify ventricular dysfunction.
Because EF is artifactually elevated in severe MR with normal myocardium, a 'low-normal' EF may already represent meaningful contractile dysfunction.
Routine surgical valve repair for significant secondary MR due to dilated cardiomyopathy has NOT been shown to improve long-term survival versus optimal guideline-directed medical therapy, especially in the era of quadruple HFrEF therapy.
Direct oral anticoagulants should NOT be used if moderate or severe rheumatic mitral stenosis coexists, and are NOT approved for mechanical prosthetic valves.
A presystolic murmur is not ordinarily heard with isolated MR.
# Management
For primary degenerative severe MR, mitral valve repair (preferred over replacement) is the definitive treatment, indicated once symptoms develop or with left-ventricular enlargement or dysfunction, new atrial fibrillation or pulmonary hypertension
For secondary functional MR, first optimise guideline-directed heart-failure therapy with beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors or angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitors, mineralocorticoid-receptor antagonists and SGLT2 inhibitors, adding cardiac resynchronisation where indicated
Transcatheter edge-to-edge mitral repair for selected patients with persistent secondary MR despite optimal medical therapy or who are at high surgical risk
Acute severe MR is a surgical emergency: stabilise with afterload reduction using vasodilators or intravenous nitrates and mechanical circulatory support such as an intra-aortic balloon pump as a bridge to urgent surgery
Manage atrial fibrillation with rate or rhythm control and anticoagulation for thromboembolic prevention
Serial echocardiographic surveillance of regurgitant severity, left-ventricular size and function and pulmonary pressures to time intervention
Refer to a multidisciplinary heart-valve team for mechanism assessment and intervention planning
