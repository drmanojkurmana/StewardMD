# Vasovagal / orthostatic syncope
Vasovagal / orthostatic syncope, Neurology / Cardiology.
# Red flags
Exertional or cardiac syncope needs cardiac workup
# Investigations
Lying/standing BP
ECG (exclude arrhythmia)
# Pathophysiology
Standing pools 500-1000 mL of blood in the lower extremities, buttocks, and splanchnic circulation, reducing venous return, ventricular filling, cardiac output, and blood pressure. Normally baroreceptors in the carotid sinus and aortic arch trigger a compensatory reflex that raises sympathetic outflow and lowers vagal activity, increasing peripheral resistance, venous return, and cardiac output. In neurally mediated (reflex/vasovagal) syncope this reflex transiently fails: there is a paroxysmal shift in autonomic efferent activity with increased parasympathetic outflow plus sympathoinhibition, producing bradycardia, vasodilation/loss of vasoconstrictor tone (vasodepressor response), and reduced cardiac output. The resulting fall in systemic blood pressure drops cerebral blood flow below the limits of autoregulation, causing transient global cerebral hypoperfusion and loss of consciousness. Multiple afferent triggers (orthostatic stress, carotid sinus, gastrointestinal tract, bladder) converge on the central autonomic network in the medulla. Hyperventilation-induced hypocarbia (cerebral vasoconstriction) and raised intrathoracic pressure (impaired venous return) contribute to many situational reflex syncopes. By contrast, orthostatic hypotension reflects chronic impairment of these homeostatic reflexes (autonomic failure), and cardiac syncope results from arrhythmias or structural disease reducing cardiac output.
# Prognosis
Syncope of noncardiac and unexplained origin in younger individuals carries an excellent prognosis with unaffected life expectancy. In contrast, cardiac syncope (structural heart disease or primary arrhythmia) is associated with an increased risk of sudden cardiac death and overall mortality, and mortality is also increased with orthostatic hypotension related to age and comorbidities. Hospitalization and mortality risk are higher in older adults.

# Clinical pearls
Syncope is a transient, self-limited loss of consciousness from acute global cerebral hypoperfusion, with rapid onset, brief duration, and spontaneous complete recovery; reorientation is almost immediate after a syncopal event.
Causes fall into three categories: neurally mediated (reflex/vasovagal) syncope, orthostatic hypotension, and cardiac syncope; clinical features, mechanisms, and prognosis differ markedly among them.
Neurally mediated syncope is the most common cause overall in population-based studies and accounts for the vast majority of syncope in the young (peak incidence ~age 15); incidence is higher in women, and a first-degree family history is common in young patients.
Vasovagal syncope (the common faint) is provoked by intense emotion, pain, the sight of blood, venipuncture, and/or orthostatic stress; situational reflex syncopes have specific localized triggers (cough, micturition, swallow, defecation, carotid sinus pressure).
Premonitory (prodromal) features of autonomic activation in neurally mediated syncope include diaphoresis, pallor, palpitations, nausea, lightheadedness, hyperventilation, and yawning.
A functioning autonomic nervous system is required to develop neurally mediated syncope, in contrast to syncope from autonomic failure (orthostatic hypotension).
Neurally mediated syncope can be subdivided by efferent pathway: vasodepressor (sympathetic vasoconstrictor failure), cardioinhibitory (bradycardia/asystole from vagal outflow), or mixed.
Orthostatic hypotension is defined as a fall in systolic BP of at least 20 mmHg or diastolic BP of at least 10 mmHg within 3 min of standing or head-up tilt; supine and standing BP/HR at 3 min should be measured in every initial evaluation.
Initial clinical assessment plus ECG identifies a cause in about 50% of patients and stratifies cardiac mortality risk.
A clinically meaningful syncope usually corresponds to a fall in systolic BP to roughly 50 mmHg or lower; cessation of cerebral blood flow for 6-8 s produces loss of consciousness.
# Additional differentials
Generalized and partial/partial-complex seizures (with secondary generalization, usually preceded by an aura such as an unpleasant smell, fear, anxiety, or abdominal/visceral sensation).
Autonomic seizures (autonomic epilepsy), whose cardiovascular, GI, pulmonary, urogenital, pupillary, and cutaneous manifestations mimic syncopal prodrome; accompanying non-autonomic auras help distinguish them.
Hypoglycemia, typically in insulin-treated type 1 or type 2 diabetes (tremor, palpitations, anxiety, diaphoresis, hunger, paresthesias; hunger is not a typical syncope prodrome).
