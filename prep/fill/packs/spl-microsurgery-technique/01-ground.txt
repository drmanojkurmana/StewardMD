Exam grounding notes for microvascular anastomosis, thrombosis, and free flap monitoring, written for grounding, not for display. Paraphrased from Plastic Surgery 5E Volume 1 on microvascular surgery.

Microvascular anastomoses are sewn under the operating microscope, usually with 9-0 or 10-0 nylon sutures placed as interrupted or continuous stitches. The 9-0 and 10-0 sizes suit vessels of 1 to 3 mm. Adventitia is trimmed, the lumen is irrigated, and a background strip improves visibility.

When the donor vessel is much wider than the recipient, the end-to-side option bridges a mismatch spanning 1.5 to 1 through 2 to 1, keeping downstream recipient flow intact. Spatulation and oblique cuts widen a small end before suturing.

Venous thrombosis occurs more frequently than arterial thrombosis and accounts for over 60 percent of all microvascular flap failures. Low venous pressure, thin walls, and kinking predispose the outflow. Two veins repaired for every one artery improve safety.

Flap thrombosis happens predominantly within the first 24 to 72 hours after surgery. Monitoring is therefore hourly through this window, checking colour, temperature, turgor, capillary refill, and pinprick bleeding. No news in the first 3 days is good news only when checks were actually performed.

An inflow failure leaves the flap white, cold, and empty: blanching takes longer than 3 seconds to recover, turgor is lost, and a needle prick releases only scant bright-red blood or none at all.

An outflow block makes the flap dusky blue and tense: refill returns in under 1 second and the prick wells up brisk dark blood. This congested pattern is the more common failure and needs equally urgent release.

Recognising failure fast matters: reopening the wound inside 4 to 6 hours rescues above 70 percent of compromised flaps, while delay past 6 hours drops salvage sharply. The team evacuates clot, revises the join, and adds vein grafts when needed.

Spasm is countered by dripping papaverine or lidocaine solution onto the vessels during the operation, with heparin dosed by body weight given for systemic cover. Papaverine relaxes smooth muscle, lidocaine blocks spasm, and heparin keeps the microcirculation open.
