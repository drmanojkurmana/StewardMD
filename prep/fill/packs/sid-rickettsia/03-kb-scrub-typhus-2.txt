# Pathophysiology
Scrub typhus is caused by Orientia tsutsugamushi, an obligate intracellular organism placed in a separate genus within the family Rickettsiaceae. It differs substantially from Rickettsia species both genetically and in cell-wall composition, notably lacking lipopolysaccharide. Disease results from infection of vascular endothelial cells with increased vascular permeability (a mechanism shared across the rickettsioses). The organism is maintained in nature by transovarial transmission within trombiculid mites; infected larval mites (chiggers, the only mite stage that feeds on a vertebrate host) inoculate the organism into the skin during feeding. Severe disease reflects widespread vascular injury, producing the encephalitis and interstitial pneumonia that characterize advanced cases. Closely related Orientia species (O. chuto, Candidatus O. chiloensis) cause scrub typhus-like illness in newly recognized foci.
# Prognosis
Illness ranges from mild and self-limiting to fatal. The case-fatality rate for untreated classic cases is about 6% and would likely be lower if all mild cases were captured; overall reported mortality varies widely by location (cited as 1% to 70%). Risk of death rises with increasing age and with the presence of myocarditis, delirium, pneumonitis, or signs of hemorrhage. Recognized in time, the disease responds well to appropriate antibiotic therapy.
# Severity classification
Severe scrub typhus is marked by encephalitis/meningoencephalitis and interstitial pneumonia due to vascular injury, and may progress to myocarditis, DIC, renal failure, hypotension, and multiorgan failure.
# Clinical pearls
Among returning travelers, fewer than 50% develop an eschar and fewer than 40% develop a rash; when a rash does appear it typically emerges on day 4-6 of illness.
Eschar formation occurs at the site of mite/chigger feeding and may develop during the incubation period; an eschar is a useful diagnostic clue that should prompt consideration of scrub typhus.
Onset after a 6-21 day incubation is characterized by fever, headache, myalgia, cough, and gastrointestinal symptoms; some patients recover spontaneously after a few days while others progress to severe disease.
Chigger bites may be asymptomatic, so the absence of a recalled bite does not exclude the diagnosis.
Immunity to scrub typhus is short-lived: protection against the homologous strain wanes over 1-3 years, and the organism's marked antigenic diversity means cross-protective immunity can be lost in as little as 1 month, so reinfection is possible.
# Infection mimics
Other rickettsioses presenting with eschar, rash, fever and headache (e.g., Mediterranean spotted fever, African tick-bite fever, rickettsialpox, murine and epidemic typhus).
Atypical/community-acquired pneumonia, given the cough and dyspnea of pulmonary involvement.
Other undifferentiated tropical febrile illnesses with thrombocytopenia and transaminitis.
# Additional investigations
Serology is the mainstay of laboratory diagnosis: indirect fluorescent antibody, indirect immunoperoxidase, enzyme immunoassays, and rapid lateral-flow chromatographic tests.
PCR amplification of Orientia genes is effective when performed on eschar material, but is less sensitive on blood.
Platelet count: thrombocytopenia is a characteristic laboratory finding in scrub typhus (as in other rickettsioses).
WBC count is typically normal or low.
Serum sodium: hyponatremia is a recognized associated finding across the rickettsioses.
Hepatic aminotransferases: mild to moderate elevation is commonly seen.
# Red flags
Altered mental status / encephalitis (meningoencephalitis is a feature of severe disease).
Interstitial pneumonia / pneumonitis with dyspnea or progression toward respiratory failure.
Myocarditis.
Signs of disseminated intravascular coagulation or hemorrhage.
Renal failure.
Progressive hypotension and multiorgan failure (a severe-disease pattern shared by the rickettsioses).
# Pitfalls
Relying on the classic triad: eschar, rash, and lymphadenopathy are frequently absent (eschar <50% and rash <40% of travelers; signs seldom seen in indigenous patients), so a negative skin exam does not exclude scrub typhus.
Expecting a recalled bite: chigger bites may be asymptomatic and the mites are tiny and rarely noticed.
Assuming geographic exclusion: although classically eastern/southern Asia, northern Australia, and Pacific/Indian Ocean islands, emerging and reemerging foci (e.g., Chile, parts of South/Central America, Africa, Arabian peninsula via related Orientia species) challenge the traditional epidemiology.
Note that PCR on blood is less sensitive than PCR on eschar material.
Some Thai isolates are poorly responsive to doxycycline or chloramphenicol.
# Deescalation
Not applicable — doxycycline is already the single most targeted agent. If the patient fails to defervesce within 48-72 hours, reconsider the diagnosis rather than escalating antibiotics.

# Framework
Do not withhold empiric doxycycline while awaiting serology in a clinically compatible presentation — early treatment dramatically reduces morbidity and the response itself supports the diagnosis.
Doxycycline is not contraindicated for short courses even in children when scrub typhus is suspected, per current guidance, given the risk of untreated disease — this is a common point of unnecessary hesitation.
Treat the full 7-day course even after the characteristic rapid defervescence, to ensure complete clearance.
