# Anorexia nervosa
Anorexia nervosa, Psychiatry / Mental health, Psychiatry.
Aliases: AN.
# Pathophysiology
Anorexia nervosa arises from an interaction of genetic and temperamental vulnerability (perfectionism, anxiety, harm avoidance), sociocultural pressures, and neurobiological factors including altered reward and interoceptive processing and serotonergic and dopaminergic dysregulation. Starvation itself perpetuates the disorder through neuroendocrine and cognitive changes, and complications reflect the systemic effects of chronic malnutrition.
# Prognosis
Anorexia nervosa has one of the highest mortality rates among psychiatric disorders, from both medical complications and suicide. Many patients recover, particularly with early intervention in adolescence, but a substantial proportion follow a chronic or relapsing course.
# Clinical pearls
Characterised by restriction of energy intake leading to significantly low body weight, intense fear of weight gain, and a disturbance in how body weight or shape is experienced
Two patterns are recognised: a restricting type and a binge-eating/purging type
Physical consequences include amenorrhoea, bradycardia, hypotension, hypothermia, lanugo hair, and osteopenia
Purging behaviours can cause electrolyte disturbances such as hypokalaemia, metabolic alkalosis, and dental erosion
Management principles centre on structured nutritional rehabilitation and weight restoration alongside psychotherapy, with family-based treatment favoured for adolescents
Medical stabilisation takes priority in severe cases; feeding must be advanced cautiously to avoid refeeding syndrome
Comorbid depression, anxiety, and obsessive-compulsive features are common, and suicide risk is elevated
Weigh patients and monitor electrolytes and cardiac status, since bradycardia and QT prolongation can be dangerous
# Additional differentials
Bulimia nervosa
Avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder
Major depressive disorder with appetite loss
Hyperthyroidism
Malabsorption and gastrointestinal disease
Malignancy or chronic infection causing weight loss
Body dysmorphic disorder
# Additional investigations
Weight, height, and body mass index with growth trajectory in young people
Electrolytes including potassium, phosphate, and magnesium
ECG to assess for bradycardia and QT prolongation
Full blood count, renal and liver function, and glucose
Bone density assessment in prolonged illness
Assessment for refeeding risk before and during nutritional rehabilitation
# Red flags
Severe bradycardia, hypotension, hypothermia, or arrhythmia indicating cardiac instability
Dangerous electrolyte derangement, particularly hypokalaemia and hypophosphataemia
Refeeding syndrome during nutritional restoration
Rapid or extreme weight loss and very low body mass index
Suicidal ideation
# Pitfalls
Refeeding too rapidly and precipitating life-threatening refeeding syndrome
Focusing on weight alone while neglecting electrolyte and cardiac monitoring
Underestimating suicide risk
Being reassured by a normal weight in the binge-purge type despite dangerous electrolyte disturbance
Delaying medical stabilisation while awaiting psychiatric transfer
# Management
Make structured nutritional rehabilitation and weight restoration the cornerstone of care, advancing feeding cautiously to avoid refeeding syndrome
Prevent refeeding syndrome by monitoring and replacing phosphate, potassium, and magnesium and giving thiamine before and during refeeding
