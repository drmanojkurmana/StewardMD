Exam grounding notes for carpal, cubital, and radial tunnel syndromes, written for grounding, not for display. Paraphrased from Plastic Surgery 5E Volume 6 on compression neuropathies.

The carpal tunnel holds ten structures: four tendons of FDS, four tendons of FDP, the flexor pollicis longus tendon, and the median nerve. Nine flexor tendons plus one nerve crowd a rigid osteofibrous canal, so any swelling compresses the nerve first.

Phalen testing provokes median paresthesias in the radial 3.5 digits by holding maximal wrist flexion for 60 seconds. The Durkan carpal compression test, pressing the examiner thumbs over the tunnel, is the most sensitive physical examination test for the condition.

Thenar wasting of abductor pollicis brevis with persistent sensory loss signals severe chronic disease. These patients skip prolonged splinting and move promptly to open or endoscopic transverse carpal ligament release before axons are lost for good.

The recurrent motor branch of the median nerve varies by the Lanz classification: extraligamentous in 50 percent, subligamentous in 30 percent, and transligamentous in 20 percent. Transligamentous branches crossing through the ligament are cut during blind release.

The cubital tunnel threatens the ulnar nerve at five potential anatomical sites. Compression most commonly strikes beneath the Osborne ligament, the fibrous arcade joining the two heads of FCU. Elbow flexion tightens this arcade.

Two signs confirm ulnar motor deficit. The Wartenberg sign is involuntary abduction of the small finger by the unopposed extensor digiti minimi. The Froment sign shows compensatory IP thumb flexion driven by FPL when adduction fails.

Pronator syndrome squeezes the median nerve in the proximal forearm, not the wrist. It is distinguished from carpal tunnel syndrome by sensory loss over the thenar eminence, because the palmar cutaneous branch is involved above the tunnel while CTS spares it.

Radial tunnel syndrome compresses the posterior interosseous nerve at the arcade of Frohse. Patients report deep aching forearm pain without motor weakness, which separates it from PIN palsy where finger extension fails.
