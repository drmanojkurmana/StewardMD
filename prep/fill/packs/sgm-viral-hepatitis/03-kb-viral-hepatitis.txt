# Acute Viral Hepatitis
Acute Viral Hepatitis, Tropical Fever / Hepatobiliary.
# Pathogens
In endemic settings with fecal-oral transmission risk (contaminated food/water), Hepatitis A and E are the dominant causes of acute viral hepatitis, with Hepatitis E of particular concern in pregnancy due to higher fulminant hepatic failure risk. Hepatitis B should be specifically considered with parenteral/sexual risk factors, since management and chronicity implications differ substantially.
Very likely: Hepatitis A virus; Hepatitis E virus.
# Likely
Hepatitis B virus (if relevant risk factors: injection drug use, unsafe transfusion, vertical transmission)
# Possible
Hepatitis C virus (less commonly causes acute icteric illness)
Other hepatotropic viruses (CMV, EBV) in atypical presentations
# Investigations
Liver function tests
Marked transaminase elevation (often >10x upper limit of normal) with hyperbilirubinemia is characteristic of acute viral hepatitis and helps distinguish it from other causes of jaundice.
Hepatitis A IgM, Hepatitis E IgM
Confirms the most likely causes in this setting; both are the standard first-line serologic tests for acute fecal-oral hepatitis.
Hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), IgM anti-HBc
Identifies acute Hepatitis B, which has different counseling, monitoring, and contact-tracing/vaccination implications than A or E.
Coagulation profile (PT/INR)
A rising INR is the key early warning sign of evolving acute liver failure and should be tracked closely.
Mental status assessment (serial)
Screens for hepatic encephalopathy, the clinical hallmark of progression to acute liver failure requiring urgent escalation of care.
Antibiotics NOT indicated
# Reason
Jaundice with nausea/vomiting and constitutional symptoms, particularly following exposure to potentially contaminated food or water, is consistent with acute viral hepatitis (commonly Hepatitis A or E in this setting). This is a viral infection — antibiotics have no role and management is supportive (rest, hydration, avoidance of hepatotoxic drugs and alcohol), with monitoring for the rare progression to acute liver failure.
# Pathophysiology
None of the hepatitis viruses is ordinarily directly cytopathic to hepatocytes; the clinical manifestations and outcome of acute liver injury are largely determined by the host immune (chiefly cell-mediated/cytolytic T-cell) response to infected hepatocytes. In hepatitis B the existence of inactive carriers with normal histology and the more severe disease seen with defective cellular immunity support an immune-mediated mechanism; in immunocompetent adults the degree of liver injury correlates with the level of HBV replication (HBV DNA). HBV enters hepatocytes via the sodium taurocholate cotransporting polypeptide (NTCP) receptor and replicates through reverse transcription of a pregenomic RNA, forming covalently closed circular DNA in the nucleus. HDV likewise enters via NTCP but depends on HBV helper function for complete virions and liver injury.
# Prognosis
Hepatitis A and acute hepatitis B in previously healthy persons usually resolve completely (HBV self-limited in ~95-99% of healthy adults; HAV essentially always), with an overall case-fatality of ~0.1% (1% among hospitalized acute hepatitis B). Hepatitis A and E cause no chronic liver disease in immunocompetent hosts. Acute hepatitis C becomes chronic in ~85-90%; among chronic HCV, cirrhosis develops in up to ~20% over 10-20 years, with an annual decompensation risk ~4% and annual HCC risk ~1-4% (about 3.36%) once cirrhotic - rates declining with effective direct-acting antiviral therapy. Chronic hepatitis B carries an enhanced HCC risk, especially with infection in infancy/childhood and high HBV DNA/HBeAg; risk of cirrhosis and HCC rises with the level of HBV replication. Waterborne hepatitis E (genotypes 1 and 2) in India/Asia has a case-fatality of 1-2%, rising to 10-20% in pregnant women. Acute HBV-HDV co-infection mortality is usually similar to HBV alone (~5% in some injection-drug-user outbreaks), but HDV superinfection of chronic HBV markedly worsens outcome (annual cirrhosis rate ~4% in chronic hepatitis D; annual HCC ~3% once cirrhotic).
# Severity classification
No formal grading system for acute viral hepatitis is given; severity is judged clinically. Features indicating severe hepatocellular disease warranting prompt hospital admission include prolonged prothrombin time, low serum albumin, hypoglycemia, and very high serum bilirubin; presenting ascites, edema, or encephalopathy indicate a poorer prognosis. Progression to fulminant hepatitis (massive hepatic necrosis) is marked by encephalopathy with rapidly shrinking liver size, rapidly rising bilirubin, and markedly prolonged PT even as aminotransferases fall.
# Clinical pearls
Five hepatotropic viruses cause almost all acute viral hepatitis: HAV, HBV, HCV, HDV (delta), and HEV. All are RNA viruses except HBV, which is a DNA virus that replicates via reverse transcription like a retrovirus.
All five types produce a clinically similar acute illness, so the type cannot be reliably distinguished by clinical or epidemiologic features alone; specific serologic testing is the accurate way to tell them apart.
Only the bloodborne agents (HBV, HCV, HDV) can become chronic and progress to cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma; HAV and HEV do not cause chronic liver disease in immunocompetent hosts.
Hepatitis A is diagnosed during acute illness by detecting IgM anti-HAV; IgM persists ~3 months (rarely 6-12). IgG anti-HAV appears in convalescence, remains detectable indefinitely, and confers lifelong immunity to reinfection.
The serologic/virologic course of acute hepatitis E mirrors hepatitis A: brief fecal shedding and viremia, an early IgM anti-HEV response dominating ~the first 3 months, then long-lasting IgG anti-HEV.
Acute hepatitis can be evaluated with five serologic tests: HBsAg, IgM anti-HAV, IgM anti-HBc, anti-HCV, and HCV RNA.
HBsAg positive plus IgM anti-HBc indicates acute (or acute reactivation) hepatitis B; HBsAg positive with IgG anti-HBc (IgM negative) indicates chronic HBV.
In about 1-5% of acute HBV infections HBsAg is below the detection threshold; in that window the presence of IgM anti-HBc establishes the diagnosis of acute hepatitis B.
The anti-HBc 'window' period (HBsAg already cleared, anti-HBs not yet detectable) is now rarely encountered because of higher-sensitivity HBsAg/anti-HBs assays.
Isolated anti-HBc usually represents remote past HBV infection (IgG class); rarely it reflects low-level viremia with HBsAg below detection, or a false-positive.
IgM anti-HBc can reappear with high titer during acute flares of chronic hepatitis B, so IgM vs IgG anti-HBc does not always reliably separate de novo acute hepatitis B from an exacerbation of chronic HBV; history and serial follow-up help.
HBeAg is a qualitative marker of active HBV replication and high infectivity; HBV DNA is the quantitative marker. HBeAg-positive HBsAg-positive mothers transmit to >90% of offspring versus only 10-15% if anti-HBe positive.
Precore/core-promoter mutant HBV cannot make HBeAg, producing HBeAg-negative chronic hepatitis with detectable HBV DNA and anti-HBe; it can run a more aggressive course and is the predominant form in the Mediterranean and Europe.
Anti-HCV (IgG) is the available antibody marker (no clinically useful IgM anti-HCV exists); with contemporary assays it is detectable early in acute hepatitis C and persists after recovery and during chronic infection.
HCV RNA is the most sensitive test and the diagnostic gold standard; it appears before aminotransferase rise and before anti-HCV. Detectable anti-HCV with negative HCV RNA signifies cleared/cured infection.
Recovery from hepatitis C leaves persistent anti-HCV but does NOT protect against reinfection; in people who inject drugs, anti-HCV plus HCV RNA may indicate reinfection rather than chronic infection.
Serum aminotransferase levels do not correlate well with the degree of liver cell damage; peak ALT/AST does not predict severity.
A low-grade fever (38-39 C) is more typical of hepatitis A and E than of B or C, except when hepatitis B presents with a serum sickness-like syndrome.
HDV is a defective virus that requires HBV (HBsAg envelope) for replication; negative HBV serology excludes hepatitis D, and the duration of HDV infection cannot outlast HBV infection.
In acute hepatitis with HBsAg and anti-HDV, the anti-HBc class clarifies the relationship: IgM anti-HBc indicates simultaneous acute HBV-HDV co-infection, whereas IgG anti-HBc indicates HDV superinfection on chronic HBV.
Among healthy adults, acute hepatitis B is self-limited in ~95-99%, whereas acute hepatitis C is self-limited in only ~15-20% (chronicity is the rule for HCV).
# Additional differentials
Autoimmune hepatitis (suggested by repeated unexplained episodes of acute hepatitis; can also be triggered after self-limited acute hepatitis A, B, or C)
Alcohol-associated hepatitis (aminotransferases usually below ~400 IU/mL with a heavy-drinking history, unlike viral hepatitis)
Drug-induced/toxin-induced liver injury, including acetaminophen, and anesthetic-agent hepatotoxicity
Acute biliary disease: acute cholecystitis, common-duct stone, or ascending cholangitis (RUQ pain, fever, jaundice overlap)
Obstructive jaundice from common-duct stone or pancreatic carcinoma (viral hepatitis in the elderly is often misdiagnosed as this)
Wilson disease and alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency (genetic/metabolic liver disorders)
Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD/MASH, formerly NAFLD/NASH)
Ischemic (hypoxic) liver injury (a leading cause of aminotransferases >=1000 IU/L, along with drug injury and acute viral hepatitis)

Cytomegalovirus hepatitis
Herpes simplex virus
Adenovirus (including the unexplained severe pediatric acute hepatitis linked to adenovirus type 41)
Coxsackievirus infection
Toxoplasmosis
SARS-CoV-2 (aminotransferase elevations in ~10% of cases and up to half of severe cases)
Leptospirosis
Brucellosis
Disseminated candidiasis
Mycobacterial infection
Pneumocystis infection
# Non infectious mimics
Passive hepatic congestion / hypoperfusion from right ventricular failure, shock, severe hypotension, or severe left ventricular failure
Budd-Chiari syndrome (hepatic vein occlusion) and hepatic veno-occlusive disease
Constrictive pericarditis or right atrial myxoma impeding venous return
Acute fatty liver of pregnancy
Intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy
Eclampsia
HELLP syndrome (hemolysis, elevated liver tests, low platelets)
Liver metastases mimicking acute or even fulminant hepatitis (rare)
# Additional investigations
Anti-HCV antibody plus HCV RNA (HCV RNA is the gold standard; needed when anti-HCV is undetectable or in immunosuppressed patients where antibody may be absent)
Anti-HDV (IgM/IgG) and/or HDV RNA in HBsAg-positive patients who are at high risk or have severe/fulminant disease
IgM anti-HEV / HEV RNA when standard A/B/C tests are negative and enteric risk factors are present (specialized labs/CDC)
HBeAg and anti-HBe to assess relative infectivity and replication in chronic HBV
Quantitative HBV DNA (PCR) to gauge replication level and risk of cirrhosis, decompensation, and HCC in chronic HBV
Serum albumin (a fall is uncommon in uncomplicated acute hepatitis; signals severe disease)
Blood glucose (hypoglycemia can occur in severe hepatitis)
Complete blood count / differential (transient neutropenia and lymphopenia, then relative lymphocytosis with 2-20% atypical lymphocytes)
Serum bilirubin with conjugated/unconjugated fractions (levels >20 mg/dL persisting late suggest severe disease)
Liver biopsy only when the diagnosis is in doubt or chronic/autoimmune hepatitis is suspected; it is rarely needed in acute viral hepatitis
# Red flags
Hepatic encephalopathy (confusion, disorientation, somnolence, progression to coma) - hallmark of fulminant hepatitis with very high mortality (>80% in deep coma without transplant)
Markedly prolonged prothrombin time / INR (reflects severe synthetic failure and extensive hepatocellular necrosis)
Rapidly shrinking liver size with rapidly rising bilirubin even as aminotransferases fall - indicates massive hepatic necrosis
Ascites and peripheral edema at presentation (poorer prognosis)
Hypoglycemia and falling serum albumin in acute hepatitis (severe hepatocellular disease)
Very high or persistently rising serum bilirubin (>20 mg/dL late in the course)
Pregnancy with acute hepatitis E - up to 10-20% risk of fulminant hepatitis and death
HDV superinfection of chronic hepatitis B - clinical deterioration with substantially increased risk of fulminant hepatitis and death (mortality >20% in 'Labrea fever' outbreaks)
Advanced age or serious underlying disease, especially pre-existing chronic liver disease (raises risk of severe and fulminant course)
Acute hepatitis E or other insult superimposed on chronic liver disease (acute-on-chronic liver failure)
# Pitfalls
Relying on HBsAg alone can miss acute hepatitis B: in ~1-5% of cases HBsAg is below detection and only IgM anti-HBc reveals the diagnosis.
IgM anti-HBc can re-emerge during an acute exacerbation of chronic HBV, so it does not always distinguish true acute hepatitis B from a chronic flare.
False-positive IgM anti-HAV occurs in rheumatologic disease (rheumatoid factor) and rarely in EBV infection; false-positive IgM anti-HBc occurs with high-titer rheumatoid factor.
Anti-HCV immunoassays can be falsely positive (low pretest probability, rheumatoid factor); confirm with HCV RNA.
In immunosuppressed patients anti-HCV may be undetectable, so HCV RNA testing is required to diagnose hepatitis C.
HBsAg titer does not reflect disease severity - an inverse correlation exists, with very low HBsAg in fulminant hepatitis.
Coexisting HBsAg and anti-HBs in chronic HBV does not signal imminent HBsAg clearance; these patients still have chronic HBV infection.
A normal or only mildly elevated alkaline phosphatase and normal albumin are expected in uncomplicated acute hepatitis; abnormal values suggest an alternative or complicated course.
The serum sickness-like prodrome of acute hepatitis B (arthralgia, rash, angioedema) is often misdiagnosed as a rheumatologic disease before jaundice appears.
Retrospective diagnosis of self-limited simultaneous acute HBV-HDV co-infection is difficult because anti-HDV is often transient and disappears once HBsAg clears.
Patients with acute viral hepatitis tolerate surgery poorly; biliary disease should be excluded clinically and with imaging before any operation.
# Deescalation
Not applicable to antibiotics. If coagulopathy, encephalopathy, or worsening liver function develops, escalate urgently to a liver unit/transplant center for evaluation of acute liver failure rather than considering any antimicrobial intervention.
# Framework
Viral hepatitis does not respond to antibiotics — they provide no benefit and add unnecessary hepatotoxic drug burden in a patient with impaired hepatic metabolism.
Avoid unnecessary hepatotoxic medications (including some antibiotics, antiemetics in excess, and definitely alcohol) during the acute illness, since impaired hepatic clearance changes drug handling broadly.
Specifically counsel and monitor pregnant patients with suspected Hepatitis E given the disproportionately high risk of fulminant hepatic failure in this group.
Antibiotics NOT indicated
