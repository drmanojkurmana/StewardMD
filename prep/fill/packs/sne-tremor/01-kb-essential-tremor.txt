# Essential tremor
Essential tremor, Neurology, Neurology.
Aliases: ET; benign essential tremor; familial tremor.
# Pathophysiology
Essential tremor is a common movement disorder thought to arise from abnormal oscillatory activity within cerebello-thalamo-cortical circuits, with the inferior olive and cerebellum implicated as key nodes of a central oscillator. It is often familial with autosomal dominant inheritance and variable penetrance, though the precise genetic and neurodegenerative substrate remains incompletely defined, and some evidence points to cerebellar pathology.
# Prognosis
Essential tremor is typically slowly progressive over years. Although often labelled benign, it can become functionally and socially disabling, impairing writing, eating, and drinking; many patients gain useful relief from medication or, in refractory cases, from surgical neuromodulation.
# Clinical pearls
The hallmark is a bilateral, largely symmetric postural and kinetic (action) tremor of the upper limbs, in contrast to the resting tremor of Parkinson disease
The head, voice, and jaw are often involved, and a titubating head tremor or quavering voice can be prominent
The tremor characteristically worsens with sustained posture and goal-directed movement and is absent at complete rest
A family history is common, and inheritance frequently appears autosomal dominant
Many patients report transient improvement of tremor after alcohol, a useful diagnostic clue
First-line pharmacological principles include a non-selective beta-blocker such as propranolol or the anticonvulsant primidone
Deep brain stimulation of the ventral intermediate thalamic nucleus can help severe, medication-refractory cases
Onset spans a wide age range, and prevalence and severity tend to increase with age
# Additional differentials
Parkinson disease (resting tremor, bradykinesia, rigidity)
Enhanced physiological tremor (e.g. anxiety, caffeine, thyrotoxicosis, drugs)
Dystonic tremor
Cerebellar (intention) tremor
Drug-induced or withdrawal tremor
Wilson disease in younger patients
# Additional investigations
Clinical assessment to distinguish action tremor from resting and cerebellar tremor
Thyroid function tests to exclude thyrotoxicosis as a cause of enhanced tremor
Serum copper and ceruloplasmin in younger patients to exclude Wilson disease
Review of medications and stimulant intake that may produce or amplify tremor
DaTscan or specialist assessment when distinguishing from Parkinson disease is difficult
# Red flags
Strictly unilateral tremor, a resting component, or associated bradykinesia and rigidity suggesting Parkinson disease
Abrupt onset or rapid progression suggesting a structural, toxic, or metabolic cause
Young age of onset with Kayser-Fleischer rings or hepatic dysfunction suggesting Wilson disease
Accompanying cerebellar signs, ataxia, or other focal neurological deficits
# Pitfalls
Confusing essential tremor with early Parkinson disease, which delays appropriate management
Overlooking a reversible cause such as thyrotoxicosis, medication, or excess caffeine
Missing Wilson disease in a young patient with tremor
Assuming the disorder is trivial and underestimating its impact on daily function and quality of life
# Management
First-line pharmacotherapy with a non-selective beta-blocker (propranolol) or the anticonvulsant primidone
Second-line agents such as topiramate, gabapentin or a benzodiazepine where response is inadequate
Review and minimise exacerbating factors, including tremorogenic drugs, stimulants, caffeine and anxiety
Occupational therapy with weighted utensils and adaptive aids to maintain function in writing, eating and drinking
Botulinum toxin injection for disabling head or voice tremor
For refractory disabling tremor, refer for surgical neuromodulation with deep brain stimulation of the ventral intermediate thalamic nucleus or MRI-guided focused ultrasound thalamotomy
