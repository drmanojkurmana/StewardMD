# Extrahepatic portal vein obstruction / cavernoma
Extrahepatic portal vein obstruction / cavernoma, Gastroenterology / Hepatology, Hepatology.
Aliases: EHPVO; portal vein thrombosis with cavernoma; portal cavernoma; extrahepatic portal venous obstruction.
# Pathophysiology
Extrahepatic portal vein obstruction results from thrombosis of the portal vein, often triggered by local factors such as neonatal umbilical infection, abdominal sepsis or surgery, together with systemic prothrombotic conditions. Following occlusion, a bypassing network of hepatopetal collateral veins develops around the obstructed segment, forming the portal cavernoma. Because the liver parenchyma is not primarily diseased, hepatocellular function is preserved, but presinusoidal portal hypertension drives varices, splenomegaly and, over time, compression of the bile ducts by the collateral network causing portal biliopathy.
# Prognosis
With preserved liver function and effective control of variceal bleeding, long-term survival is generally good, though portal biliopathy and recurrent bleeding can cause significant morbidity.
# Clinical pearls
A major cause of non-cirrhotic portal hypertension, especially in children and young adults, in whom it often presents with well-tolerated variceal bleeding
The portal vein is replaced by a network of collateral veins (the portal cavernoma) that develops after chronic occlusion
Synthetic liver function is characteristically preserved because the hepatic parenchyma itself is intact
Splenomegaly and hypersplenism are common, and growth retardation may occur in affected children
Neonatal umbilical sepsis or catheterisation, intra-abdominal infection, and inherited or acquired prothrombotic states are important precipitants
Long-standing cavernoma can compress the biliary tree, producing portal biliopathy with cholestasis and stones
Management centres on prevention and treatment of variceal bleeding, with anticoagulation and shunt or meso-Rex bypass considered in selected patients
# Additional differentials
Cirrhotic portal hypertension
Idiopathic non-cirrhotic portal hypertension / porto-sinusoidal vascular disease

Nodular regenerative hyperplasia
Schistosomal portal fibrosis
Congenital hepatic fibrosis
# Additional investigations
Doppler ultrasound demonstrating portal vein occlusion and cavernomatous collaterals
Contrast-enhanced CT or MR portography to map the venous anatomy and biliopathy
Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy to assess varices
Thrombophilia and myeloproliferative-disorder screening
Liver function and synthetic tests, typically preserved
MR cholangiography where portal biliopathy is suspected
# Red flags
Acute variceal haemorrhage with haemodynamic compromise
Cholangitis or obstructive jaundice from portal biliopathy
Extension of thrombus into the mesenteric veins with bowel ischaemia
Features of an underlying myeloproliferative or prothrombotic disorder requiring urgent evaluation
# Pitfalls
Mislabelling the portal hypertension as cirrhotic and misjudging prognosis
Overlooking an underlying prothrombotic or myeloproliferative disorder
Missing portal biliopathy as a cause of cholestasis or recurrent cholangitis
Failing to recognise mesenteric extension that threatens bowel viability
# Management
Prevent and control variceal bleeding with endoscopic band ligation and non-selective beta-blockers
Acute variceal haemorrhage: resuscitation, vasoactive drugs (terlipressin or octreotide), endoscopic haemostasis and prophylactic antibiotics
Anticoagulation in selected patients with an underlying prothrombotic state, generally after variceal eradication
Investigate for and treat an underlying inherited or acquired prothrombotic or myeloproliferative disorder
Manage portal biliopathy with endoscopic biliary drainage or stenting for symptomatic cholestasis or stones
Consider meso-Rex bypass or a portosystemic shunt in children or in refractory bleeding
Surveillance endoscopy, monitoring of hypersplenism and growth in children, under hepatology follow-up
