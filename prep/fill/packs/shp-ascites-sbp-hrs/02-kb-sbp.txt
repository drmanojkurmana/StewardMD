# Spontaneous Bacterial Peritonitis
Spontaneous Bacterial Peritonitis, Hepatobiliary / GI.
# Pathogens
SBP is monomicrobial in the great majority of cases and reflects bacterial translocation from the gut, dominated by gram-negative Enterobacteriaceae, with gram-positive cocci accounting for most of the remainder.
Very likely: Escherichia coli; Klebsiella pneumoniae.
Likely: Streptococcus species (including Streptococcus pneumoniae).
# Possible

# Pathophysiology
SBP is spontaneous infection of ascitic fluid in the absence of an intraabdominal surgical source. The presumed mechanism is bacterial translocation: gut flora cross an edematous, leaky intestinal wall into mesenteric lymph nodes, producing bacteremia that seeds the ascites, which is a good growth medium. Susceptibility in cirrhosis reflects impaired hepatic filtration from a diseased liver and altered portal circulation, low ascitic complement levels (lower than in non-cirrhotic ascites), and diminished opsonic and phagocytic function of PMNs in advanced liver disease. Cirrhosis is also associated with gut dysbiosis (increased Enterobacteriaceae), small-intestinal bacterial overgrowth, reduced Paneth-cell defensins, decreased intestinal motility, reduced pancreatobiliary secretions, and portal-hypertensive enteropathy, all of which promote pathologic translocation. SBP occurs in up to ~30% of hospitalized cirrhotics with ascites but is an uncommon event overall, affecting 10% or fewer of cirrhotic patients.
# Prognosis
SBP is a common and potentially lethal complication of cirrhotic ascites and carries significant morbidity and mortality, largely because it tends to occur in patients with the most advanced liver disease. In-hospital mortality is roughly 17-25%; one tertiary-center series reported ~23% mortality at 30 days and ~37% at 90 days. Recurrence is high, with up to ~70% of patients having another episode within a year. Patients with PBP usually respond to appropriate antibiotics within about 72 hours. The underlying prognosis of cirrhosis with ascites is itself poor, with under 50% surviving 2 years after ascites onset in some series, so SBP should prompt consideration of liver transplantation.
# Severity classification
Risk stratification is framed around predictors of poor outcome and renal injury: higher SBP risk in patients with low ascitic-fluid total protein (<1.0-1.5 g/dL), impaired renal function (creatinine >=1.2 mg/dL, BUN >=25 mg/dL, or serum sodium <=130 mEq/L), and advanced liver failure (Child-Turcotte-Pugh score >=9, bilirubin >=3 mg/dL); these are used to identify candidates for prophylaxis. The greatest benefit of adjunctive albumin is seen in those presenting with serum creatinine >=1 mg/dL, BUN >=30 mg/dL, or total bilirubin >=5 mg/dL.
# Clinical pearls
An ascitic-fluid PMN (neutrophil) count of at least 250/microL is diagnostic of SBP, irrespective of whether cultures grow an organism.
Fever is the single most common manifestation, present in roughly 80% of cases; abdominal pain, acute onset, and peritoneal signs aid diagnosis but their absence does not rule SBP out.
Abdominal tenderness is found in only about 40% of patients and rebound tenderness is uncommon, so a benign abdomen does not exclude the diagnosis.
SBP can present with no abdominal symptoms at all, instead manifesting as new or worsening hepatic encephalopathy, altered mental status, jaundice, or acute kidney injury without another explanation.
In any cirrhotic patient hospitalized with ascites who deteriorates or shows signs of infection, a diagnostic paracentesis is mandatory; tapping within 12 hours of admission lowers mortality through early SBP detection.
Ascitic fluid should be injected directly into blood-culture bottles at the bedside to improve yield, and blood cultures drawn simultaneously, since the organism burden in the fluid is low and bacteremia frequently coexists.
Culture samples should be obtained before antibiotics are started to maximize recovery of the organism.
SBP is almost always monomicrobial; a single isolate is the rule.
Gram stain of ascitic fluid is frequently negative in SBP and a negative result should not deter treatment.
SBP virtually always arises on a background of preexisting ascites, which predates the infection.
A repeat paracentesis after 48 hours of therapy should confirm at least a 25% fall in the ascitic PMN count from baseline; failure to fall, or persistent ascitic leukocytosis after treatment, should prompt a search for an alternative or secondary diagnosis.
# Additional differentials
Secondary bacterial peritonitis from a perforated or inflamed viscus or intraabdominal abscess
Hepatic encephalopathy from other precipitants (GI bleed, volume depletion, hyponatremia, constipation) when SBP presents as altered mentation
Acute kidney injury / hepatorenal syndrome from non-infectious causes when SBP presents as renal deterioration
# Infection mimics
Secondary bacterial peritonitis (perforated viscus, ruptured appendix/diverticulum/ulcer, intraabdominal abscess)
Spontaneous bacterial empyema complicating hepatic hydrothorax
Tuberculous peritonitis (ascitic lymphocytosis; can complicate or mimic infected ascites)
# Non infectious mimics
Peritoneal carcinomatosis / malignant ascites (low-SAAG ascites; SBP is rare in malignant ascites)
Pancreatic ascites (high ascitic amylase, typically >1000)
Worsening uncomplicated tense ascites with increased girth but no infection
# Additional investigations
Ascitic fluid PMN/neutrophil count (>=250/microL is diagnostic)
Ascitic fluid inoculated into blood-culture bottles at the bedside
Gram stain of ascitic fluid (often negative; does not exclude SBP)
Serum-ascites albumin gradient (SAAG) and ascitic total protein to characterize the underlying ascites and assess risk
Ascitic glucose and LDH when secondary peritonitis is suspected
Contrast-enhanced abdominal CT to seek an intraabdominal source / exclude secondary peritonitis
Chest and abdominal radiography to exclude free air (perforation) when abdominal pain is present
Repeat paracentesis at 48 hours to confirm >=25% fall in ascitic PMN count
# Red flags
New or worsening hepatic encephalopathy or altered mental status in a cirrhotic with ascites
Unexplained acute kidney injury, jaundice, or nonlocalizing malaise/fatigue in a susceptible patient
Multiple organisms recovered from ascitic fluid, especially with anaerobes, suggesting secondary peritonitis requiring source control
Free air under the diaphragm on imaging, signaling perforation
Concurrent GI/variceal bleeding, which markedly increases SBP frequency
Failure of the ascitic PMN count to fall, or persistence of WBCs after therapy, indicating treatment failure or an alternative diagnosis
# Pitfalls
Diagnosis rests on the ascitic PMN count >=250/microL, not on a positive culture; ascitic cultures are often negative because the organism burden is low.
Recovery of more than one organism (a mixed flora, especially with anaerobes) alongside an elevated PMN count points to SECONDARY peritonitis from a ruptured viscus or abscess, not SBP, and demands a search for a surgical source.
Multiple organisms WITHOUT an elevated PMN count suggest bowel perforation by the paracentesis needle rather than true infection.
Failing to perform a diagnostic paracentesis in a decompensated cirrhotic who deteriorates (encephalopathy, AKI, sepsis) misses SBP, since the abdomen may be benign and tenderness is present in only ~40%.
A low ascitic total protein (<1.0-1.5 g/dL) reflects low opsonic activity and identifies patients at higher risk for SBP; this group warrants heightened suspicion.
The 250/microL PMN threshold is specific to SBP and should not be applied to secondary peritonitis or to peritoneal-dialysis peritonitis, where lower leukocyte cutoffs are used.
Gastric acid suppression (e.g., proton-pump inhibitors) has been associated with increased SBP risk in observational data.
# Deescalation
If ascitic fluid culture identifies a specific organism with known susceptibility, narrow to the most targeted single agent; reassess with repeat paracentesis at 48 hours if response is inadequate to exclude secondary peritonitis or a resistant organism.

# Framework
Diagnosis requires ascitic fluid analysis (neutrophil count ≥250 cells/mm³ supports the diagnosis) — avoid treating presumptively without paracentesis unless the patient is too unstable to tolerate delay.
IV albumin should be given alongside antibiotics in patients with significant renal impairment or bilirubin elevation, as this reduces mortality and renal complications — a key adjunct, not optional.
Secondary prophylaxis (e.g., norfloxacin) should be planned at discharge in survivors of an SBP episode, but this is a separate long-term decision from acute empiric treatment.
