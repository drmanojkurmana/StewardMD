# Hepatorenal syndrome
Hepatorenal syndrome, Hepatology.
Aliases: HRS; type 1 hepatorenal syndrome; type 2 hepatorenal syndrome; functional renal failure of cirrhosis.
# Pathophysiology
Advanced liver disease with portal hypertension causes marked splanchnic arterial vasodilation, which reduces effective circulating arterial volume despite total-body volume overload. This triggers compensatory activation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, sympathetic nervous system, and vasopressin release, producing intense renal vasoconstriction and a progressive fall in glomerular filtration despite normal renal histology. Systemic inflammation and bacterial translocation from the gut further worsen splanchnic vasodilation and renal hypoperfusion, and superimposed infection frequently precipitates the acute form.
# Prognosis
Historically associated with very high short-term mortality, particularly the rapidly progressive form, unless renal function improves with therapy or the patient undergoes timely liver transplantation. Even with treatment response, underlying hepatic decompensation continues to drive long-term outcomes.
# Clinical pearls
A diagnosis of exclusion: renal failure in advanced cirrhosis/portal hypertension (with or without ascites) that fails to improve after volume expansion and stopping diuretics
Kidneys are structurally normal — HRS is a functional, potentially reversible form of renal hypoperfusion driven by splanchnic vasodilation and effective arterial underfilling
Newer nomenclature classifies HRS as HRS-AKI (rapidly progressive, formerly type 1) versus HRS-NAKI (slower, chronic course, formerly type 2)
Urinalysis and urine sediment are typically bland, with minimal proteinuria and few cells or casts
Urine sodium is usually very low, reflecting intense renal sodium avidity from neurohormonal activation
A common precipitant is spontaneous bacterial peritonitis or other infection, so sepsis must always be actively sought and excluded
Trial of volume expansion (typically with albumin) for 48 hours with cessation of diuretics/nephrotoxins is required before the diagnosis can be confirmed
Definitive treatment is liver transplantation; medical therapy is a bridge, not a cure
# Additional differentials
Prerenal azotemia from volume depletion (vomiting, diarrhea, over-diuresis, GI bleeding)
Acute tubular necrosis from ischemia, sepsis, or nephrotoxin exposure
Drug-induced acute kidney injury (NSAIDs, aminoglycosides, contrast, ACE inhibitors/ARBs)
Glomerulonephritis associated with chronic liver disease (e.g., IgA nephropathy, membranoproliferative pattern in hepatitis C)
Abdominal compartment syndrome from tense ascites
Obstructive uropathy
# Infection mimics
Sepsis-associated acute kidney injury
Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis with secondary renal hypoperfusion
Urinary tract infection with associated prerenal physiology
# Non infectious mimics
Acute tubular necrosis
Prerenal azotemia from diuretic overuse or GI losses
Contrast-associated or drug-induced nephropathy
Cardiorenal syndrome from cirrhotic cardiomyopathy or coexistent heart failure
Abdominal compartment syndrome
# Additional investigations
Serum creatinine trend and estimated GFR — establishes rate and severity of renal decline defining HRS-AKI vs HRS-NAKI
Urinalysis with microscopy — bland sediment supports HRS over intrinsic renal disease
Urine sodium and fractional excretion of sodium — typically very low, reflecting avid sodium retention
Renal ultrasound — excludes obstruction and assesses for underlying structural kidney disease
Diagnostic paracentesis with ascitic fluid cell count and culture — excludes spontaneous bacterial peritonitis as a precipitant
Blood and urine cultures — identify occult infection precipitating renal decline
