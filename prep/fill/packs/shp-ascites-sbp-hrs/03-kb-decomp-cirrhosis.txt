# Pathophysiology
Decompensation is driven by portal hypertension plus renal sodium and water retention. Portal hypertension (elevation of the hepatic venous pressure gradient >5 mmHg) arises from two simultaneous processes: increased intrahepatic resistance from fibrosis disrupting sinusoidal architecture, stellate-cell-mediated smooth-muscle contraction, regenerative nodules, microthrombi, and reduced intrahepatic endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) causing intrahepatic vasoconstriction; and increased splanchnic blood flow from systemic vasodilation. Systemically, raised nitric oxide, VEGF and TNF cause splanchnic arterial vasodilation, pooling blood and reducing effective circulating volume; the kidneys sense this as hypovolemia and activate ADH, the sympathetic nervous system and the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, producing free-water and sodium retention (ascites/edema). Encephalopathy results from gut-derived neurotoxins bypassing the liver via portosystemic shunting and reduced hepatic mass.
# Prognosis
Decompensation defines a poor-prognosis state: with clinically significant portal hypertension that decompensates, median survival is under 2 years, and fewer than 50% of patients survive 2 years after the onset of ascites. Encephalopathy in chronic liver disease is a poor prognostic sign. HRS (both AKI and CKD types) carries a poor prognosis unless liver transplantation is achieved. ACLF 28-day mortality ranges from 20-70%, rising with the number of organ failures. Severity and transplant priority are graded by Child-Pugh class and the MELD/MELD-Na (MELD 3.0) systems.
# Severity classification
Child-Pugh score (5 factors: bilirubin, albumin, prothrombin time/INR, ascites, encephalopathy; range 5-15) classifies cirrhosis as class A (5-6, compensated), B (7-9), or C (10-15); a score ≥7 (class B) defines decompensation. MELD score uses INR, bilirubin, and creatinine to predict short-term mortality and allocate organs; it was extended with serum sodium and reweighted as MELD-Na / MELD 3.0 for transplant listing. PELD is the pediatric equivalent. Clinically significant portal hypertension is defined by HVPG ≥10 mmHg.
# Clinical pearls
Compensated cirrhosis can be asymptomatic for a decade or more; the transition to decompensation is marked by the first major event — ascites/edema, variceal hemorrhage, hepatic encephalopathy, or jaundice.
Once clinically significant portal hypertension (HVPG ≥5 mmHg, with substantial decompensation risk at ≥10 mmHg) progresses to overt decompensation, median survival is under 2 years.
Encephalopathy in chronic liver disease is almost always triggered by an identifiable precipitant (GI bleeding, infection, volume depletion/overdiuresis, hyponatremia, constipation, uremia, sedatives/narcotics) rather than spontaneous progression.
Ammonia levels correlate poorly with the severity of encephalopathy or liver disease; the diagnosis of hepatic encephalopathy is clinical, and most hepatologists do not rely on ammonia to make the diagnosis or follow progress.
A new or worsening encephalopathy episode mandates a search for occult infection — if ascites is present it should be tapped to exclude SBP.
A hyperdynamic circulation (widened pulse pressure, increased cardiac output, reduced peripheral resistance from splanchnic vasodilation) is a hallmark of advanced cirrhosis.
Acute-on-chronic liver failure (ACLF) is acutely decompensating cirrhosis with one or more extrahepatic organ failures, behaves like a sepsis syndrome, and carries 28-day mortality of 20-70% rising with the number of failing organs; it is most often precipitated by bacterial/fungal infection or alcoholic hepatitis.
# Additional differentials
Cardiac ascites / constrictive pericarditis (high-SAAG ascites; pro-BNP elevated, ascitic protein typically ≥2.5 g/dL)
Budd-Chiari syndrome (hepatic vein thrombosis) and sinusoidal obstruction syndrome (high-SAAG ascites)
Massive hepatic metastases (high-SAAG ascites)
Peritoneal carcinomatosis (low-SAAG, protein-rich ascites)
Tuberculous peritonitis (low-SAAG, lymphocyte-rich ascites; raised ascitic adenosine deaminase)
Pancreatic ascites (low-SAAG, ascitic amylase typically >1000)
Nephrotic syndrome / protein-losing enteropathy / malnutrition (low-SAAG ascites from hypoalbuminemia)
# Infection mimics
Secondary bacterial peritonitis from a perforated viscus or abscess — distinguished from SBP by ascitic glucose <50 mg/dL, ascitic LDH above serum LDH, and multiple organisms on culture with a raised PMN count
Tuberculous peritonitis presenting with new ascites
Systemic sepsis producing leukocytosis and altered mental status that can mimic or precipitate decompensation
# Non infectious mimics
Other causes of coma/altered mentation to exclude before attributing to encephalopathy: electrolyte imbalance, sedative use, renal or respiratory failure
Hepatopulmonary syndrome causing dyspnea/hypoxemia (platypnea, orthodeoxia) mimicking primary cardiopulmonary disease
Portopulmonary hypertension causing exertional dyspnea and fatigue
# Additional investigations
Diagnostic paracentesis with serum-ascites albumin gradient (SAAG): ≥1.1 g/dL indicates portal-hypertensive ascites, <1.1 g/dL indicates non-portal-hypertensive cause
Ascitic fluid cell count/differential, total protein, Gram stain and culture inoculated into blood-culture bottles at the bedside
In high-SAAG ascites, ascitic protein <2.5 g/dL suggests cirrhosis vs ≥2.5 g/dL suggesting cardiac/early Budd-Chiari sinusoidal sources
Serum pro-BNP to identify heart failure as the cause of high-SAAG ascites
Upper endoscopy to screen for and grade esophageal/gastric varices once cirrhosis is established
Liver ultrasound (and contrast CT/MRI showing nodular liver and collateral circulation) for portal hypertension and HCC surveillance
Transient elastography (liver stiffness) to stage fibrosis and predict variceal hemorrhage, ascites, and liver-related death
Hepatic venous pressure gradient measurement in selected cases to confirm sinusoidal portal hypertension
Pulse oximetry as a screen for hepatopulmonary syndrome
Serial serum creatinine and exclusion of volume depletion/intrinsic renal disease when HRS is suspected
# Red flags
Variceal hemorrhage carries a 20-30% mortality per bleeding episode and is an immediate life-threatening event
A stepwise progressive rise in creatinine with large-volume ascites suggests hepatorenal syndrome (HRS-AKI), a poor-prognosis functional renal failure
ACLF with four or more organ failures persisting after 3-7 days of ICU support, when transplant is not an option, signals a transition toward palliative care
Avoid NSAIDs, aminoglycosides, and use ACE inhibitors/ARBs cautiously — they precipitate renal failure in cirrhotic ascites
New-onset or worsening encephalopathy, jaundice, or tense ascites in known cirrhosis should prompt an urgent search for a precipitant
# Pitfalls
Relying on ammonia levels to diagnose or track hepatic encephalopathy — they correlate poorly with disease severity
Attributing altered mental status to encephalopathy without excluding electrolyte disturbance, sedatives, and renal/respiratory failure
Failing to tap the ascites in a decompensated cirrhotic with new symptoms — abdominal tenderness is present in only ~40% of SBP and rebound is uncommon, so SBP can be clinically silent
Multiple organisms on ascitic culture with a raised PMN count indicate secondary (not spontaneous) peritonitis and a surgical source; multiple organisms without raised PMN suggest bowel perforation by the paracentesis needle
Withholding paracentesis for fear of coagulopathy — paracentesis is safe even in coagulopathic patients
Restricting dietary protein for encephalopathy — its harm to nutrition outweighs benefit and it is strongly discouraged

# Steps
Identify and treat the PRECIPITANT — always do a diagnostic ascitic tap to exclude SBP (treat with IV cefotaxime + albumin if PMN ≥250).
Ascites: salt restriction + spironolactone ± furosemide; large-volume paracentesis with albumin for tense ascites.
Encephalopathy: lactulose ± rifaximin; treat triggers (bleed, infection, constipation, electrolytes).
Variceal bleed: resuscitation, terlipressin, prophylactic antibiotics, urgent endoscopic band ligation.
Avoid nephrotoxins/NSAIDs; nutrition; assess for transplant; manage AKI/HRS.
Acute complications — SBP and variceal bleeding
Treat precipitant; SBP → cefotaxime + albumin; bleed → terlipressin + antibiotics
