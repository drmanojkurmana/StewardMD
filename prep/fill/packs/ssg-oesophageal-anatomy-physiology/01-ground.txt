The oesophagus is a muscular tube in three portions: a short cervical part behind the trachea, a long thoracic part hugging the aorta and pericardium, and a stubby abdominal part below the diaphragm. Its blood arrives segmentally, from inferior thyroid branches above, bronchial and direct aortic twigs in the chest, and the left gastric and inferior phrenic vessels below. A surgeon freeing the gullet must respect these segmental vessels, because stripping them devascularises the wall, and must identify the two vagal trunks: the anterior vagus, derived from the left vagus, running on the front of the oesophagus, and the posterior vagus, derived from the right vagus, lying behind it near the hiatus.

The gate at the bottom is the lower oesophageal sphincter, a 3 to 4 cm sleeve of tonically contracted smooth muscle reinforced outside by the crural diaphragm pinching the hiatus. Resting pressure in this zone normally sits well above gastric baseline; a resting pressure below 10 mmHg marks a feeble barrier, and gastric contents wash upward producing heartburn and regurgitation.

When dysphagia dominates, high-resolution manometry maps every swallow. The Chicago Classification version 4 reads these tracings, sorting failed peristalsis with poor sphincter relaxation into achalasia types I, II and III, and labelling isolated outflow resistance as oesophagogastric junction outflow obstruction. The key number on the trace is the integrated relaxation pressure, the mean lowest pressure across the sphincter in the seconds after swallowing; an elevated integrated relaxation pressure proves the sphincter is not opening.

Reflux needs proof rather than symptoms. Ambulatory 24-hour pH-impedance monitoring carries a catheter for a full day and night, counting acid exposure time and reflux episodes against meals and posture. A DeMeester score above 14.7, or an acid exposure time above 6 percent of the monitored period, confirms pathological reflux under Lyon Consensus 2.0 rules, separating true disease from functional heartburn.

The timed barium swallow watches emptying in real time. In achalasia the column hangs up and tapers to a smooth bird-beak point at the closed sphincter; in distal oesophageal spasm the lumen writhes into a corkscrew or rosary-bead pattern. Films taken at 1 and 5 minutes quantify clearance: a retained barium column at 5 minutes marks failed emptying and tracks response to myotomy or dilation.

Cancer staging leans on endoscopic ultrasound, which resolves the five wall layers and assigns the T category by depth of invasion, then samples celiac nodes by fine-needle aspiration to detect distant nodal spread. A shallow lesion stays endoscopic, a locally advanced one goes for neoadjuvant therapy first, and a resectable deeper tumour proceeds to oesophagectomy.

A sudden tear declares itself dramatically. Forceful vomiting against a closed glottis can burst the distal gullet, and the Mackler triad of vomiting, chest pain and crackling subcutaneous emphysema follows, with a Hamman crunch audible over the mediastinum as the heart beats against escaped air. The first confirmatory test is a water-soluble contrast swallow, chosen over barium because free barium in the mediastinum provokes fibrosis.

The vagal trunks also matter in ulcer surgery history. Truncal vagotomy cuts both the anterior and posterior trunks at the diaphragmatic hiatus to abolish acid drive, but denervation paralyses the antral pump, so every truncal vagotomy must be paired with a drainage procedure: a pyloroplasty that opens and widens the outlet, or a gastrojejunostomy that bypasses it.
