# Gout and hyperuricemia (grounding reference: Kelley & Firestein, Harrison, ACR/EULAR criteria)

Rheumatology topic srh-gout: overview of pathophysiology, diagnostic criteria, clinical features, and management guidelines.

Hyperuricemia exceeds the physiological solubility limit of monosodium urate of 6.8 mg/dL (404 micromol/L) at 37 degrees Celsius; renal underexcretion via URAT1 and GLUT9 accounts for >90% of primary cases.

Acute gout presents with rapid-onset, excruciating inflammatory monoarthritis peaking within 12 to 24 hours, involving the first metatarsophalangeal joint (podagra) in over 50% of initial attacks.

Synovial fluid analysis under compensated polarized light microscopy demonstrates intracellular, needle-shaped monosodium urate crystals displaying strong negative birefringence (yellow parallel to the compensator axis).

First-line therapy for acute gout flares includes low-dose colchicine (1.2 mg immediately followed by 0.6 mg 1 hour later), oral NSAIDs, or systemic glucocorticoids (prednisone 30 to 35 mg daily for 5 days).

Urate-lowering therapy (ULT) is indicated for patients with >=1 subcutaneous tophi, radiographic damage from gout, or frequent flares (>=2 per year), initiated during an acute flare alongside anti-inflammatory prophylaxis.

Allopurinol is the preferred first-line xanthine oxidase inhibitor for all patients, starting at <=100 mg daily (lower in CKD) and titrated every 2 to 5 weeks to achieve a serum urate target strictly <6.0 mg/dL (<5.0 mg/dL in severe tophaceous gout).

Screening for HLA-B*5801 is strongly recommended before starting allopurinol in patients of Southeast Asian and African descent due to a high risk of allopurinol severe cutaneous adverse reactions (SCAR, SJS/TEN).

Anti-inflammatory flare prophylaxis with low-dose colchicine (0.5 to 0.6 mg daily) or low-dose NSAIDs is mandatory when initiating urate-lowering therapy, continued for at least 3 to 6 months after achieving target urate.
